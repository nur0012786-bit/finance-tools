Debt-to-Income Ratio (DTI) Explained

Learn what debt-to-income ratio means, how to calculate it, why lenders use it, and why definitions can vary by loan type.

Educational finance guidance

Debt-to-income ratio compares qualifying monthly debt obligations with qualifying gross monthly income. Lenders may use different definitions and thresholds.

The basic DTI formula

A simplified DTI calculation is qualifying monthly debt divided by gross monthly income, multiplied by 100 to express a percentage.

Illustrative calculation

If qualifying monthly debt were $1,800 and gross monthly income were $6,000, the simplified DTI would be 30%. This example is for arithmetic illustration only.

Why DTI matters

Lenders may use DTI as one part of an underwriting review. They can also consider credit history, income stability, assets, collateral, loan type and other criteria.

Different calculations can produce different results

The debts included, income definition and treatment of proposed payments can vary by lender and loan program. Do not assume one DTI percentage applies universally.

DTI is not the same as affordability

A lender's qualifying calculation is not a substitute for a personal budget. A payment that meets an underwriting rule may still be uncomfortable for a household.

Frequently asked questions

Will a calculator tell me what I should borrow or spend?

No. Calculators are educational tools that apply the assumptions entered by the user. Actual borrowing terms, eligibility and personal affordability require additional information.

Are loan and credit rules the same for every lender?

No. Lenders can have different product terms, underwriting criteria, fees and disclosures. The lender's agreement and disclosures control an actual transaction.

Where should I verify the terms of an actual account?

Review the lender or card issuer's official disclosures, statements and agreement, and ask the provider about any item you do not understand.
