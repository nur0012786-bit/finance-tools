Debt-to-Income Ratio (DTI) Explained

Understand DTI calculations, gross qualifying income, counted monthly debts, mortgage underwriting examples and practical ways to improve the ratio.

Educational finance guidance

Debt-to-income ratio compares qualifying monthly debt obligations with qualifying gross monthly income. Lenders may use different definitions and thresholds.

What is debt-to-income ratio?

Debt-to-income ratio (DTI) compares the monthly debt payments counted by a lender with the borrower's gross monthly income. Lenders use it as one measure of how much of a borrower's income is already committed to debt payments and how much capacity may remain for a new obligation.

The basic calculation is:

DTI = qualifying monthly debt payments ÷ gross monthly income × 100

For example, if the debts counted by a lender total $2,000 per month and gross monthly income is $6,000, the DTI is 33.33%. This is a lender's qualifying ratio—not a complete household budget. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) explains that DTI uses monthly debt payments divided by gross monthly income and that limits vary by loan product and lender. See the CFPB definition of DTI.

How to calculate DTI step by step

  1. Find gross monthly income. Use income before taxes and other payroll deductions, but only include income that is eligible and documented under the lender's rules.
  2. List qualifying monthly debt payments. Include the proposed housing payment when calculating mortgage DTI, as well as other obligations the lender counts.
  3. Convert payments to monthly amounts. If a debt is paid weekly, biweekly, quarterly or annually, use the lender's required conversion method rather than simply using the most recent payment.
  4. Add the monthly obligations. Include all debts required by the specific loan program and underwriting method.
  5. Divide by gross monthly qualifying income and multiply by 100. The result is the DTI percentage.

For a basic illustration, monthly debt payments of $2,000 divided by $6,000 gross monthly income equal 0.3333, or approximately 33.33% DTI.

What income does a lender use?

Gross monthly income is generally income before tax and payroll deductions. For salaried income, a simple starting point is annual gross salary divided by 12. Hourly, commission, bonus, overtime, self-employment and rental income can require additional documentation and qualifying calculations. Lenders generally do not use every dollar a borrower receives automatically; income must meet the relevant program's eligibility, stability and documentation requirements.

For mortgage underwriting, Fannie Mae describes DTI as total monthly obligations divided by the total monthly income of borrowers to the extent that income is used to qualify. A lender may exclude income that is not eligible or not being relied on for the application. Do not substitute take-home pay or an informal estimate for the qualifying income documented by the lender.

Which debts may be included?

The exact list depends on the loan type, lender and underwriting rules. For a conventional mortgage under Fannie Mae guidance, total monthly obligations can include the qualifying housing payment, installment debts, revolving debts, lease payments, certain alimony or child-support obligations, other recurring obligations and certain rental-property losses.

  • Housing: The qualifying payment on the home being purchased or refinanced, including applicable principal, interest, taxes, insurance, mortgage insurance, association dues and other required housing expenses.
  • Installment debts: Auto loans, personal loans, student loans and other fixed-payment debts, subject to the program's rules for remaining term and payment treatment.
  • Revolving debts: Credit card and other revolving account payments, based on the lender's required calculation.
  • Leases: Vehicle or other lease payments where the underwriting rules require them.
  • Support and recurring obligations: Certain alimony, child support, maintenance and other recurring monthly payments.
  • Rental-property losses: Net losses may affect qualifying obligations under program-specific calculations.

Do not assume this list is identical for every application. For example, Fannie Mae's rules specify how certain installment debts with ten months or less remaining are treated, and lenders may have consistent, more conservative approaches that are permitted by the guide. Review the Fannie Mae Selling Guide section on DTI and ask the lender how each account is being counted.

What is usually not included in mortgage DTI?

DTI is not the same as the percentage of income spent on all living costs. Routine household expenses such as groceries, gas, childcare, phone service, utilities and commuting costs are generally part of a personal affordability budget, but they are not all treated as monthly debt obligations under every mortgage underwriting framework. Some recurring contractual obligations can still be counted, and treatment varies by program.

Fannie Mae's guide, for example, identifies certain payroll deductions such as federal, state and local taxes, FICA, commuting costs, union dues and voluntary deductions as items that are not counted as liabilities or deducted from gross income for its DTI calculation. That is a specific conventional-mortgage rule, not a universal instruction for every type of loan.

Housing expense ratio vs. total DTI

Mortgage discussions sometimes distinguish the housing expense ratio from total DTI:

  • Housing expense ratio: Qualifying monthly housing expense divided by gross monthly qualifying income.
  • Total DTI: Qualifying housing expense plus other counted monthly debt obligations, divided by gross monthly qualifying income.

For example, assume gross monthly income is $6,000, the proposed housing payment is $1,500 and other counted monthly debts are $500. The housing expense ratio is 25% ($1,500 ÷ $6,000). Total DTI is 33.33% (($1,500 + $500) ÷ $6,000).

These ratios answer different questions. Someone can have a moderate housing ratio but a higher total DTI because of auto, student, credit-card or other debt payments. The terms and treatment of housing costs can vary by loan program and property type.

Are there standard DTI limits?

There is no single DTI limit that applies to every lender and loan product. Requirements differ across conventional, FHA, VA, USDA, jumbo, personal-loan and other credit programs. A lender may also consider credit history, assets and reserves, income stability, loan-to-value, collateral, the proposed payment and the overall strength of the application.

For context, Fannie Mae's Selling Guide published April 22, 2026 states the following maximum total DTI ratios for its conventional mortgage framework:

Fannie Mae underwriting pathMaximum total DTI in the guideImportant qualification
Manual underwriting36%Can be increased up to 45% when the applicable credit-score and reserve requirements are met.
Desktop Underwriter (DU)50%Subject to DU's overall risk assessment, program eligibility and other requirements.

These are Fannie Mae program limits, not a promise of approval at those percentages and not universal limits for the U.S. mortgage market. Certain transactions have exceptions or lower maximums, and government-backed loans follow their own agency requirements. See the current Fannie Mae Selling Guide and the detailed DTI requirements.

DTI is not the same as affordability

A lender's DTI calculation is an underwriting measure. It does not show how comfortable a payment will feel after taxes, health costs, childcare, food, transport, savings, emergencies and other living expenses. A borrower can meet a lender's DTI rules and still have too little monthly cash flow for the lifestyle and financial resilience they want.

Build a separate household budget using take-home income. Include irregular bills and expenses that are not counted as DTI debts. Consider whether you can handle maintenance, insurance increases, changes in income and unexpected costs without relying on new borrowing.

Ways to improve your DTI

  • Reduce monthly debt obligations. Paying down a debt may lower a counted monthly payment, but check whether closing or paying off the account has any fees or other consequences.
  • Avoid taking on new monthly debt. New auto loans, personal loans or credit-card balances can change the DTI used during underwriting.
  • Check your credit report and application details. Correct inaccurate balances, duplicate obligations or debts that are not yours through the appropriate creditor and reporting process.
  • Document qualifying income accurately. Provide requested records for eligible salary, variable income, self-employment or other income sources; do not assume projected or unverified income will count.
  • Compare loan structures carefully. A longer term may lower a monthly payment while increasing total interest. Do not choose a loan solely to reduce DTI without considering the full cost and risk.
  • Ask the lender for a written breakdown. Confirm the qualifying income, each included debt, the proposed housing payment and the DTI calculation used for your program.

Worked example: calculate a mortgage DTI from a monthly budget

Assume a borrower has $7,500 in gross qualifying income each month. The lender is evaluating a proposed home payment of $2,100, and the borrower has an auto-loan payment of $450, a student-loan payment of $250, a qualifying credit-card payment of $100 and a child-support obligation of $300.

Monthly obligationAmount
Proposed housing payment$2,100
Auto loan$450
Student loan$250
Credit-card payment used by lender$100
Child support counted under applicable rules$300
Total qualifying monthly obligations$3,200

DTI = $3,200 ÷ $7,500 × 100 = 42.67%

The housing expense ratio in this illustration is $2,100 ÷ $7,500 = 28%. The total DTI is higher because it also includes the other counted obligations. The figures are illustrative; the lender must determine the qualifying payment and whether each obligation belongs in the calculation.

How much monthly debt capacity does a target ratio imply?

You can rearrange the formula for planning: maximum total monthly obligations at a chosen DTI = gross qualifying monthly income × chosen DTI percentage. At $7,500 of qualifying income, a hypothetical 36% ratio corresponds to $2,700 in total monthly obligations, while 45% corresponds to $3,375. If the proposed housing payment and other debts total $3,200, the arithmetic difference to the 45% figure is $175.

This is not a lender approval calculation or a recommendation to borrow up to a threshold. The chosen percentage may not be available for your loan, the lender may calculate income and debts differently, and a payment that passes an underwriting limit may still be unaffordable after taxes and living expenses.

What happens if one monthly payment changes?

If the $250 student-loan payment were legitimately eliminated and the lender accepted the payoff documentation, the example's obligations would fall to $2,950 and DTI would be 39.33% ($2,950 ÷ $7,500). If the borrower instead had $500 more in eligible gross qualifying monthly income, with obligations unchanged, DTI would be 40% ($3,200 ÷ $8,000). These are arithmetic scenarios only. Do not use savings to pay off debt without considering emergency reserves, payoff timing, fees and the lender's documentation requirements.

Convert income to a monthly figure carefully

DTI uses a monthly income figure, but paychecks do not always arrive monthly. A simple annualized conversion can help you organize records before a lender applies its income-qualification rules:

Pay frequencyBasic conversion to monthlyIllustration
Annual salaryAnnual amount ÷ 12$90,000 ÷ 12 = $7,500
Twice monthly (24 pay periods)Gross paycheck × 2$3,750 × 2 = $7,500
Every two weeks (26 pay periods)Gross paycheck × 26 ÷ 12$3,000 × 26 ÷ 12 = $6,500

Do not confuse biweekly pay (every 14 days) with twice-monthly pay (two set dates each month). They produce different annual paycheck counts. The table is a budgeting conversion, not a substitute for underwriting. Lenders may average variable earnings, review a history of overtime, bonus or commission income, assess self-employment income from tax returns, or exclude income that is not sufficiently stable, documented or eligible under the loan program.

For a mortgage application, ask the loan officer which income documents are needed and what amount will actually be used to qualify. A recent paystub alone may not establish the qualifying amount for every type of income.

Special debt situations that need lender review

Student loans

Do not assume a student loan is excluded because it is deferred, in forbearance or showing a $0 payment on a statement. Mortgage programs can prescribe how a qualifying payment is determined when the reported payment is zero or does not reflect a fully amortizing obligation. The required method depends on the loan program and current agency or investor guidance. Give the lender the latest statement and repayment-plan information, and ask which monthly amount is being used.

Co-signed loans and debts paid by someone else

If you co-signed a loan, you may remain legally responsible even when another person normally makes the payments. Some underwriting rules permit exclusion in specific circumstances when the lender receives sufficient evidence that another party has made the payments for the required period. Do not assume a verbal explanation or informal transfer history is enough; ask the lender what documentation the applicable program requires.

Installment debts with only a few payments remaining

Some mortgage rules distinguish between debts with more than ten months remaining and debts with ten months or less remaining, while still allowing short-term payments to be counted when they materially affect repayment capacity. Fannie Mae's guide sets out its own treatment. Other loan programs may use different rules. Provide the current balance, payment amount and scheduled payoff date rather than deciding on your own that a debt can be omitted.

Credit cards and revolving accounts

The lender may use a required minimum-payment calculation or another program-prescribed amount rather than the payment you happen to make voluntarily. If the statement shows no minimum due, a zero balance or a promotional arrangement, ask how that account will be treated. Avoid making large new charges or opening accounts during underwriting because the balance and required payment can change.

Alimony, child support and other recurring obligations

Support obligations can have specific treatment under mortgage underwriting rules, including distinctions based on duration and whether an amount is deducted from income or included as a monthly liability. Provide the relevant order or agreement and payment details. The lender, not a generic online DTI calculator, must apply the correct rule to your loan program.

What to ask your lender about the DTI calculation

  • What gross monthly income amount is being used, and which income sources were excluded?
  • How were weekly, biweekly, commission, bonus, overtime or self-employment earnings converted or averaged?
  • What exact housing payment is included, including taxes, insurance, mortgage insurance and association dues where applicable?
  • Which credit-card, student-loan, installment, lease, support or co-signed obligations are included?
  • Are any debts being excluded, and what payoff evidence or payment history is required?
  • Which underwriting program and DTI limit apply to this application?
  • Is the result a preliminary estimate, an automated underwriting finding or a final underwritten calculation?
  • How would a proposed debt payoff or change in down payment affect cash reserves and the complete loan application?

What can change during mortgage underwriting?

DTI can change if income is reduced, a new debt is opened, a credit-card payment changes, a loan balance or required payment is updated, or the proposed housing payment changes. Fannie Mae's guide requires lenders to reassess DTI when certain additional debts or reduced income are discovered during the mortgage process. Do not assume an initial preapproval is final approval.

While a mortgage is being processed, tell the loan officer about changes to income, employment and debt. Avoid taking on new financing or making major financial changes without first discussing the effect with the lender. The lender must evaluate the actual updated application, not just the initial estimate.

Frequently asked questions

Is DTI calculated using gross or take-home income?

The standard DTI formula uses gross monthly qualifying income—income before taxes and other deductions. A lender may apply program-specific rules to decide which income is eligible and how it must be documented.

Do utilities and groceries count in DTI?

They are important household expenses, but routine living costs such as groceries and utilities are not generally treated as debt payments in many mortgage DTI calculations. A specific recurring contractual obligation may be treated differently. Include living costs in your personal budget even when they are not part of lender DTI.

Are credit-card balances included or only monthly payments?

DTI generally uses a qualifying monthly payment for revolving debts, not the full outstanding balance divided by income. The lender determines the payment amount using the applicable program rules and account information.

Does a lower DTI guarantee mortgage approval?

No. DTI is only one part of underwriting. Credit history, income documentation, assets and reserves, property value, loan-to-value, loan type and other eligibility rules can affect the decision.

Is 36% the maximum DTI for every mortgage?

No. Limits vary by lender and program. Fannie Mae's manual-underwriting framework uses a 36% maximum that can extend to 45% if specified requirements are met, while its DU maximum is 50%. Other mortgage programs have different requirements.

Can a lender approve a borrower above 43% DTI?

Potentially, depending on the loan program and underwriting method. There is no single 43% cap that applies to all mortgage products. Ask the lender which program and current rule apply to your application.

Does paying off a credit card immediately lower DTI?

It may reduce the payment counted for the debt, but the lender must verify the payoff and apply the relevant program rules. The account's reported balance and the documentation available can affect when the change is reflected in underwriting.

Can my DTI change after preapproval?

Yes. New debt, changes in required payments, reduced income or a revised housing payment can change the ratio. Tell your lender about changes during the application and before closing.

Where can I find the DTI used by my lender?

Ask for a breakdown of the qualifying gross income, housing payment, other debts included and the underwriting program's DTI limit. Your lender can explain why particular debts or income sources were included or excluded.
