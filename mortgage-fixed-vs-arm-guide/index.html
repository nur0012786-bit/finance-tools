Fixed-Rate vs. Adjustable-Rate Mortgage (ARM): What's the Difference?

Learn the differences between fixed-rate and adjustable-rate mortgages, including payment changes, rate periods, caps, risks and comparison questions.

What is a fixed-rate mortgage?

A fixed-rate mortgage has a contractual interest rate that stays the same for the life of the loan. For a standard fully amortizing fixed-rate mortgage, the scheduled principal-and-interest payment stays the same as long as you make payments under the loan terms.

Your total housing payment can still change. Property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, escrow deposits, HOA dues and other housing costs may rise or fall even though the mortgage interest rate does not change.

What is an adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM)?

An adjustable-rate mortgage has an interest rate that can change during the loan. Many ARMs begin with an initial period when the rate is fixed, then adjust periodically according to the loan agreement. The initial rate may be lower than a comparable fixed-rate offer, but the borrower takes the risk that the rate and payment can rise later.

Do not assume all ARMs use the same schedule. Some have an initial fixed period; others may adjust sooner. The note and the ARM disclosures define when adjustments can occur, how the new rate is calculated and how much it can change.

How ARM rates are calculated: index plus margin

After the initial rate period, the ARM rate is generally calculated using two components:

Index + Margin = Fully indexed rate, subject to the loan's rate caps and other terms.

  • Index: A published benchmark that changes with broader market conditions. One index used in some ARMs is the Secured Overnight Financing Rate (SOFR). Your loan documents identify the specific index and how it is measured.
  • Margin: A number of percentage points the lender adds to the index. The margin is set in the loan agreement and generally does not change after closing.
  • Fully indexed rate: The index plus the margin, subject to caps, floors and other contractual provisions.

The margin matters when comparing lenders. Two ARMs can have the same introductory rate but different margins, indexes or caps, leading to different future pricing. Review the actual index and margin rather than comparing introductory rates alone.

What does a 5/1, 5/6 or 7/6 ARM mean?

The first number generally identifies how many years the initial rate lasts. The second identifies how often the rate can adjust after that period. For example:

  • 5/1 ARM: The initial rate is fixed for five years, then the rate can adjust once each year.
  • 5/6 ARM: The initial rate is fixed for five years, then the rate can adjust every six months.
  • 7/6 ARM: The initial rate is fixed for seven years, then the rate can adjust every six months.

These are examples of common naming conventions, not a substitute for the loan's actual terms. Confirm the first adjustment date, subsequent adjustment frequency and any special provisions in the Loan Estimate, AIR table and note.

Understand the three main ARM rate caps

Most ARMs have limits on how much the rate may change. The CFPB describes three common categories:

  • Initial adjustment cap: Limits the increase or decrease at the first adjustment after the initial fixed period.
  • Subsequent adjustment cap: Limits the change at each later adjustment.
  • Lifetime cap: Limits how far the rate can move above its initial rate over the life of the loan. A floor may also limit how far the rate can fall.

A shorthand such as 2/1/5 often means a maximum two-percentage-point change at the first adjustment, one point at each later adjustment, and a maximum five-point increase over the initial rate during the loan. Always verify the definitions and whether the caps apply to increases and decreases in the specific contract.

Illustrative cap example: With a 5% initial rate and 2/1/5 caps, the first adjusted rate could not rise above 7% at the first adjustment. Later adjustments could add no more than one percentage point each, and the lifetime cap would limit the rate to five percentage points above the 5% initial rate, making 10% the maximum rate. The index and margin may imply a lower rate than a cap allows; caps are limits, not predictions of where the rate will go.

Teaser rates and payment shock

Some ARMs begin with a discounted introductory rate that is below the fully indexed rate. This is sometimes called a teaser rate. The rate can increase at the first reset even if the index has not changed, because the introductory discount expires and the index-plus-margin formula takes effect.

Example: If the index is 3.5% and the margin is 2.75%, the fully indexed rate is 6.25% before applying the caps and any floor. If the initial teaser rate is 5%, the rate can rise to the fully indexed rate at the first adjustment even when the index remains unchanged, subject to the contract's caps.

Payment shock can also occur when the payment is recalculated over a shorter remaining repayment period. A lower initial payment does not tell you what the payment will be after the introductory period ends.

A real disclosure illustration from the CFPB handbook

The CFPB's September 2026 Consumer Handbook on Adjustable-Rate Mortgages includes a sample $350,000 ARM with a 5% initial rate. The sample shows initial monthly principal and interest of $1,878.88. In the illustrated maximum-rate scenario, the rate can reach 10% in year eight and monthly principal and interest can reach $2,854.

That is approximately $975 more per month in principal and interest than the initial payment. It excludes any separate change in property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance or other housing expenses. This is the CFPB's educational Loan Estimate example, not a prediction, current market quote or typical payment increase for every ARM.

The point is to examine the maximum payment disclosed for your own loan—not to assume the initial rate will last or that you will be able to refinance before it changes.

Where to find ARM terms in the Loan Estimate

The CFPB handbook explains the ARM-specific information in the Loan Estimate:

  • Page 1 — Product: Identifies whether the loan is an adjustable-rate product, such as a 7/6-month ARM. Check the rate-lock status too.
  • Page 1 — Loan Terms: Shows the initial interest rate, whether it can increase, the initial principal-and-interest payment and whether that payment can change.
  • Page 1 — Projected Payments: Review the projected payment ranges and the time periods when payments may change. Distinguish principal and interest from taxes, insurance, mortgage insurance and escrow.
  • Page 2 — Adjustable Interest Rate (AIR) Table: Review the index plus margin, initial rate, minimum and maximum rates, first and later adjustment timing, and rate-change limits.

Read the full table and the promissory note. Do not rely on a verbal description or a marketing name alone. The loan's actual documents control.

Special ARM features that need extra scrutiny

Not every ARM has these features, but ask whether any apply to the product you are considering:

  • Interest-only period: Payments cover interest but do not reduce principal during the stated period. When principal repayment begins, the payment can rise even if the interest rate does not change.
  • Negative amortization: If the required payment is less than the interest due, unpaid interest can be added to the balance. The amount you owe can increase rather than decrease.
  • Payment option ARM: May offer different payment choices, including a minimum payment that could cause negative amortization.
  • Rate floor: Sets a minimum interest rate, so the loan may not benefit fully if the index falls.
  • Conversion feature: May allow conversion to a fixed rate under a formula in the agreement, potentially with a fee. The conversion rate may not match rates available in the market at that time.
  • Prepayment penalty: Check whether a fee could apply if you pay off or refinance the loan early.

Ask the lender to show the cost and payment impact of each special feature. A smaller initial payment may come with additional fees or much higher payments later.

Do not rely on selling or refinancing before the reset

It is risky to choose an ARM on the assumption that you will sell the home or refinance before the initial rate period ends. Your home's value could fall, your credit or income could change, or you could face unexpected expenses. You might not qualify for a new loan when you want one.

Evaluate the ARM as though you may keep it through the adjustment period. If you could not afford the highest rate and payment allowed by the contract, the loan may expose your household to significant financial risk.

How to compare an ARM with a fixed-rate offer

  • Compare offers for the same loan amount, repayment term, property, occupancy and points or lender-credit structure.
  • Compare the initial interest rate and initial principal-and-interest payment—but do not stop there.
  • Record the ARM index, margin, first adjustment date and how often the rate adjusts.
  • Compare initial, subsequent and lifetime rate caps, plus any floor.
  • Ask for the maximum possible interest rate and maximum principal-and-interest payment under the contract.
  • Compare total projected housing payments, including mortgage insurance, property taxes, homeowners insurance and escrow items.
  • Review points and upfront charges. Points paid on an ARM may reduce the rate only during the initial period, rather than for the whole loan.
  • Consider how long you expect to keep the loan, but do not assume a sale or refinance will be possible.

Illustrative fixed vs. ARM decision

Imagine a fixed-rate offer and an ARM offer for the same loan amount. The ARM begins with a lower rate and payment, but adjusts after its initial period. A borrower who values payment certainty may prefer the fixed-rate offer even if its initial payment is higher. A borrower considering the ARM should be able to withstand the maximum payment, understand the reset schedule and have a plan that does not depend on future refinancing.

There is no universal winner. The right comparison depends on the written loan terms, your budget, how long you may keep the loan and your ability to absorb payment changes.

Questions to ask your lender

  • How long does the initial rate last, and when is the first adjustment?
  • How often can the rate adjust after the first reset?
  • Which index is used, where is it published and what is the margin?
  • What are the initial, subsequent and lifetime caps? Is there a floor?
  • What is the fully indexed rate today, and how does it compare with the initial rate?
  • What is the highest interest rate and highest principal-and-interest payment permitted by the contract?
  • Can the loan have negative amortization or an interest-only period?
  • How and when will I receive notices before a rate or payment change?
  • Are points being charged, and do they reduce the rate only during the initial period?
  • Does the loan have a conversion option or prepayment penalty?

Frequently asked questions

Is a fixed-rate mortgage safer than an ARM?

A fixed-rate mortgage offers more certainty about the contractual interest rate and scheduled principal-and-interest payment. An ARM adds the risk of future interest-rate and payment increases. Whether the added risk is acceptable depends on the loan terms and the borrower's ability to pay the maximum possible amount.

Can an ARM rate go down?

It can, depending on the index, margin, caps and floor. Some loans limit decreases or have a floor rate. Review both upward and downward adjustment rules in the loan documents.

What does 5/1 ARM mean?

It generally means the initial interest rate lasts five years and then can adjust once each year. Confirm the exact first adjustment date and subsequent schedule in the contract.

What does 5/6 ARM mean?

It generally means the initial interest rate lasts five years and then can adjust every six months. The loan documents specify the actual adjustment dates and formula.

What is the fully indexed rate?

It is generally the current index plus the loan's margin, subject to rate caps and other contractual limits. It may be higher than a discounted introductory rate.

What does a 2/1/5 cap mean?

It commonly describes a two-percentage-point maximum first adjustment, a one-point maximum change at each later adjustment and a five-point lifetime cap above the initial rate. Verify the precise definitions in your ARM agreement.

Can I refinance before the ARM rate adjusts?

You may be able to apply for a refinance, but approval is not guaranteed. Home value, credit, income, debt, rates and lending rules may change. Do not take an ARM on the assumption that refinancing will definitely be available.

Can my total mortgage payment rise even if the rate does not?

Yes. Property taxes, insurance, escrow deposits, mortgage insurance and other housing expenses may change independently of the mortgage interest rate.

What is negative amortization?

Negative amortization occurs when the payment does not cover all interest due and unpaid interest is added to the loan balance. The balance can grow even while payments are being made.

Where can I find the maximum ARM rate and payment?

Review the ARM disclosures and AIR Table, and ask the lender to calculate the maximum rate and payment. The Loan Estimate includes the key ARM features and projected payment information; read it together with the loan agreement.
