How to use this debt payoff calculator

Add each credit card, personal loan or other debt as a separate entry. For each one, enter a recognizable name, current balance, annual interest rate and the monthly payment you plan to make. For revolving accounts, use the current required minimum as the starting planned payment; actual minimums can change, so update the figure when your statement changes.

Enter the extra amount you can consistently devote to debt each month. The calculator adds that to the sum of all entered monthly payments. Compare avalanche and snowball, then choose which strategy's monthly schedule to inspect. The results assume that your overall monthly debt budget remains the same as each debt is cleared.

Debt avalanche: highest interest rate first

The avalanche method directs extra money to the debt with the highest APR while continuing the planned payment on every other debt. Once a debt is paid off, its unused payment amount rolls into the next highest-rate debt. This approach generally reduces interest cost when balances, rates and payment assumptions are otherwise comparable.

Debt snowball: smallest balance first

The snowball method directs extra money to the smallest outstanding balance, regardless of APR. When that debt is cleared, its payment rolls into the next-smallest balance. Clearing accounts sooner can create visible progress and help some people stay motivated, but it may cost more interest if higher-rate debts remain outstanding longer.

Worked example: choose an order

Imagine three debts: a $4,000 credit card at 27% APR with a $150 monthly payment, a $2,000 credit card at 21% APR with an $80 monthly payment, and a $6,000 personal loan at 12% APR with a $250 monthly payment. Add $100 in extra monthly budget.

  • Avalanche order: the 27% card, then the 21% card, then the 12% loan.
  • Snowball order: the $2,000 card, then the $4,000 card, then the $6,000 loan.
  • Total monthly budget: $150 + $80 + $250 + $100 = $580.

The calculator simulates each method using the same $580 total monthly budget. With the monthly-interest assumptions used here, the example takes about 25 months and $2,461.20 in interest with avalanche, compared with about 26 months and $2,578.93 in interest with snowball. These are calculator illustrations, not predictions of changing minimum payments, daily interest or fees.

How the calculator rolls payments forward

At the start of each modeled month, the calculator adds one month's interest to each remaining balance. It then applies the planned payment entered for each debt. Any part of the total budget that is not needed to satisfy those payments is directed to the next target in the selected strategy. If a debt is paid in full before its planned payment is used, the unused portion is also available for the target debt.

This keeps the total monthly budget constant and models the common “rollover” approach. Real creditors set their own minimum payments, due dates, posting rules and interest calculations. Your required payment may change as a balance falls, so replace the entered payment amounts with current statement values when planning.

Important limits of this estimate

  • It applies monthly interest as APR divided by 12, rather than calculating daily interest from transaction dates.
  • It treats each APR as fixed for the simulation and does not model variable-rate changes or promotional expirations.
  • It does not add new purchases, borrowing, late fees, annual fees, transfer fees or other charges.
  • It uses the entered monthly payment as a fixed planned amount. Credit card minimums can change from statement to statement.
  • It does not model every lender's allocation rules for different APR balances within one credit card account.
  • It assumes the same total monthly budget is available throughout the payoff period.

Credit card payment allocation is not the same as choosing which account to pay

This calculator chooses which separate debt account receives extra money. Federal credit card rules also govern how a card issuer allocates a payment across different APR balances within one credit card account. Generally, the amount paid above that account's required minimum must go first to its highest-APR balance. A special rule applies during the two billing cycles immediately before certain deferred-interest promotions expire. See Regulation Z, § 1026.53 and check your card agreement.

Build a realistic monthly debt budget

Choose an extra amount that remains affordable after essential expenses, housing, utilities, food, transportation, insurance and other required obligations. Avoid setting a target so high that you need to borrow again to pay everyday costs. If your income or expenses change, revise the budget and rerun the estimate.

If you cannot make a required payment, contact the creditor before missing it and ask whether a hardship arrangement is available. Get the payment amount, interest rate, fees, duration and consequences in writing. Be cautious of debt-relief companies that guarantee they can erase debts, demand upfront fees or tell you to stop communicating with creditors. The FTC explains how to recognize debt-relief scams.

Frequently asked questions

Which debt payoff strategy saves the most interest?

The avalanche method generally saves more interest when it targets the highest-rate debt first and other assumptions are comparable. The exact difference depends on balances, APRs, fees and payments.

Is the snowball method a bad choice?

No. It prioritizes smaller balances and can provide quicker account-level progress. Some people find those milestones easier to maintain, even if the method may cost more interest.

Should I keep paying the other debts while targeting one?

Yes. Continue paying at least the required amount on every account. Direct the extra budget to the current target while keeping the other accounts current.

Can I include credit cards and personal loans together?

Yes. Enter each account separately with its own balance, APR and planned monthly payment. The model applies the same monthly-interest assumptions to every debt, so actual terms may differ.

Why does a credit card minimum payment change?

Issuer formulas can use a percentage of the balance, interest, fees or past-due amounts. The amount can therefore change between statements. Update the planned payment in the calculator using your current statement.

What if my total payment budget does not cover all monthly interest?

The calculator may not be able to pay off the debts within its 100-year simulation limit. Increase the planned payment or extra budget where affordable, or contact creditors about hardship options. Do not rely on a payoff estimate that does not reach zero.

Does this calculator include late fees or new charges?

No. It assumes no new borrowing or additional fees. Add those costs to your own planning separately and avoid adding new balances while trying to repay debt.

Will the results match my creditor's payoff amount?

No. The estimate uses monthly interest and fixed payments. A creditor's payoff amount may include interest through a specific date, fees or other account-specific items. Request an official payoff figure when you need an exact amount.
