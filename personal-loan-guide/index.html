PERSONAL LOANS

What is a personal loan?

A personal installment loan lets you borrow a stated amount and repay it in scheduled installments over an agreed term. The lender generally provides the loan proceeds at the beginning, then you make payments according to the contract. Many personal loans have fixed monthly payments, but rates and terms differ by lender and product.

Common uses may include consolidating higher-interest debt, paying for a planned home project, covering a large expense or handling an unexpected cost. A loan is not automatically the right solution for any of these purposes. Before borrowing, compare the total cost with alternatives and make sure the payment fits your budget.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) describes personal installment loans as closed-end loans that are repaid in fixed amounts over a specific period, while noting that lenders may offer fixed or adjustable rates and consider factors such as credit history, income, existing debt, loan amount and term. Read the CFPB overview of personal installment loans.

Secured vs. unsecured personal loans

Many personal loans are unsecured, meaning the borrower does not pledge a specific asset as collateral. The lender evaluates the borrower's credit and ability to repay, and the borrower remains legally responsible for the debt. Failure to repay can still lead to collections, credit-report consequences or legal action as allowed by the agreement and law.

A secured loan is backed by collateral, such as money in an account or another eligible asset. If the borrower defaults, the lender may have rights to take or sell the collateral under the contract and applicable law. A secured offer may have a different rate or eligibility standard, but pledging an asset adds a risk that should not be overlooked. Ask exactly what collateral is required and what happens after a missed payment.

Do not assume a lower advertised rate makes a secured loan safer or less expensive overall. Compare the APR, fees, repayment term and consequences of default. See the Secured vs. Unsecured Loans Guide for a broader comparison.

How interest rate and APR differ

The interest rate is the rate charged on the outstanding loan balance. APR is a broader annualized measure intended to help compare the cost of credit. Depending on the product and applicable disclosure rules, APR may reflect the interest rate and certain finance charges, such as an origination fee. It does not necessarily include every cost you might incur, such as late fees or optional services.

Compare the APR and the interest rate together. A loan with a lower interest rate can have a higher APR when it carries substantial upfront finance charges. Also check whether the rate is fixed for the entire term or can change. A fixed rate generally makes scheduled principal-and-interest payments predictable; a variable rate can change under the contract and may change the payment or total interest cost.

For more detail, read the Personal Loan APR vs. Interest Rate Guide. The CFPB also explains why APR is useful when comparing the costs of different credit products. See the CFPB APR explanation.

Understand the fees before you accept

Personal loans can include fees in addition to interest. The CFPB identifies origination fees, documentation fees and late fees among charges that may apply. Credit insurance, disability insurance or other add-on products may be offered as well; ask whether each is optional, what it costs and whether the price is included in the APR or financed into the loan.

  • Origination fee: A charge for processing or originating the loan. It may be deducted from the proceeds before funds are sent to you.
  • Documentation or processing fee: A fee associated with preparing or processing the loan, if disclosed in the offer.
  • Late fee: A charge that may apply if a payment is late, subject to the contract and law.
  • Optional credit or disability insurance: An add-on that may cover specified events under its policy terms. It is not automatically required; ask for the price and exclusions and compare whether it is worth buying.
  • Prepayment charge: A fee some contracts may impose for paying early. Check whether one applies and how it is calculated.

Read the lender's disclosures and the final agreement, not only the headline rate in an advertisement. The CFPB's personal installment loan fee guide recommends reviewing loan documents and comparing offers from multiple lenders. The FDIC also notes that fees may be deducted from loan proceeds and that some loans have prepayment penalties. Review the FDIC loan-cost overview.

Gross loan amount vs. money you receive

The amount you sign for is not always the amount deposited into your account. If an origination fee is deducted upfront, the net proceeds can be lower than the principal balance you must repay.

For example, suppose the loan amount is $10,000 and a 5% origination fee ($500) is deducted from the proceeds. You may receive approximately $9,500 while the contract still requires repayment of the $10,000 principal, plus applicable interest and other charges. The precise treatment depends on the agreement and disclosure.

This matters when you are borrowing to pay a specific bill or consolidate debt. Make sure the net proceeds are enough for the intended purpose. Do not compare a $10,000 gross loan with another lender's $10,000 net disbursement as if the offers were identical.

How loan amount and term affect payments

For a standard fixed-rate installment loan, the scheduled payment depends mainly on the amount financed, interest rate and repayment term. Borrowing more generally increases the payment. Extending the term usually lowers the scheduled monthly payment but can increase the total interest paid because the debt remains outstanding for longer.

Consider two offers for the same amount and APR: the longer-term offer may look easier to fit into a monthly budget, but it can cost more over the full term. Compare the monthly payment, total of scheduled payments, total interest and fees—not just one number.

Also check whether the lender uses daily simple interest, how it applies extra payments, whether additional payments go to principal, and whether a final payoff quote includes accrued interest. Do not assume an extra payment automatically shortens the term unless the lender applies it to the principal as you expect.

Illustrative example: compare monthly payment and total cost

Suppose you borrow $10,000 at a fixed 12% annual interest rate for 36 months, with no fees. Under a standard monthly amortization estimate:

  • Estimated monthly principal-and-interest payment: about $332.14.
  • Total of 36 scheduled payments: about $11,957.
  • Estimated interest over the term: about $1,957.

This is an arithmetic illustration using a fixed rate and monthly payment schedule. It excludes origination fees, optional products, late charges and other contract costs. A lender's disclosed payment may differ slightly because of rounding, payment dates and the way the loan is calculated.

How to compare personal loan offers

Get written offers from more than one lender when practical. Compare offers for the same borrowing amount and a similar term so that the differences are easier to identify. Review these details side by side:

  • APR and interest rate, and whether the rate is fixed or variable.
  • Gross principal amount, origination fee and net proceeds you will receive.
  • Repayment term, scheduled payment and total amount of scheduled payments.
  • Late fees, returned-payment fees and any prepayment charge.
  • Optional insurance or other add-ons and whether they are included in the amount financed.
  • Whether an advertised discount depends on autopay, direct deposit or another condition.
  • Collateral, co-signer or co-borrower requirements, if any.
  • What happens if you miss a payment or need temporary hardship assistance.

Ask the lender for the complete loan disclosure and agreement before signing. Confirm the exact due date, first payment date, how payments are applied, how to make extra payments and how to obtain a payoff quote. Keep a copy of the final signed documents.

Using a personal loan to consolidate debt

A personal loan can combine multiple debts into one installment payment, but consolidation does not erase the debt. It replaces several balances with a new obligation. Compare the new loan's APR, origination fee, term and total repayment cost with the current debts' costs and payoff plans.

A lower monthly payment is not proof that consolidation saves money. The payment may be lower because the new loan lasts longer, which can increase the total interest paid. Calculate the total cost across the full term and check whether the loan proceeds will actually pay off the old balances in full.

There is another behavioral risk: if you consolidate credit-card balances and then use the cards again, you can end up with both the new loan and new card debt. Consider whether you have addressed the spending or cash-flow issue that caused the balances before using consolidation as a solution. Keep making required payments on existing accounts until you confirm the old balances have been paid and the creditors have posted the payments.

Check affordability before you borrow

Build a monthly budget using take-home income, not just the gross income a lender may use for underwriting. Include housing, utilities, food, transportation, insurance, childcare, healthcare, savings and all existing debt payments. Then add the proposed personal-loan payment and leave room for irregular costs and a cash buffer.

A lender's approval does not mean the payment is comfortable for your household. A payment that leaves no room for emergencies can force you to borrow again. If the loan is intended to cover recurring living expenses, review the underlying budget shortfall before taking on another fixed obligation.

What happens if you miss a payment?

A late or missed payment may lead to fees, additional interest, collection activity and negative credit reporting, depending on the contract, timing and applicable law. If you think you will not be able to make a payment, contact the lender as soon as possible. Ask whether deferment, forbearance, a temporary payment plan or another hardship option is available. Get any revised terms in writing and understand whether interest continues to accrue and whether the loan term changes.

The CFPB notes that lenders may offer options such as deferment, forbearance or a payment plan, but you should confirm the terms for your specific loan. Read the CFPB's personal installment loan guidance.

Watch for personal loan scams

Be cautious of unsolicited messages or companies that promise guaranteed approval regardless of your credit, pressure you to act immediately, or demand payment before you can receive the promised loan. The FTC warns that advance-fee loan scams may describe an upfront payment as processing, insurance or paperwork. A legitimate lender may disclose application or other fees, but paying a fee should not be represented as a guarantee that you will receive a loan.

Verify the lender's legal name, contact details and registration with the relevant state regulator. Do not send money through gift cards, cryptocurrency or wire transfers to an individual in exchange for a promised loan. The FTC explains warning signs and steps to protect yourself in its guide to advance-fee loan scams.

Frequently asked questions

Are personal loans installment loans?

Many personal loans are closed-end installment loans: you receive a stated amount and repay it in scheduled installments over a set term. Product terms vary by lender.

Does a personal loan have a fixed interest rate?

Some personal loans have fixed rates, while others may have adjustable rates. Read the agreement to understand whether the rate and payment can change.

Is APR more important than the interest rate?

APR is useful for comparing the annualized cost of credit because it can reflect certain finance charges. You should still inspect the interest rate, fees, net proceeds, term and total repayment amount.

Does an origination fee reduce the amount I receive?

It can. Some lenders deduct an origination fee from the loan proceeds, so the net amount deposited may be lower than the principal amount you must repay. Confirm the disbursement amount in the written offer.

Does a longer personal loan term cost more?

It can. A longer term usually spreads repayment over more months and may lower the scheduled payment, but can increase total interest. Compare total repayment cost as well as monthly affordability.

Can I pay a personal loan off early?

Some loans allow early repayment without a charge; others may have a prepayment penalty or specific payoff calculation. Check the agreement and request a payoff quote before sending the final payment.

Should I use a personal loan to consolidate credit cards?

Compare the new loan's APR, fees, term and total cost with the existing card balances. A lower monthly payment can result from a longer term, and using the paid-off cards again can leave you with more debt.

What if I cannot make my monthly payment?

Contact the lender as soon as possible and ask about hardship options such as a payment plan or temporary deferment. Confirm fees, interest, credit reporting and the revised schedule in writing.

Can a lender guarantee approval before checking my information?

A promise of guaranteed approval in exchange for an upfront fee is a major warning sign. The FTC advises consumers not to pay for a promise of a loan and to verify unfamiliar lenders before sharing money or sensitive information.
