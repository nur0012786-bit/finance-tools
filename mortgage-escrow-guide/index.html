Mortgage Escrow Account Explained

Learn what a mortgage escrow account is, which housing costs may be paid through escrow, and how escrow affects monthly mortgage payments.

UNDERSTAND THE BASICS

What is a mortgage escrow account?

A mortgage escrow account is an account your mortgage servicer controls to collect and hold money for certain property-related bills, then pay those bills when they come due. It is sometimes called an impound account. Instead of paying a large property-tax or insurance bill yourself once or twice a year, you generally contribute toward it through your monthly mortgage payment.

For many homeowners, common escrow items include property taxes and homeowners insurance. Depending on the loan and agreement, the account may also cover flood insurance or other permitted property-related charges. Not every loan requires escrow, and some loan types or legal requirements may make it mandatory.

What escrow does — and what it does not do

Escrow helps the servicer make scheduled payments for covered bills. It does not reduce your mortgage principal or pay mortgage interest. Those amounts are separate parts of your housing payment.

Your monthly amount sent to the servicer may include:

  • Principal: reduces the amount you borrowed.
  • Interest: the cost of borrowing under your loan terms.
  • Escrow deposit: money collected toward eligible taxes, insurance and other escrowed bills.
  • Other loan-related charges: such as mortgage insurance or HOA dues, if applicable. These may be collected separately and are not automatically part of the escrow account.

Do not assume that every property expense is included. Check your Loan Estimate, Closing Disclosure, monthly statement and escrow disclosure to see which bills the servicer will pay and which bills you must pay directly.

How the monthly escrow amount is estimated

The servicer estimates the annual expenses expected to be paid from escrow and generally collects one-twelfth of that estimated total each month. For covered federally related mortgage loans, Regulation X limits the amount a servicer may require and generally permits a cushion of no more than one-sixth of estimated annual escrow disbursements. The loan documents or state law may set a lower limit, and the rules do not require every account to use the maximum cushion.

Illustrative calculation: Suppose estimated annual property taxes are $4,800 and annual homeowners insurance is $1,800. The estimated annual escrow bills total $6,600. Dividing by 12 gives a baseline escrow deposit of $550 per month, before any permitted cushion or adjustment for an existing shortage or surplus.

The $550 is only the estimated escrow portion. It is not the full mortgage payment, and the actual deposit depends on the servicer's estimates, bill due dates, account balance, applicable law and loan documents.

Why your mortgage payment can change

Even with a fixed interest rate, the total amount you send each month can change when escrow costs change. Examples include:

  • Your local government raises the assessed value or tax rate, increasing property taxes.
  • Your homeowners or flood insurance premium increases, or the policy coverage changes.
  • The servicer's annual analysis identifies a shortage that must be repaid or a new projected monthly deposit.
  • A prior estimate differed from the actual bill, or a new escrowed expense has been added or removed.

A fixed-rate mortgage's scheduled principal-and-interest payment generally does not change just because property taxes or insurance premiums rise. The escrow portion can change, which changes the total monthly payment. Adjustable-rate loans, temporary buydowns, mortgage insurance changes and fees are separate potential reasons for a payment change.

What is an annual escrow analysis?

For escrow accounts covered by Regulation X, the servicer conducts an analysis at the end of the escrow computation year. The annual escrow statement generally must be sent within 30 calendar days after that year ends. It compares the account's activity during the previous year with a projection for the next year.

Review the statement for:

  • Your current total monthly payment and the part allocated to escrow.
  • How much went into escrow during the prior year.
  • How much was paid out for taxes, insurance and other identified charges.
  • The account balance at the end of the period.
  • The next year's projected payments and any explanation of a surplus, shortage or deficiency.

Compare the tax and insurance amounts with the actual bills or renewal notices you received. Check that each payment is listed, the payee is correct and the disbursement dates make sense.

Escrow shortage, surplus and deficiency explained

These terms are not interchangeable:

  • Shortage: the account balance is below the target balance calculated in the escrow analysis.
  • Surplus: the account balance is above the target balance.
  • Deficiency: the account has a negative balance.

For covered loans, Regulation X sets procedures for handling these amounts. For example, when a current borrower has a surplus of $50 or more, the servicer generally must refund it within 30 days of the analysis; a smaller surplus may be refunded or credited to the next year's escrow payments. Shortage repayment options depend on the size of the shortage and may include spreading repayment over at least 12 months. Read the statement carefully: an escrow shortage repayment can be added to the new monthly escrow amount, making the payment increase temporarily or for the next computation year.

Ask the servicer for the escrow analysis worksheet if the calculation is unclear. Do not assume the whole payment increase is a shortage repayment; a higher tax or insurance estimate may also increase the ongoing monthly escrow deposit.

Can you cancel or waive an escrow account?

Sometimes, but not automatically. Whether escrow can be waived or cancelled depends on the loan documents, loan type, investor or program requirements, applicable law, equity, payment history and servicer rules. Some loans require escrow for a specified period or for the life of the loan. Ask your servicer whether your loan is eligible, whether a fee applies, and what written approval is required before changing how bills are paid.

Without escrow, you become responsible for paying property taxes and insurance directly and on time. Budget for the full bills and keep proof of payment. Missing property taxes can lead to penalties, a tax lien or foreclosure; failing to maintain required insurance can lead to lender action, including force-placed insurance that may be more expensive and offer less protection.

Illustrative annual escrow example

Assume a homeowner's servicer estimates $4,800 in annual property taxes and $1,800 in annual homeowners insurance. The projected bills total $6,600, so the baseline monthly escrow contribution is $550. Later, the insurer renews the policy at $2,100 instead of $1,800. If the tax estimate stays the same, expected annual bills become $6,900 and the baseline deposit becomes $575 per month. A separate shortage from the prior year could increase the amount collected further, depending on the escrow analysis and applicable rules.

This example isolates the escrow calculation. It is not a quote, does not include a mortgage principal-and-interest payment, and does not predict a specific servicer's adjustment.

What to do if an escrow bill was not paid

Check your mortgage statement and escrow transaction history, then contact your servicer promptly. If a tax authority or insurer says a bill is unpaid, contact that organization too so you understand deadlines, penalties and any risk to coverage or property title. Keep copies of notices, bills, payment confirmations and notes from calls.

If you believe the servicer made an error, ask for an explanation and a corrected statement. You may need to send a written Notice of Error or Request for Information to the specific address listed by your servicer for those requests; that address may differ from the payment address. The CFPB recommends monitoring statements and tax and insurance bills and contacting the servicer promptly when something appears wrong.

Escrow checklist for homeowners

  • Confirm which exact bills are paid from escrow and which you must pay yourself.
  • Keep copies of property-tax bills, insurance declarations and renewal notices.
  • Review your monthly statement and annual escrow analysis rather than assuming the amount is correct.
  • Check tax and insurance disbursement amounts, recipients, dates and account balance.
  • Ask how a shortage is calculated and whether repayment will be spread across monthly payments.
  • Before requesting an escrow waiver, confirm eligibility, fees and any loan-program restrictions in writing.
  • Keep enough savings to cover property expenses if your loan does not use escrow or if you become responsible for bills directly.

Frequently asked questions

Is an escrow account the same as a down payment?

No. A down payment is money you contribute toward the home's purchase price. Escrow deposits are funds collected for eligible recurring property expenses such as taxes and insurance. Initial escrow deposits may also be part of cash to close, but they are not an additional down payment.

Does escrow pay my mortgage principal and interest?

No. Escrow funds are held for the bills identified in the escrow arrangement. Principal and interest are separate parts of your mortgage payment.

Does every mortgage require an escrow account?

No. Some lenders or loan programs require one, while other loans may permit an escrow waiver. Requirements depend on the loan terms, program and applicable law.

Why did my payment go up if I have a fixed mortgage rate?

Your principal-and-interest payment may remain the same while property taxes, insurance premiums or escrow shortage repayments increase the escrow portion of your total payment. Review the itemized payment statement and annual escrow analysis.

How much can the servicer keep as an escrow cushion?

For covered federally related mortgage loans, Regulation X generally caps the cushion at one-sixth of estimated annual escrow disbursements, which is approximately two months. State law or the loan documents may require a lower amount, and a servicer may choose a smaller cushion or none.

What happens if my escrow account has a surplus?

For covered loans, if the annual analysis identifies a surplus of at least $50 and you are current under the applicable rule, the servicer generally must refund it within 30 days after the analysis. A smaller surplus may be refunded or credited to the next year's escrow payments.

What happens if my escrow account has a shortage?

The servicer's options depend on the shortage amount and the applicable rules. It may allow the shortage to remain or require repayment using permitted methods, including installments in qualifying circumstances. Your annual statement should explain how the shortage is handled.

Can the servicer pay a tax bill late?

For covered loans, Regulation X generally requires timely escrow disbursements by the deadline to avoid a penalty, provided your mortgage payment is not more than 30 days overdue. If a bill appears unpaid, contact both the servicer and the tax authority promptly.

Can I cancel escrow and pay taxes and insurance myself?

Possibly, but eligibility depends on your loan terms, program, applicable law and servicer requirements. Obtain written approval before stopping escrow payments, and make sure you can pay every bill directly by its due date.
