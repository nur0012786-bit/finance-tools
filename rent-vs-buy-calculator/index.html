RENT VS BUY GUIDE

How should you compare renting and buying?

Renting and buying have different cash flows and risks. The CFPB notes that buying can involve principal and interest plus property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, HOA fees and maintenance. Selling a home can also involve transaction costs.

Why time horizon matters

Buying and selling a home can be expensive, so the number of years you expect to stay matters. The CFPB specifically recommends considering whether you are willing to stay put for a few years and notes that moving again after a short period can make buying more costly.

What this calculator measures

This calculator projects rent payments with an annual increase, then compares them with estimated ownership cash outflows. For buying, it models mortgage principal and interest, property taxes, homeowners insurance, maintenance, upfront closing costs and selling costs. It also estimates remaining mortgage balance and home equity after the selected period.

Rent vs buy example

Try $2,500 monthly rent, a $400,000 home, $80,000 down, a 6.5% mortgage rate and a 7-year horizon. Adjust rent growth, appreciation, taxes, insurance and maintenance to reflect your local situation. The result is a scenario comparison—not a prediction or a recommendation.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Does buying always cost less than renting?

No. The result depends on assumptions such as rent growth, home appreciation, mortgage rate, taxes, insurance, maintenance, transaction costs and how long you stay.

Why does the calculator include maintenance?

Homeowners are responsible for repairs and maintenance. The CFPB recommends budgeting for these costs because they can range from small repairs to major replacements.

Why are selling costs included?

Selling a home can involve commissions, taxes and other transaction costs. Including an estimated selling-cost percentage makes the comparison more complete over a finite time horizon.

Does the calculator include mortgage principal as a cost?

Principal is a cash outflow, but it also reduces the mortgage balance and contributes to home equity. The model therefore shows both ownership cash outflows and ending equity rather than treating principal as pure economic expense.

Does it include tax deductions?

No. Tax effects can depend on individual circumstances, filing status and applicable federal and state rules. The calculator leaves them out so users can compare the core housing assumptions without making a personalized tax calculation.

Is this a financial recommendation?

No. It is a scenario calculator. Use realistic local assumptions and consider your cash reserves, mobility, maintenance responsibilities and long-term plans before making a housing decision.
