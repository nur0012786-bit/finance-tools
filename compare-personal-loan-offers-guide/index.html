How to Compare Personal Loan Offers

Compare personal loan offers using the same net cash amount and term. Review APR, fees, payment, total cost, credit inquiries and lender terms.

Educational finance guidance

Comparing personal loans is easier when the offers are evaluated using the same loan amount and time horizon. Look beyond a single advertised rate.

COMPARE LOAN OFFERS

Start with the same borrowing need

A useful comparison starts by deciding how much money you actually need and how quickly you can realistically repay it. Ask each lender to quote a similar loan amount and repayment term. If one offer provides more cash or stretches repayment over more months, its monthly payment cannot be compared fairly with an offer structured differently.

For a fair comparison, focus on the amount of money you will actually receive after upfront deductions—not only the gross principal printed at the top of the offer. A loan with an origination fee deducted from proceeds may require you to repay more principal than the cash deposited into your account.

Personal installment loan terms can vary with your credit history, income, existing debts, requested amount, term and lender. The CFPB recommends shopping around and reviewing the lender's loan disclosures and documents before agreeing. See the CFPB guide to personal loan fees.

Compare the interest rate and APR

The interest rate is the rate used to calculate interest on the outstanding loan balance. APR is a broader annualized measure that can include certain finance charges as well as interest. APR is useful for comparing similar offers, particularly when fees differ, but it does not necessarily include every charge you could incur after the loan is made.

Look at both numbers. Ask whether the rate is fixed for the full term or variable, and identify the finance charges included in the disclosed APR. A lower advertised interest rate does not guarantee the lower-cost loan if the lender deducts a large origination fee or requires paid add-ons.

Read the Personal Loan APR vs. Interest Rate Guide for the distinction between these figures and how upfront fees can affect the APR.

Compare fees and the net amount you receive

Review the full fee schedule. Personal installment loans may include origination or documentation fees, and optional credit or disability insurance may be offered. Late fees and other contract-specific charges can also apply. Ask whether each fee is required, optional, refundable, deducted from the proceeds, added to the loan balance or paid separately.

  • Origination fee: Find the percentage or flat amount and when it is collected.
  • Net proceeds: Confirm the amount that will actually be deposited or sent to creditors.
  • Optional products: Ask whether insurance or add-ons are truly optional and what they cost over the entire term.
  • Late and returned-payment charges: Check the trigger, amount, payment deadline and any grace period.
  • Prepayment terms: Confirm whether early payoff is allowed without a penalty and how extra payments are applied.

For more detail, see the Personal Loan Fees Guide. The CFPB's personal installment loan fee guidance lists common charges and advises consumers to review the required loan disclosures.

Compare the monthly payment and the total amount repaid

The monthly payment tells you how much the loan is scheduled to require from your budget. The total of scheduled payments shows how much you will pay if you make every payment as agreed. Review both, along with the amount of cash you receive.

A longer term often lowers the scheduled monthly payment because repayment is spread over more months. It can also increase total interest because the balance remains outstanding for longer. Do not choose the smallest payment automatically; compare the full repayment cost and decide whether the payment is affordable without stretching the term unnecessarily.

Ask each lender to confirm the number of payments, the first due date, whether there is a final different-sized payment, and whether the quoted payment includes every required charge. If an offer has a variable rate, ask how a rate increase could affect the payment or total cost.

The lesson is not that a no-fee loan is always better. It is that you should compare the same net amount, term and repayment assumptions before deciding.

Check whether prequalification uses a soft or hard credit inquiry

Some lenders let you check an estimated rate or prequalify using a soft inquiry, which does not affect your credit score. A formal application commonly involves a hard inquiry, which may affect your score. The terms “prequalification” and “preapproval” are not used identically by every lender, so do not rely on the label alone.

Before you submit information, ask the lender:

  • Will checking my rate use a soft inquiry or a hard inquiry?
  • At what point will a hard credit inquiry occur?
  • Will applying with a co-borrower or co-signer change the inquiry or eligibility review?
  • Does the quoted APR represent a personalized offer or only a range for qualified applicants?
  • How long will the quoted terms remain available, and what could cause them to change?

When comparing multiple lenders, understand how credit-scoring models treat inquiries for the specific type of loan. The CFPB explains that scoring models may group inquiries for the same type of loan within a reasonably short period, but the exact treatment depends on the scoring model and inquiry circumstances. Rate-shopping protections are commonly described for mortgages, auto loans and student loans; do not assume that multiple personal-loan applications will be grouped. Ask the lender which credit bureau and scoring model may be used, and check the applicable model’s rules. Review the CFPB's explanations of credit inquiries that have no effect and hard and soft inquiries.

Review repayment flexibility and contract terms

Two offers with similar APRs can still differ in important ways. Review the payment due date, accepted payment methods, autopay requirements, grace period if any, fees after a missed or returned payment, and the lender's process for hardship assistance. Ask whether automatic payment is required to receive a rate discount and what happens if you later cancel autopay or change your deposit account.

Check whether the loan allows additional principal payments and early payoff without a fee. If you plan to repay early, ask how the lender calculates a payoff amount and whether any prepayment charge, minimum finance charge or other provision applies. Get the answer in writing rather than relying on a verbal statement.

If the loan is secured, identify the collateral and understand the consequences of default. If a co-signer or co-borrower is required, explain that person’s legal responsibility for repayment and how missed payments may affect their credit.

Evaluate lender reliability—not just the numbers

Confirm the lender's legal name, physical or registered business information, contact channels and licensing or registration status in the state where it operates. Read reviews and complaints as one input, but do not treat a high review score as proof that the offer is suitable. Make sure the written agreement comes from the same company you researched.

Be alert to unsolicited loan texts or messages, guaranteed-approval promises and requests for money in exchange for a promise to release a loan. The FTC warns that advance-fee loan scams may describe the payment as processing, insurance or paperwork. Legitimate lenders may disclose application-related fees, but no legitimate lender can guarantee approval simply because you paid someone first. Read the FTC's advance-fee loan warning.

Debt consolidation: compare more than the new payment

If you are comparing a personal loan to consolidate credit-card balances, calculate the total cost of the new loan and compare it with the cost of repaying the existing cards. Include origination fees, the loan term, the rate after any promotional period on the current debts, and whether the loan proceeds will pay each creditor in full.

A consolidation loan can lower the monthly payment by extending repayment, even when it increases total interest. Also consider what will happen to the paid-off cards. If you continue using them and add new purchases, you could end up with both the consolidation loan and new revolving debt.

Keep making required payments on existing accounts until you confirm the old creditors have received and posted the payoff amounts. For a broader repayment comparison, use the Debt Payoff Calculator.

Frequently asked questions

How many personal loan offers should I compare?

There is no fixed number that guarantees the best offer. Comparing several lenders can help you understand the rates and terms available to you. Start with soft-inquiry prequalification where offered, then compare personalized written offers before deciding.

Should I choose the loan with the lowest interest rate?

Not automatically. Compare APR, net proceeds, fees, total scheduled payments, term, payment affordability and contract conditions. A lower rate can be offset by an origination fee or a longer repayment term.

Is a lower monthly payment always better?

No. It may reflect a longer term and higher total interest. Compare the total of scheduled payments and the amount of cash you receive as well as the monthly amount.

Does prequalification affect my credit score?

It may use a soft inquiry that does not affect your score, but processes differ. Ask the lender whether the rate check is a soft or hard inquiry before submitting the request. A formal application commonly involves a hard inquiry.

Will rate-shopping inquiries count as one?

Credit-scoring models generally recognize comparison shopping for the same type of loan within a limited period, but the exact window and treatment depend on the model and inquiry circumstances. Do not assume every personal-loan application will be combined into one inquiry.

What if an offer has an origination fee?

Calculate the net proceeds after the fee and compare them with the principal and total payments you must make. Ask whether the fee is deducted, financed or paid separately and how it is treated in the disclosed APR.

Should I accept autopay to get a lower rate?

Compare the discount with the conditions and risks. Confirm whether the discount ends if you cancel or miss an automatic payment, and make sure funds will be available on the scheduled debit date.

Can I pay the loan off early?

Check the contract for a prepayment penalty and ask how extra payments are applied. Request a payoff quote before sending the final amount.

What if I am consolidating credit-card debt?

Compare the total cost of the new loan with the balances you plan to repay, including fees and any longer term. Keep making required card payments until each payoff has posted, and avoid building new balances on cards you have paid off.

How do I know a lender is legitimate?

Verify the lender's legal name and state registration or licensing where required. Be wary of unsolicited messages, guaranteed-approval claims and requests for money in exchange for a promised loan. The FTC explains common advance-fee loan scam warning signs.
