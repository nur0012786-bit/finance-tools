Compound Interest Explained: How Your Money Can Grow

Learn how compound interest works, why time matters, and how rate, starting balance and regular contributions affect savings growth.

Educational finance guidance

Compound growth means interest or investment earnings can be added to the balance so that future growth can build on a larger base. The exact result depends on the account and compounding terms.

The basic idea

Compound interest is interest earned on both your original principal and interest that has already been added to the account. For example, if $1,000 earns 5% annually, it grows to $1,050 after year one. In year two, 5% is calculated on $1,050—not just the original $1,000—so the balance becomes $1,102.50. That additional $2.50 is interest earned on the first year's interest. The CFPB describes this as earning interest on savings and on the interest earned along the way. Read the CFPB's compound-interest explanation.

The compound interest formula

For a fixed rate with no additional deposits or withdrawals, the standard formula is:

A = P(1 + r/n)nt

  • A: future balance, including interest
  • P: starting principal
  • r: annual nominal interest rate as a decimal (5% = 0.05)
  • n: number of compounding periods per year
  • t: number of years

This formula assumes the stated rate and compounding schedule remain constant and that no deposits, withdrawals, fees or taxes change the balance. If you make regular contributions, use a formula or calculator designed for recurring deposits and specify whether deposits occur at the beginning or end of each period.

The factors that matter

  • Starting balance: A larger principal generally produces more interest at the same rate and over the same period.
  • Rate: A higher rate increases projected growth, but advertised rates can change and investment returns are not guaranteed.
  • Compounding frequency: With the same nominal rate and other assumptions, more frequent compounding generally produces a slightly higher balance.
  • Time: More periods allow previous interest to participate in later growth.
  • Contributions and withdrawals: Deposits add to the amount that can earn future interest; withdrawals reduce it.
  • Fees and taxes: Costs and taxes can reduce the amount that remains invested or deposited and compounds.

Why time matters

Because growth compounds on previous growth, a longer time horizon can create a larger difference even when the starting balance and rate remain the same.

Regular contributions

Adding money over time can increase the balance that participates in future compounding. Contribution timing can affect the result.

Worked example: $5,000 over 10 years

Assume you deposit $5,000 once, earn a constant 5% nominal annual rate, and leave the money untouched for 10 years. The following estimates use the standard compound-interest formula and exclude taxes, fees and rate changes:

  • Compounded annually: $5,000 × (1.05)10 = about $8,144.47.
  • Compounded monthly: $5,000 × (1 + 0.05/12)120 = about $8,235.05.
  • Difference: Monthly compounding produces about $90.58 more under these assumptions.

The difference is real but modest in this example. Do not choose a financial product based on compounding frequency alone; compare its APY, fees, access restrictions, rate terms and other conditions.

How regular monthly contributions change the result

Now assume you start with $5,000, earn a constant 5% nominal annual rate compounded monthly, and add $100 at the end of every month for 10 years. Under the standard end-of-month contribution model:

  • Initial deposit: $5,000
  • Additional deposits: $100 × 120 = $12,000
  • Total amount you deposit: $17,000
  • Estimated ending balance: $23,763.28
  • Estimated growth above deposits: $6,763.28

This is a mathematical illustration, not a promised account return. It assumes every monthly contribution is made on time, earns the same rate for the remaining period, and is compounded monthly. Beginning-of-month deposits would produce a slightly higher result because each contribution has one extra month to grow.

Interest rate vs. APY: what should savers compare?

The stated interest rate and annual percentage yield (APY) are not always the same. The interest rate is the annual rate paid on the account and does not itself reflect the effect of compounding. APY expresses the annualized yield including compounding under the applicable disclosure assumptions. When comparing U.S. deposit accounts, APY is generally the more useful figure for comparing the effect of compounding, provided you also compare fees, minimum balances, withdrawal restrictions and whether the rate is fixed or variable. Federal deposit-account disclosure rules define how APY is calculated. See Regulation DD, Appendix A: APY calculation.

For example, a 5.00% nominal rate compounded monthly produces an effective annual yield of about 5.12%, before fees and taxes. The actual APY disclosed by a bank may depend on the account's terms and calculation period.

How the Rule of 72 estimates doubling time

A quick mental estimate is the Rule of 72: divide 72 by an annual percentage rate to estimate how many years it may take an amount to double. At a hypothetical constant 6% annual compound rate, 72 ÷ 6 suggests roughly 12 years. This is only an approximation; it becomes less precise at very high or low rates and does not account for taxes, fees, changing rates, contributions or withdrawals. For investments, an assumed rate of return is not guaranteed. Investor.gov explains compound interest and the Rule of 72.

Compounding can work against you on debt

Compound growth is not automatically good. When interest is added to an amount you owe and future interest is calculated on the larger balance, debt can grow faster. Credit cards and other borrowing products may use daily or other contractual interest calculations rather than the simple savings formula shown above. Check the agreement, APR, fees, payment allocation and statement. Is APY the same as the interest rate?

No. The interest rate does not include the effect of compounding, while APY reflects the annualized yield including compounding under the account's disclosure assumptions. Compare APY along with fees and account restrictions.

Does more frequent compounding always make a big difference?

Not necessarily. At the same nominal rate, more frequent compounding increases the mathematical result, but the difference can be small. The rate, time horizon, fees and account terms can matter more.

Are compound-interest calculator results guaranteed?

No. They are estimates based on inputs. A savings account's rate may change, while investment returns fluctuate and can be negative. Taxes, fees and transaction timing can also change actual results.

Can compound interest increase debt?

Yes. When interest is added to an amount owed and subsequent interest is calculated on the larger balance, the amount owed can grow. The exact method depends on the credit agreement and applicable rules.

Should I use the interest rate or APY to compare savings accounts?

APY is generally the better starting point for comparing deposit-account yields because it reflects compounding. Still compare fees, minimum balance requirements, withdrawal rules, introductory rates and whether the rate can change.
