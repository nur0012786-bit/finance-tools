US MORTGAGE GUIDE

How Mortgage Payments Are Calculated

A practical guide to estimating a US mortgage payment, understanding principal and interest, and accounting for taxes, homeowners insurance and mortgage insurance.

First, separate principal and interest from the full housing cost

Your principal-and-interest (P&I) payment repays the mortgage loan. Principal reduces the amount you owe; interest is the lender's charge for lending the money. P&I is only one part of the cost of owning a home.

Your total monthly payment to the mortgage servicer may also include mortgage insurance and escrow deposits for property taxes and homeowners insurance. Homeowners association (HOA) dues are commonly paid separately. Utilities, maintenance and repairs are additional ownership costs, not normally part of the mortgage payment.

This distinction matters: a calculator that reports only P&I is not showing your complete monthly housing budget. The CFPB explains the difference between P&I and the total monthly payment in its mortgage payment guidance.

What is included in a mortgage payment?

  • Principal: the amount borrowed that you repay. Principal payments reduce the outstanding loan balance.
  • Interest: the cost charged by the lender for the money borrowed. It does not directly reduce the loan balance.
  • Property taxes: taxes charged by local or state authorities. They may be collected monthly through escrow or paid directly by the homeowner.
  • Homeowners insurance: the insurance premium for the property. It may be collected through escrow or paid directly to the insurer.
  • Mortgage insurance: an additional charge when required by the loan program or lender. Its type, amount and cancellation rules depend on the loan.

When an escrow account is used, the servicer collects portions of certain tax and insurance bills with your monthly payment and pays those bills when due. If there is no escrow account, you still owe those bills; you pay them separately and need to budget for them. See the CFPB's explanation of PITI and escrow accounts.

How the fixed-rate mortgage payment formula works

For a fully amortizing, fixed-rate mortgage, the scheduled monthly principal-and-interest payment can be estimated with this formula:

M = P × [r(1 + r)n] ÷ [(1 + r)n − 1]

  • M = monthly principal-and-interest payment
  • P = starting loan principal
  • r = monthly interest rate, expressed as a decimal
  • n = total number of monthly payments

To use the formula, divide the annual note interest rate by 12 to get the monthly rate, then multiply the loan term in years by 12 to get the number of payments. For example, a 30-year mortgage has 360 scheduled monthly payments. This formula estimates P&I; it does not automatically add taxes, insurance, mortgage insurance, HOA dues or closing costs.

Worked example: a $300,000 fixed-rate mortgage

Assume a borrower takes a $300,000 fully amortizing mortgage for 30 years at a 6.50% fixed annual interest rate. The monthly rate is 0.065 ÷ 12, and the term contains 360 payments.

  • Estimated monthly P&I: $1,896.20
  • Interest in the first payment: $1,625.00
  • Principal in the first payment: $271.20
  • Estimated balance after the first payment: $299,728.80

In the early years, a larger share of each P&I payment generally goes to interest because the balance is high. As principal is repaid, the interest charged each month falls and a larger share of the same scheduled P&I payment goes toward principal. This process is called amortization. The figures above are an illustration of the formula, not a lender quote; actual payment details can differ because of loan terms, payment timing and rounding. Learn more from the CFPB's guide to how mortgage amortization works.

Why your total payment can change even with a fixed interest rate

With a typical fixed-rate mortgage, the scheduled P&I payment generally stays the same when you make payments as agreed. But the total amount collected each month can still change.

  • Property tax changes: a reassessment, tax-rate change or exemption change can alter the annual bill.
  • Insurance premium changes: a new homeowners insurance premium changes the amount needed for escrow.
  • Escrow analysis: a servicer may adjust the monthly escrow amount after reviewing expected bills and the account balance.
  • Mortgage insurance: the premium can change or end depending on the type of mortgage insurance and applicable loan rules.

The CFPB notes that escrow amounts can change when property taxes or insurance premiums change. Review your mortgage statement and annual escrow analysis rather than assuming that every increase means your fixed interest rate has changed. See why a mortgage payment can change.

Fixed-rate loans, adjustable-rate loans and loan terms

A fixed-rate mortgage generally keeps its scheduled P&I payment level over the loan term. An adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM) can have a payment that changes when its interest rate adjusts. The first ARM payment is calculated using the initial rate; future payments may be recalculated using the adjusted rate and remaining term.

A longer term usually lowers the scheduled monthly P&I payment because repayment is spread over more months. It can also increase the total interest paid if the loan is kept for the full term. A shorter term usually requires a higher monthly P&I payment but repays principal faster. Do not choose a term based only on the lowest payment; consider total interest, cash reserves and how long you expect to keep the loan.

Some loans have nonstandard structures, including balloon payments. A balloon loan can require a large remaining balance at a specified date even when the regular payment is lower than a fully amortizing payment. Check the loan type and disclosures carefully. The CFPB describes fixed-rate, adjustable-rate and balloon-loan payment mechanics in its mortgage payment calculation guide.

How down payment and loan amount affect P&I

A down payment reduces the amount financed when the purchase price and other financing terms are unchanged. For example, a $400,000 home with $80,000 down has a $320,000 starting loan amount before any financed fees or other adjustments. A smaller loan generally means a lower P&I payment at the same interest rate and term.

A larger down payment may also affect whether mortgage insurance is required, but there is no single rule that applies to every loan program. Compare the actual loan options, mortgage insurance costs and cash you would have left after closing.

How to compare mortgage offers correctly

Do not choose a mortgage by comparing only the advertised interest rate or P&I payment. Review each lender's Loan Estimate and compare the loan amount, loan type, term, interest rate, projected total monthly payment, mortgage insurance, upfront costs, lender credits and cash to close.

Also check whether taxes and insurance are included in escrow. Two lenders may show different total monthly payments because their escrow estimates or account arrangements differ, even when the underlying loan terms are similar. Separate lender-controlled charges from property costs that the lender does not control. The CFPB explains what to review in its Loan Estimate guide and loan-offer comparison guide.

Points and lender credits can change the tradeoff between upfront costs and the interest rate. Compare the written offers over the period you reasonably expect to keep the mortgage, rather than assuming the lowest initial payment is automatically the cheapest choice. Mortgage payment FAQ

Is the principal-and-interest payment the full mortgage payment?

No. The total payment sent to the servicer may also include mortgage insurance and escrow deposits for property taxes and homeowners insurance. HOA dues and other homeownership costs are often separate.

Does a mortgage payment always include property taxes and homeowners insurance?

No. They may be collected through an escrow account, but some borrowers pay the tax authority and insurer directly. If they are not in escrow, include them in your personal monthly housing budget.

Can a fixed-rate mortgage payment go up?

The scheduled P&I amount on a typical fixed-rate mortgage generally stays level, but the total amount collected can change when escrow costs or other payment components change.

Why does more of my early payment go to interest?

Interest is calculated on the outstanding balance. The balance is largest near the start of the loan, so the interest portion is initially larger. As the balance falls, the interest portion generally falls and more of the scheduled P&I payment goes to principal.

Does a larger down payment lower the monthly payment?

Generally, yes, when it reduces the amount borrowed and the rate and term stay the same. It may also affect mortgage insurance, depending on the loan program. Keep enough cash for closing costs, reserves and unexpected expenses.

Why is my lender's estimated payment different from this calculator?

A calculator depends on the inputs you provide and may not include every loan-specific fee or payment component. Lenders may use different tax, insurance or mortgage-insurance estimates, and actual loan terms may differ. Use your Loan Estimate and final Closing Disclosure for the transaction-specific figures.
