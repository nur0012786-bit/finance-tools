Fixed vs. Variable Loan Rates: What Is the Difference?

Understand fixed and variable loan rates, how payment changes can work, and what to review before choosing a loan.

Educational finance guidance

A fixed-rate loan generally keeps its stated interest rate unchanged according to the loan terms. A variable-rate loan can change when its contract specifies an adjustment mechanism.

What is a fixed interest rate?

A fixed interest rate is set by the loan agreement and generally does not change during the fixed-rate period. For a conventional fixed-rate installment loan with an unchanged balance schedule, the principal-and-interest payment is predictable. A fixed rate can make budgeting easier because market-rate movements do not directly reset the contract rate during that period.

Fixed does not always mean every part of a bill is guaranteed to remain identical. For example, a mortgage's principal-and-interest payment can stay fixed while property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, or escrow adjustments change the total amount due. Check which part of the payment is actually fixed.

What is a variable interest rate?

A variable interest rate can change over time under a formula or adjustment procedure in the contract. The rate might be tied to a market index or another stated reference rate, plus a lender-set margin. Some products instead use a creditor's published rate or another formula. The agreement should explain what can change, when it can change, and how the new rate is determined.

Variable-rate loans are not all structured alike. Adjustable-rate mortgages (ARMs), variable-rate credit cards, home equity lines of credit, and other credit products can have different indexes, margins, adjustment rules, caps, billing methods, and consumer disclosures. Do not assume a rule from one product applies to another.

Index + margin: how a variable rate can be set

For many adjustable-rate mortgages, the rate after the initial period is based on an index plus a margin, subject to any contractual limits. The index moves with market conditions. The margin is the lender's added percentage amount specified in the agreement and generally does not change after closing. The fully indexed rate is the index plus the margin, before applying any applicable rate caps or floors.

Illustrative calculation: if an ARM uses an index of 3.25% and a margin of 2.75%, the fully indexed rate is 6.00% before applying the contract's caps, floors, and other rules. If the initial rate is a temporary discounted or “teaser” rate, it may be lower than the fully indexed rate even if the index does not move. Check the fully indexed rate and the terms governing the first adjustment—not just the opening rate.

The lender's ARM disclosures should identify the index, margin, adjustment timing, and how the rate and payment are determined. Ask where you can track the index and how the servicer will notify you before an adjustment.

When and how often can a variable rate change?

The initial rate period and the later adjustment frequency are defined by the loan. For example, a 5/1 ARM generally has an initial rate period of five years and then adjusts once each year. A 5/6 ARM generally has a five-year initial period followed by adjustments every six months. Those labels describe common mortgage conventions; the loan documents control the actual schedule.

Other variable-rate products may adjust at different intervals or use a different calculation. Find the first adjustment date, every subsequent adjustment date, the index measurement date, any lookback period, and the payment recalculation rule in the actual agreement.

What are rate caps and floors?

Rate caps limit how much an adjustable rate can rise or fall. Many ARMs describe three separate limits:

  • Initial adjustment cap: limits the change at the first adjustment from the initial rate.
  • Periodic or subsequent adjustment cap: limits the change at each later adjustment.
  • Lifetime cap: limits how far the rate can move above the initial rate over the life of the loan.

A floor is a lower bound on the rate. Some contracts have a minimum rate even when the index falls. A cap is not a promise that the rate will stay low; it only limits changes according to the contract. Ask the lender to show the maximum rate and the payment at that rate, not only the first payment.

Why the payment can rise after a rate adjustment

When a variable rate rises, more of the payment may be needed to cover interest. If the lender recalculates the payment to repay the remaining balance over the remaining term, the required principal-and-interest payment can increase. The size of the change depends on the balance, remaining term, new rate, and adjustment rules.

Some mortgage contracts have payment limits or other features that can cause the payment to change differently from the rate. In certain structures, the payment may not cover all the interest due. Unpaid interest may be added to the principal balance, a feature known as negative amortization. This is product- and contract-specific. Ask whether the balance can grow even while you make the required payment, and under what conditions.

Worked example: a fixed-rate mortgage vs. a 5/1 ARM

Consider a hypothetical $250,000, 30-year mortgage. Offer A has a fixed 6.50% rate for the full term. Offer B is a 5/1 ARM with a 5.50% initial rate for five years, then—solely for this example—adjusts to 7.50% at the first reset. The example assumes monthly principal-and-interest payments, a fully amortizing schedule, no fees, and no other payment changes.

The actual reset rate would depend on the ARM's index, margin, caps, floor, and adjustment date—not on this example.

This is a payment illustration, not a forecast or lender quote. It excludes taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, HOA dues, closing costs, and any fees. The example also assumes the contract permits the stated reset and fully recalculates the payment over the remaining 25 years. A real ARM's caps could prevent that exact change, or its terms could produce a different payment.

How to compare the cost of two offers

Do not choose a variable-rate loan just because its first payment is lower. Compare the total borrowing cost over a realistic holding period and test what happens if the rate rises at the first reset and at later resets. For an ARM, review both the initial rate and the fully indexed rate, then model the highest payment allowed by the caps.

  1. Compare APR and fees. Review the annual percentage rate, points, origination charges, and other costs shown in the disclosures. APR is a comparison measure, not a replacement for reading the full terms.
  2. Compare payments over time. Record the first payment, payment after the first adjustment, and the highest potential payment.
  3. Check the index and margin. Confirm the exact index, source, margin, and how the index is measured.
  4. Read the caps and floor. Note the initial, periodic, and lifetime limits, and whether a floor applies.
  5. Check the adjustment calendar. Record the first reset date, later frequency, and when the servicer will send notices.
  6. Stress-test your budget. Ask whether you could afford the maximum permitted payment if income fell or other costs increased.
  7. Do not rely on refinancing as a guarantee. You may not qualify or receive favorable terms later. Consider whether you can keep the loan if refinancing or selling becomes impractical.

When could a fixed rate be a better fit?

A fixed rate may fit borrowers who value payment predictability, expect to keep the loan for a long time, or would struggle to absorb a higher payment. It can reduce exposure to market-rate increases during the fixed period. That certainty may be worth comparing against a higher starting rate or other costs.

When could a variable rate be worth considering?

A variable rate may be worth comparing when the initial rate and total costs are attractive, you understand the reset formula and limits, and your budget can tolerate a higher payment. A shorter expected holding period can be relevant, but it does not eliminate risk: a move, sale, or refinance can be delayed, and future refinancing terms are not guaranteed.

The right comparison depends on the loan product, actual disclosures, your cash flow, the length of time you expect to keep the debt, and your ability to manage payment increases. Neither rate type is automatically cheaper.

Questions to ask the lender before signing

  • Is the rate fixed for the full term, or only for an initial period?
  • What index and margin determine future rates, and where can I track the index?
  • When is the first adjustment, and how often can the rate change after that?
  • What are the initial, periodic, and lifetime caps and any rate floor?
  • What would my payment be at the maximum rate permitted by the contract?
  • Can the required payment ever be less than the interest due or allow the balance to grow?
  • How and when will I receive notice before a rate or payment adjustment?
  • Are there points, origination fees, prepayment penalties, or other costs?

Frequently asked questions

Is a fixed rate always better than a variable rate?

No. A fixed rate offers more rate certainty during its fixed period, while a variable rate may begin lower or move down if its reference rate falls. Compare the full terms, possible future payments, fees, and your ability to handle an increase.

Can a variable rate go down as well as up?

It can if the contract's index or formula moves down and the loan's rules allow the rate to decrease. A floor or other limitation may prevent the rate from falling below a stated level.

Is the initial ARM rate the rate I will pay for the whole mortgage?

Not necessarily. An ARM may have an initial rate period followed by adjustments. The later rate is determined by the contract's index, margin, caps, floor, and adjustment schedule.

What does a 5/1 ARM mean?

It commonly describes an adjustable-rate mortgage with an initial fixed-rate period of five years followed by annual adjustments. Confirm the exact first adjustment date and later schedule in the loan documents.

Do rate caps prevent my payment from increasing?

No. Rate caps limit interest-rate changes under the contract; they do not necessarily keep the payment unchanged. A rate increase within the cap can still raise the required payment.

Can I count on refinancing before my variable rate adjusts?

No. Future approval, home value, income, credit, rates, closing costs, and lender requirements are uncertain. Choose a loan you can manage even if refinancing is unavailable or unattractive.
