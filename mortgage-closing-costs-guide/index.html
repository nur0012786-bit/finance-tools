Mortgage Closing Costs Explained

Closing costs are the expenses involved in completing a home purchase or mortgage transaction. They are separate from the down payment and can include lender charges, title and settlement services, government charges, and prepaid or escrow items.

THE BASICS

What are mortgage closing costs?

Closing costs are charges and expenses associated with making and completing a mortgage or real-estate transaction. The exact items vary by loan, property, lender, location, and transaction structure.

Some costs are connected to the mortgage itself, while others are connected to transferring, documenting, protecting, or preparing the property transaction. Some amounts may also be collected in advance for future expenses such as property taxes or homeowners insurance.

Common categories of closing costs

1. Lender and loan costs

These can include origination charges, underwriting or processing charges and other fees for making the loan. Discount points are optional upfront charges connected to a lower interest rate. One point is generally 1% of the loan amount, but the rate reduction for each point depends on the lender, loan and market. Ask for a comparison with zero points before paying them.

2. Third-party services

These can include an appraisal, credit report, title search, lender's title insurance, settlement or closing service, survey and other required services. On the Loan Estimate, some services are listed as ones you cannot shop for and others as services you can shop for. Read the lender's written provider list and compare quotes for services you are allowed to choose.

3. Government and recording charges

These can include recording fees for the deed or mortgage, transfer taxes and other local or state charges. Amounts and names differ by location. Some charges are set by government authorities and may not change when you choose a different lender.

4. Prepaid expenses

Prepaids are amounts paid at closing for expenses that come due shortly after or cover a period after closing. Examples can include daily interest from the closing date to the end of the month and an advance homeowners insurance premium. These are not all lender fees; they are costs the borrower would otherwise need to pay later.

5. Initial escrow deposits

If your mortgage uses an escrow account, the lender or servicer may collect an initial balance for upcoming property-tax and insurance bills. This is money set aside for future bills, not an additional lender origination fee. Ask how many months are being collected and how the escrow amount was calculated.

6. Other transaction expenses

Depending on the property and contract, buyers may also pay for inspections, surveys, HOA transfer or setup charges, association documents, municipal certificates, courier services or other items. Some buyer expenses, such as a home inspection, may be paid before closing and may not appear as a standard Loan Estimate closing-cost line.

The Loan Estimate groups many charges into Loan Costs and Other Costs. Its itemized categories help you see which amounts relate to the loan, services, government fees, prepaids and initial escrow. The CFPB's Loan Estimate guide explains the form.

Closing costs vs. down payment

The down payment is the part of the purchase price paid by the buyer instead of borrowed from the lender. Closing costs are the charges and prepaid amounts associated with arranging the mortgage and completing the transaction. The down payment is not included in the Loan Estimate's “Total Closing Costs” figure.

For example, on a $400,000 home with an $80,000 down payment, the base mortgage before any financed costs is $320,000. If closing costs and prepaids total $12,900, those costs are separate from the $80,000 down payment. Deposits, credits, amounts already paid and any eligible financed costs then affect the final cash-to-close calculation.

What does “cash to close” mean?

Cash to close is the amount you need to bring to the closing table after the down payment, closing costs, prepaid items, deposits already paid, seller or lender credits, and transaction adjustments are accounted for. It is not the same as total closing costs, because it also reflects the down payment and money already paid or credited.

A simplified planning calculation is:

Cash to close ≈ down payment + closing costs and applicable adjustments − deposits already paid − credits − costs already paid or financed.

Your lender's Loan Estimate and final Closing Disclosure show the transaction-specific calculation. Items such as tax prorations, seller-paid expenses, earnest money, lender credits and financed costs can change the final amount. Ask the lender or settlement agent to explain any item you do not recognize.

Worked cash-to-close example

Use this illustrative scenario:

  • Home price: $400,000
  • Down payment: $80,000
  • Loan costs: $5,700
  • Government fees and other services: $2,300
  • Prepaid expenses and initial escrow: $4,900
  • Earnest money deposit already paid: $5,000
  • Seller credits and lender credits: $0

Total estimated closing costs are $12,900 ($5,700 + $2,300 + $4,900). Cash to close in this simplified example is $87,900 ($80,000 down payment + $12,900 costs − $5,000 deposit). This example excludes other adjustments and assumes no costs were paid separately or financed in the loan.

Seller credits and lender credits

Seller credits are amounts the seller agrees to contribute toward eligible buyer costs under the purchase contract and loan-program rules. The agreed credit should appear correctly on the Closing Disclosure. Seller contributions are not automatically available for the down payment or cash back to the buyer.

For Fannie Mae conventional loans, interested-party contribution limits depend on occupancy and loan-to-value ratio. Contributions generally may cover eligible closing costs and prepaids, but cannot be used to fund the down payment or required reserves. Amounts above the permitted limit or above the borrower's eligible costs may be treated as a sales concession and affect underwriting. FHA, VA, USDA, jumbo and other loan programs can have different rules; ask your lender before negotiating a credit.

Lender credits reduce eligible upfront closing costs. When connected to mortgage pricing, they generally come in exchange for a higher interest rate. That can lower cash due today while increasing the monthly payment and borrowing cost over time. Compare written offers with and without credits over the time you expect to keep the loan rather than judging the credit amount alone.

Discount points work in the opposite direction: you pay more at closing for a lower rate. One point is generally 1% of the loan amount. The amount by which a point changes the rate varies by lender and market. Compare the loan with points, without points and with lender credits using the same loan amount and term.

Sources: Fannie Mae: Interested Party Contributions and CFPB: how to use lender credits and points.

How to review your Loan Estimate

The Loan Estimate summarizes the proposed loan terms, projected payments and estimated closing costs. For most covered mortgage applications, the lender must provide it within three business days after receiving the six required application items. It is an estimate, not a final bill or loan approval.

Compare Loan Estimates from different lenders using the same loan amount, product, term, down payment and points or credits. Check the interest rate and whether it is locked, the monthly payment, origination charges, services you can shop for, government fees, prepaids, initial escrow and estimated cash to close. Ask the lender to explain any charge or assumption that is unclear.

Some fees are subject to limits on how much they can increase between the Loan Estimate and closing; other costs, such as certain prepaids or services you choose outside the lender's written provider list, can change more freely. Changes to the application or other qualifying circumstances can also affect the estimates. Do not assume every fee may increase without limit. Ask for an explanation and any revised Loan Estimate when costs change.

How to review your Closing Disclosure

The Closing Disclosure lists the final loan terms, final closing costs, who pays each amount and the cash to close. For most covered transactions, you must receive it at least three business days before closing. Use that time to compare it with the most recent Loan Estimate and resolve questions before signing.

  • Confirm your name, property address, loan amount, loan type, term and interest rate.
  • Check whether the rate is locked and whether the rate and points match what you agreed to.
  • Compare loan costs, other costs, prepaids, escrow deposits and lender credits with the latest Loan Estimate.
  • Confirm seller credits and the earnest money or other deposits already paid are shown correctly.
  • Review the final cash-to-close figure and ask why it differs from the earlier estimate.
  • Check whether taxes, insurance or assessments are escrowed or must be paid separately.

If a cost or credit changed, ask for the reason and where it appears in the disclosure. Certain changes trigger a new three-business-day waiting period, but not every corrected disclosure does. Get answers before signing rather than assuming an unexplained difference is harmless.

Questions to ask before closing

  • Which charges are lender fees and which are third-party services?
  • Which services can I shop for, and does the lender have a written provider list?
  • Which amounts are prepaid or going into an escrow account, and how were they calculated?
  • Have all agreed seller credits, lender credits and deposits been applied correctly?
  • Are discount points included, and how much does the rate change with or without them?
  • What is the final cash-to-close amount, and how does it differ from the Loan Estimate?
  • Which changed costs are subject to federal tolerance limits, and is there a valid reason for any increase?
  • Are there local taxes, recording fees, HOA charges or other property-specific costs?
  • How should I deliver closing funds, and how can I independently verify the instructions?

Frequently asked questions

Are closing costs the same for every home buyer?

No. Costs can vary by lender, loan program, property, location, services used, and the terms negotiated in the transaction.

Can closing costs be rolled into a mortgage?

Whether a cost can be financed depends on the loan program, transaction type, loan-to-value limits and lender requirements. Some refinance transactions may include eligible costs in the new loan amount. For a home purchase, do not assume every closing cost can be added to the mortgage; many costs must be paid at or before closing. Ask the lender to identify exactly which amounts are financed and how the higher loan balance affects payment and total interest.

Are prepaid taxes and insurance a closing cost?

They can appear among the amounts due at closing, but prepaid and escrow amounts serve a different purpose from many lender or third-party fees. Review the detailed disclosure to see how each amount is categorized.

Does a larger down payment eliminate closing costs?

No. A larger down payment reduces the amount financed, but it does not eliminate lender charges, third-party services, government fees, prepaids or escrow deposits. It can also change the loan-to-value ratio and, depending on the loan, mortgage-insurance requirements.
