What Are Mortgage Points?

UNDERSTAND THE BASICS

What are mortgage discount points?

Mortgage discount points are an upfront charge you pay at closing in exchange for a lower interest rate than the lender would offer on an otherwise comparable loan without points. One discount point generally equals 1% of the loan amount. On a $300,000 mortgage, one point costs $3,000; half a point costs $1,500. The rate reduction is not fixed: it depends on the lender, loan type, market conditions and the pricing available to you.

Be precise about the word points. Some lenders use it loosely for percentage-based origination charges or other fees that do not reduce the rate. Ask the lender to identify which charges are actual discount points and show the rate offered with zero points, with points and with any lender credits.

Discount points, origination charges and lender credits

  • Discount points: upfront prepaid interest tied to a lower mortgage rate.
  • Origination charges: lender fees for processing or making the loan. A percentage-based fee is not automatically a discount point.
  • Lender credits: the reverse trade-off. You accept a higher interest rate in exchange for the lender covering some eligible closing costs.
  • Seller-paid points: the seller may agree to pay eligible points as part of the transaction, subject to the purchase contract, loan rules and applicable limits.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains that points and lender credits exchange upfront cost for interest-rate cost over time. It also notes that the amount by which a point changes the rate varies by lender and market. Review the CFPB guide to points and lender credits.

How to calculate the break-even period

A basic break-even calculation divides the additional upfront cost of the points by the monthly principal-and-interest savings:

Break-even months = additional points cost ÷ monthly payment savings

This is a first-pass comparison, not a complete financial analysis. It assumes you keep the loan long enough to receive the monthly savings and does not account for the time value of money, taxes, changes in payment structure or other differences between offers.

Worked example: are one point worth $3,000?

Assume two hypothetical 30-year fixed-rate offers for the same $300,000 loan. The zero-point offer has a 6.75% rate. The offer with one discount point costs $3,000 at closing and has a 6.50% rate. These rates are illustrative only; a lender may quote a different rate reduction.

Zero points6.75%$0$1,945.79
One point6.50%$3,000$1,896.20

The lower-rate offer saves about $49.59 per month in scheduled principal and interest. Dividing $3,000 by $49.59 gives a simple break-even point of approximately 61 months, or just over five years. If you sell or refinance before then, the monthly savings may not recover the upfront points cost. If you keep the loan longer, the points may produce net savings under these assumptions.

This comparison holds the loan amount and term constant and excludes property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, HOA dues, tax effects and the opportunity cost of using $3,000 at closing. It is not a quote or a prediction of future rates.

When paying points may or may not make sense

Points may be worth comparing when you expect to keep the same mortgage beyond its break-even period, have enough cash for closing and reserves, and receive a meaningful rate reduction. They may be less attractive if you expect to move, refinance, sell or pay off the mortgage before reaching break-even, or if paying points would leave you short of emergency savings.

Do not choose based on the monthly payment alone. Compare the same loan amount, loan type, term, rate-lock period and points or credits across lenders. A lender with a higher starting rate may still offer a better overall deal after fees, so request comparable Loan Estimates.

How to compare lender offers

  1. Request a written quote with zero points and a separate quote showing the points option.
  2. Ask how many basis points the rate changes for each point and whether the points are optional or required for the quoted offer.
  3. Compare the rate, APR, monthly principal-and-interest payment, total lender fees and cash to close.
  4. Calculate the break-even period and compare it with realistic plans for keeping the home and mortgage.
  5. Repeat the comparison for lender credits if lower upfront costs matter more than a lower monthly payment.
  6. Review the points and lender-credit entries on the Loan Estimate and Closing Disclosure, and ask the lender to explain any unclear line item.

Are mortgage points tax deductible?

Do not assume every fee called a point is deductible or that the full amount can be deducted in the year paid. IRS rules depend on factors such as the loan purpose, the home securing the mortgage, how the points are charged and other requirements. Points on a refinance generally receive different treatment from qualifying points on a purchase of a main home. Read the current IRS Publication 936 and consult a qualified tax professional about your circumstances.

Frequently asked questions

Does one mortgage point always lower the rate by 0.25%?

No. One point is generally 1% of the loan amount, but the rate reduction depends on the lender, loan product and market pricing. Ask for written options rather than assuming a standard reduction.

Are discount points the same as origination fees?

No. Discount points are tied to a lower interest rate. Origination fees pay for lender services and do not necessarily reduce the rate. Ask the lender to identify each charge clearly.

Can I pay points with seller credits?

Sometimes. The purchase contract, lender rules and applicable seller-contribution limits determine how seller funds may be used. Confirm the permitted use with the lender before relying on it.

What if I refinance before reaching break-even?

You may not recover the upfront cost through monthly payment savings. Compare the expected time in the loan with the break-even period before paying points.

Where do points appear in mortgage disclosures?

Review the Loan Estimate and Closing Disclosure and ask the lender to explain the points and lender-credit entries. The CFPB guide explains where these items appear and how they affect the rate and closing costs.
