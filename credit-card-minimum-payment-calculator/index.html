How this credit card minimum payment calculator works

Enter your current balance, purchase APR, the minimum-payment formula closest to your card agreement, a percentage and minimum-dollar floor, and a fixed monthly payment to compare. The calculator estimates payoff time and interest using a simplified monthly-interest model.

It does not know your issuer's exact minimum-payment formula. Your current statement and cardmember agreement control the amount due. If your account has different balance categories—such as purchases, balance transfers and cash advances—each may have its own APR or special repayment terms.

How issuers commonly calculate minimum payments

Minimum-payment formulas vary among issuers and even among products from the same issuer. The CFPB's December 2025 Consumer Credit Card Market Report found that, among agreements it reviewed, fixed minimum-dollar floors ranged from $15 to $50, with $40 the most common. Most issuers set a percentage around 1% of the balance and add finance charges, fees and past-due amounts separately.

That is a market observation, not a universal rule. Your card may use a different percentage, floor, fee treatment, past-due amount or separate formula for promotional balances. Look for the section titled "How we calculate your minimum payment" in the cardmember agreement and compare it with your current statement.

The two formula models in this calculator

  • Percentage of balance plus cycle interest: The model calculates the chosen percentage of the starting balance, adds estimated interest for the cycle, and compares that result with the fixed dollar floor. This approximates a common issuer approach. Fees and past-due amounts are not added by this calculator.
  • Percentage of statement balance: The model first adds estimated cycle interest to the starting balance, then applies the chosen percentage to that modeled statement balance. The fixed dollar floor still applies.

For either model, the payment is capped at the modeled statement balance. Change the percentage and floor to approximate your own card's formula. If a selected formula does not cover the estimated interest, the calculator will show an error rather than inventing a payoff date.

Why minimum payments can keep debt around for years

When the required payment declines as the balance declines, the amount left to reduce principal can become small. Interest and fees may consume a large share of the payment, so the balance falls slowly. A fixed payment above the minimum can generally speed repayment because more money is available to reduce the balance each cycle.

The CFPB's 2025 report gives an example of a $2,000 balance at 29% APR with a minimum payment equal to the greater of accrued interest plus 1% of the balance or $35. Its analysis estimates more than $3,000 in interest over almost ten years. A monthly-interest approximation produces a similar illustrative result, but it does not reproduce an issuer's exact daily statement calculations.

How the 36-month benchmark helps

The calculator also estimates the fixed monthly payment needed to repay the starting balance in 36 months under the same monthly-rate assumption. This can help you compare a realistic planned payment with a three-year payoff target.

For covered open-end, non-home-secured credit card accounts, Regulation Z generally requires periodic statements to include a minimum-payment warning and an estimate of the time and total cost to repay under minimum payments. In specified circumstances, issuers must also disclose a payment amount that would repay the balance in 36 months, together with related cost and savings estimates. Exceptions and special treatment apply, including where minimum payments do not amortize the balance.

This website's 36-month figure is an independent estimate, not the issuer's required disclosure. Issuers must use the applicable account formula and regulatory calculation method in Regulation Z and Appendix M1.

Why actual credit card interest can differ

Many issuers calculate interest daily, often using an average daily balance and a daily periodic rate. This calculator uses a simplified monthly approximation of APR divided by 12. Actual statement interest can differ because of the number of days in the billing cycle, payment and transaction dates, daily compounding, multiple APR categories and grace-period rules.

The model assumes no new purchases, balance transfers, cash advances, promotional-rate changes, deferred-interest balances, late fees, past-due amounts or recurring fees. If you continue to use the card, the actual payoff period may be longer. A single APR also cannot precisely represent an account with multiple balance categories.

How payments are allocated across balances

If you carry balances at different APRs, payment allocation can affect how quickly high-rate debt is reduced. Under federal rules, the amount paid above the required minimum is generally applied to the balance with the highest APR first. The issuer generally has more discretion over how the minimum-payment portion is allocated, subject to special rules for deferred-interest balances.

Check your statement for each balance category and its APR. Paying more than the minimum can help, but understand how your issuer applies the extra amount and which balance will receive it.

What to do if you can only afford the minimum

Make at least the required minimum by the due date to avoid delinquency and possible late fees. Then review the balance categories, APRs, charges and spending that are keeping the balance high. Contact your issuer before missing a payment and ask whether it offers a hardship plan, a reduced-rate option or another repayment arrangement.

If you are struggling with multiple debts, consider contacting a reputable nonprofit credit counselor. Be cautious of companies that guarantee to erase debt, tell you to stop communicating with creditors, or demand large upfront fees. Debt settlement programs can create credit, tax, collection and legal consequences.

Frequently asked questions

Does every credit card use a 1% plus interest formula?

No. Issuer methods vary. Some apply a percentage of the balance and add finance charges separately; others use different formulas or floors. Your card agreement and current statement show the method for your account.

Why is the calculator's default minimum floor $40?

The CFPB's 2025 market report found that $40 was the most common fixed floor among the issuer agreements reviewed, with floors ranging from $15 to $50. Your card may use a different amount, so change the input to match your agreement.

What if the modeled minimum does not cover monthly interest?

The modeled balance will not decline. The calculator will show an error rather than a payoff date. Check your card's exact formula, APR categories and balance before relying on the estimate.

Does the calculator include late fees or past-due amounts?

No. It models the starting balance and selected formula without adding fees, amounts past due or new charges each cycle. Your actual minimum due may include these amounts.

Does the calculator use an average daily balance?

No. It approximates interest monthly as APR divided by 12. Many issuers calculate interest daily, so the result can differ from your statement.

Does the 36-month payment match my statement?

Not necessarily. This calculator uses a simplified monthly model. Issuers must use the applicable account formula and regulatory assumptions for their own repayment disclosures.

Should I stop using my credit card while paying it off?

New purchases add to the balance and can extend repayment. This calculator assumes no new charges, so using the card during repayment makes the estimate less reliable.

Should I pay the highest-interest balance first?

Generally, reducing the highest-APR balance can reduce interest costs. Federal rules generally apply the amount paid above the minimum to the highest-APR balance first, subject to specific exceptions.
