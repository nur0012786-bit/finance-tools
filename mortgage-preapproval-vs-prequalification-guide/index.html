Mortgage Preapproval vs. Prequalification

Learn how mortgage prequalification and preapproval differ, what information lenders may review, and why neither is the same as final loan approval.

UNDERSTAND THE BASICS

What is mortgage prequalification?

Prequalification is an early lender assessment of whether you may qualify for a mortgage and the amount you might be able to borrow. Some lenders base it mainly on information you report about your income, debts, assets and credit. Other lenders may use a more detailed review. The word itself does not tell you exactly what the lender checked.

Prequalification can help you begin understanding your possible price range, but it is not a guaranteed loan offer or final approval.

What is mortgage preapproval?

Preapproval is a lender's tentative indication that it may be willing to lend up to a specified amount, based on assumptions and the information reviewed so far. A lender may verify more of your income, assets, debts and credit before issuing the letter, but lenders do not all use the term or process in the same way.

A preapproval letter can help demonstrate to a seller that you have spoken with a lender and appear likely to obtain financing. It does not guarantee the loan will close. The lender still needs to review the property, appraisal or valuation, title, final documents and any changes to your financial situation.

The CFPB emphasizes that lenders use the words “prequalification” and “preapproval” differently. Focus on what the lender actually reviewed, verified and still needs—not just the heading on the letter. Read the CFPB explanation of prequalification versus preapproval.

What information might a lender request?

Requirements depend on your income, assets, debts, loan program and lender. A lender may ask for items such as:

  • Identity and application details: legal name, contact information, Social Security number and property or purchase information when available.
  • Employment and income: recent pay statements, W-2 forms, tax returns, employer information or other documentation for the income sources you report.
  • Self-employment or variable income: business records, tax returns, profit-and-loss information or documentation needed to understand commissions, bonuses, overtime or other variable income.
  • Assets and funds: bank, investment or retirement account statements to document money available for a down payment, closing costs and reserves.
  • Debts and obligations: information about auto loans, student loans, credit cards, personal loans, leases and other recurring obligations.
  • Additional explanations: documentation for large deposits, transfers, gifts or other financial items that need clarification.

This is an illustrative list, not a universal document checklist. Ask for the lender's exact requirements and send documents through its approved secure channel. Do not alter a statement or omit a liability to make the application appear stronger.

Will getting preapproved affect your credit?

A lender may check your credit when evaluating a prequalification or preapproval request. The type of inquiry and its effect can vary by lender and process. Before consenting, ask whether the lender plans to use a soft inquiry or a hard credit inquiry, when it will happen, and whether it is required for the letter.

If you are comparing several lenders, tell them you are shopping for a mortgage and ask how to coordinate credit checks. Do not assume every lender uses the same process or that an inquiry has no effect. Review your credit report for errors before applying and avoid unnecessary new credit while preparing for a mortgage.

Source: CFPB guidance on lender credit checks and preapproval letters.

How long does a preapproval letter last?

A preapproval letter often has an expiration date. The CFPB says many letters are typically valid for 30 to 60 days, but the lender sets the period and its own renewal requirements. Confirm the exact date printed on your letter.

If the letter expires before you find a property, ask the lender what it needs to update the letter. It may request new pay statements, bank statements, credit information or other updated documents. A renewal may require another credit review. Do not assume an expired letter remains acceptable to a seller.

Source: CFPB: Get a preapproval letter.

What a preapproval letter should clarify

Read the letter and ask the lender to explain any missing or unclear information. Depending on the lender's format, check:

  • The borrower name or names covered by the letter.
  • The maximum loan amount or purchase price and the assumptions used.
  • The intended loan type, term, down payment or other conditions, if stated.
  • The date issued and expiration date.
  • Whether income, assets, debts and credit were verified or were based on information you reported.
  • Any conditions that remain, including the need for a suitable property, appraisal, title review or updated documents.

A seller or real estate agent may have their own expectations for the form of letter they will accept. Ask your agent what is customary in your local market, but do not let a seller's preferred letter wording replace your own review of the lender's assumptions and conditions.

Preapproval is not final mortgage approval

After you make an offer and the lender has a property address and purchase contract, the loan moves into a fuller application and underwriting process. The lender may need to:

  • Verify current income, employment, assets, debts and funds for closing.
  • Review the purchase contract, property eligibility and required occupancy information.
  • Obtain an appraisal or another permitted property valuation.
  • Complete title, insurance and other transaction reviews.
  • Resolve underwriting conditions and provide final disclosures.

Material changes can affect the decision. A new debt, reduced income, a change in employment, unexplained asset movement, a different loan amount or a property issue may require additional review. Tell the lender about changes instead of assuming the original letter settles the matter.

For the later steps, see the Mortgage Approval Timeline Guide and Closing Disclosure Guide.

Should you get preapproved before shopping for a home?

It can be useful to speak with lenders before making offers. A preapproval may help identify documentation issues early and give you a lender's tentative view of how much it may lend. Sellers in many markets may ask for a preapproval letter before considering an offer.

Timing matters. The letter may expire, and the lender may check your credit. The CFPB suggests considering a preapproval when you are ready to shop seriously, while also noting that getting one earlier may help identify issues you have time to address. Ask how long the letter lasts and whether an updated review will be needed.

Do not confuse preapproval with choosing a lender

Getting preapproved does not usually commit you to using that lender for the final mortgage. The CFPB recommends comparing official Loan Estimates after you have a property and are ready to proceed with a specific loan. That is when you can compare the actual loan amount, interest rate, monthly payment, closing costs, lender credits and other terms for the transaction.

Ask more than one lender about its process, but compare the eventual Loan Estimates on the same assumptions. A higher preapproval amount or a faster letter is not proof that a lender offers the lowest-cost or most suitable mortgage.

Source: CFPB: Get a preapproval letter.

How much should you borrow?

The amount a lender is tentatively willing to lend is not a recommendation to spend that amount. Your own housing budget should account for take-home income, existing debts, property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, HOA dues, utilities, maintenance, savings and unexpected expenses.

Use the Mortgage Affordability Calculator to test a conservative monthly budget, and the Mortgage Calculator to estimate payments for a specific loan scenario. These tools are for planning; they do not verify income, credit, assets or lender eligibility.

Questions to ask each lender

  • What do you mean by prequalification and preapproval, and what information will you verify?
  • Will you check my credit? Is it a soft or hard inquiry, and when will it happen?
  • What documents do you need for my income type and assets?
  • What assumptions did you use for the loan amount, interest rate, down payment, taxes and insurance?
  • What conditions remain before final approval?
  • How long is the letter valid, and what is required to renew it?
  • Will you need updated documents or another credit review after I make an offer?
  • Does this letter commit me to use your company for the final mortgage?
  • When can I receive an official Loan Estimate for a specific property and loan?

Frequently asked questions

Is preapproval better than prequalification?

Often, a more fully reviewed preapproval gives a seller more information than an estimate based only on self-reported details. But lenders use the terms differently. Ask exactly what was reviewed and verified rather than relying on the label.

Does preapproval guarantee I will get the mortgage?

No. It is a tentative assessment based on assumptions and information reviewed so far. Final approval can depend on updated documents, underwriting, the property, appraisal or valuation, title, insurance and other conditions.

Can I get preapproved before I find a house?

Yes. A lender can review your financial information and issue a letter based on a hypothetical purchase and stated assumptions. The property and final loan terms still need to be reviewed later.

How long is a mortgage preapproval valid?

Many letters are typically valid for 30 to 60 days, but the lender sets the expiration date and renewal process. Check the date printed on your letter and ask what updates are required.

Does a preapproval require a hard credit check?

Not always, and lender processes vary. Some lenders use a soft inquiry at an early stage; others may require a hard inquiry. Ask which type will be used before you authorize the credit review.

Do I have to use the lender that preapproved me?

Getting a preapproval does not generally require you to use that lender for the final mortgage. Compare official Loan Estimates from lenders after you have a property and are ready to proceed.

Can a lender change or withdraw a preapproval?

A letter is based on assumptions and the information available at the time. Material financial changes, new debts, property issues or failure to satisfy conditions can change the lender's assessment. Ask the lender what changes must be reported.

Does the highest preapproval amount mean I can afford that house?

No. The lender's calculation does not fully reflect every household expense, savings goal or personal priority. Choose a payment that leaves room for normal expenses, repairs, emergencies and saving.
