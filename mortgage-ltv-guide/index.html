What Is Loan-to-Value Ratio (LTV)?

Learn what mortgage loan-to-value ratio means, how to calculate LTV, and why it can affect mortgage insurance and loan terms.

UNDERSTAND THE BASICS

What is loan-to-value ratio?

Loan-to-value ratio (LTV) compares the amount financed for a property with the property's value used by the lender. Lenders use LTV as one factor when evaluating mortgage risk, eligibility, pricing and whether mortgage insurance is required. A higher LTV means the loan represents a larger share of the property's value; a lower LTV means the borrower has more equity at the start.

The simple formula is:

LTV = loan amount ÷ property value × 100

For a $400,000 property and a $320,000 mortgage, the simplified LTV is 80%. The remaining 20% is the borrower's equity contribution in this simplified purchase example, before closing costs and other adjustments.

Which property value does the lender use?

Do not assume the lender always divides by the purchase price or by an online estimate of current market value. The value used depends on the transaction, loan program and underwriting rules.

  • Purchase: Under Fannie Mae's standard conventional guidance, property value is generally the lower of the sales price or current appraised value.
  • Refinance: Fannie Mae's standard LTV calculation generally uses the current appraised value.
  • Financed mortgage insurance: Fannie Mae specifies a separate calculation in which the original loan amount plus the financed mortgage-insurance amount is divided by the applicable property value.
  • Other loan programs: FHA, VA, USDA, jumbo and portfolio loans can have distinct definitions and requirements. Confirm the rule that applies to your specific loan.

Example: You agree to buy a home for $400,000, but the appraisal is $390,000. If the mortgage amount is $312,000, dividing by the $400,000 price gives 78%. However, using the lower $390,000 value gives an 80% LTV. For the standard Fannie Mae purchase calculation, the lower value is the relevant denominator.

LTV, CLTV and HCLTV: what is the difference?

These are related but different measures. They matter when a property has more than one loan or a home equity line of credit (HELOC) secured by it.

  • LTV (loan-to-value): Measures the first mortgage against the property's value under the applicable calculation rules.
  • CLTV (combined loan-to-value): Includes the first mortgage and other subordinate financing. Under Fannie Mae's standard calculation, this includes the original first-mortgage amount, the amount drawn on a HELOC, and unpaid balances on other closed-end subordinate loans.
  • HCLTV (home equity combined loan-to-value): Used when a HELOC is involved. Under Fannie Mae's standard calculation, it includes the original first-mortgage amount, the full HELOC credit line whether drawn or undrawn, and unpaid balances on other closed-end subordinate financing.

For these Fannie Mae calculations, the denominator is generally the lower of the sales price or appraised value. Other loan products may calculate ratios differently.

Worked example: LTV vs. CLTV vs. HCLTV

Assume a $400,000 property, an original first mortgage of $320,000, a HELOC with $20,000 drawn from a $40,000 credit line, and a separate closed-end second mortgage with a $10,000 unpaid balance.

  • LTV: $320,000 ÷ $400,000 = 80%.
  • CLTV: ($320,000 + $20,000 + $10,000) ÷ $400,000 = 87.5%.
  • HCLTV: ($320,000 + $40,000 + $10,000) ÷ $400,000 = 92.5%.

The difference is the HELOC treatment: CLTV counts the amount drawn; HCLTV counts the full available line. This example follows the described Fannie Mae definitions and is not a mortgage approval or a statement of every lender's policy.

How does a down payment affect LTV?

For a standard purchase example where the loan amount equals the purchase price minus the down payment, a larger down payment lowers the starting LTV:

  • 5% down payment = 95% LTV.
  • 10% down payment = 90% LTV.
  • 15% down payment = 85% LTV.
  • 20% down payment = 80% LTV.
  • 25% down payment = 75% LTV.

These percentages are arithmetic illustrations, not universal minimum down payments or loan-program rules. Closing costs, financed fees, subordinate financing and program-specific calculations can change the final figures.

Why LTV can affect mortgage costs

LTV is one of several factors lenders may use when deciding whether to lend, setting pricing or determining mortgage insurance requirements. A higher LTV can mean greater perceived collateral risk and may lead to a higher rate, additional pricing adjustments, a smaller range of available loan options or mortgage insurance. The impact depends on the loan program, credit profile, property, occupancy, loan amount and other underwriting factors.

For many conventional mortgages, a down payment below 20% can mean private mortgage insurance (PMI), but 20% is not a universal rule across all loan types. Government-backed mortgage insurance has different program rules and costs. Compare the full Loan Estimate, including rate, points, lender fees and mortgage-insurance charges, rather than choosing a mortgage from LTV alone.

Does reaching 80% LTV remove PMI?

For many conventional mortgages covered by the federal Homeowners Protection Act, a borrower may request PMI cancellation when the principal balance is scheduled to reach 80% of the home's original value, or earlier if additional payments bring the balance to that level. "Original value" generally means the lower of the purchase contract price or appraised value at purchase; for a refinance, it generally means the appraisal used for that refinance.

Cancellation is subject to conditions. The request generally must be in writing, the borrower must have a good payment history and be current, there must be no junior liens, and the servicer may require evidence that the property's value has not declined. For eligible covered mortgages, PMI generally must terminate automatically when the scheduled balance reaches 78% of original value if the borrower is current. A separate midpoint rule may apply if PMI remains in place.

These federal rules do not apply in the same way to FHA or VA mortgage insurance, lender-paid mortgage insurance or every type of loan. A home's current market value rising to a level that produces 80% current LTV does not automatically mean the HPA cancellation test has been met. Contact your servicer and review your PMI disclosure for the rules that apply to your loan.

Current equity vs. the lender's underwriting LTV

Homeowners often estimate equity by subtracting their current mortgage balance from an estimate of today's home value. A rough current LTV estimate is current mortgage balance divided by estimated current value. This can help with personal planning, but it is not necessarily the same figure a lender uses to approve a refinance, remove PMI or underwrite a new loan.

A lender may require an appraisal or another permitted valuation and may use the original loan amount, a particular property-value definition, subordinate liens or the full HELOC limit. Do not treat a public home-value estimate or your own current-equity calculation as a lender's official LTV determination.

What can change your LTV over time?

  • Principal payments: Paying down the mortgage generally increases equity, assuming property value and other liens stay the same.
  • Property value changes: A rise or fall in market value changes a rough current LTV estimate, but may not change the original-value test used for certain PMI rights.
  • New borrowing: A HELOC or second mortgage can increase CLTV or HCLTV even when the first-mortgage balance does not change.
  • Refinancing: A new loan, new appraisal, financed costs or subordinate liens can change the ratios used in the new underwriting decision.

Questions to ask your lender

  • Which property value will you use: purchase price, appraised value, or the lower of the two?
  • Are you quoting first-mortgage LTV, CLTV or HCLTV?
  • How are my HELOC and any second mortgage included in the calculation?
  • Does the loan require borrower-paid or lender-paid mortgage insurance, and how much will it cost?
  • For an existing loan, which original-value test and cancellation rules apply to my PMI?
  • Will a new subordinate lien or increased HELOC limit require the mortgage to be re-underwritten?

Frequently asked questions

What is a good LTV for a mortgage?

There is no single LTV that is best for every borrower or loan program. Lower LTV can improve equity and may help with pricing or mortgage-insurance costs, but using more cash for a down payment can reduce your reserves. Compare the available loan options and keep enough money for closing, emergencies and ongoing ownership costs.

Is 80% LTV the same as a 20% down payment?

In a simple purchase where the loan equals the price minus the down payment and the lender uses the purchase price as property value, yes. If the appraisal is lower, the lender uses another value definition, fees are financed or there is subordinate financing, the relationship may differ.

Does a higher home value automatically lower my official LTV?

It may lower a rough current LTV estimate, but a lender may need an appraisal or an approved valuation and may use a transaction-specific calculation. A higher estimated value does not automatically change the original-value test for PMI cancellation.

Does LTV include closing costs?

Not usually as a separate item in the basic formula. However, if certain costs or mortgage insurance are financed into the loan amount, the amount used in the lender's LTV calculation may increase. The exact treatment depends on the loan program.

What is the difference between CLTV and HCLTV?

Under Fannie Mae's standard definitions, CLTV counts the drawn HELOC balance, while HCLTV counts the full HELOC credit line whether drawn or not. Both can include the first mortgage and other subordinate financing.

Does an 80% LTV guarantee PMI cancellation?

No. For eligible conventional loans covered by the Homeowners Protection Act, borrowers can generally request cancellation at 80% of original value if the required conditions are met. Automatic termination generally occurs at 78% of original value when the borrower is current. Other mortgage-insurance programs have different rules.

Can I have more than 100% LTV?

Yes, depending on how the loan and property value are calculated. If the amount financed is greater than the value used in the calculation, LTV is above 100%. Some loan programs may permit particular structures, but eligibility and costs depend on the program.

Where can I check the LTV my lender is using?

Ask your loan officer for the property's value used in underwriting, the loan amount included in the numerator, and whether subordinate financing is included. For a purchase, compare the appraisal and purchase price; for an existing loan, review your mortgage and HELOC statements.
