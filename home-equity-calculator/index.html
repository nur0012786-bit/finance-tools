CALCULATION METHOD

How this US home equity calculator works

The basic home-equity calculation is estimated home value minus property-secured debt. If a home is worth $400,000 and the mortgage balance is $250,000, estimated equity is $150,000.

This calculator also adds any other property-secured debt you enter. Total secured debt is therefore the mortgage balance plus the other secured debt. This matters when estimating a combined loan-to-value ratio.

Loan-to-value ratio

The current LTV is calculated as total secured debt divided by estimated home value. For example, $250,000 of secured debt on a $400,000 home produces a 62.5% LTV.

Equity percentage

Equity percentage is the estimated equity divided by the home value. In the same example, $150,000 of equity represents 37.5% of the home's estimated value.

Potential borrowing room

The calculator multiplies the home value by your selected target maximum combined LTV, then subtracts existing secured debt. If the result is negative, borrowing room is shown as $0. This is only a mathematical scenario; lenders use their own property valuation, underwriting rules and credit limits.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Home equity example

Suppose a home is estimated at $400,000 and the current mortgage balance is $250,000, with no other secured debt.

  • Estimated equity: $400,000 − $250,000 = $150,000
  • Current LTV: $250,000 ÷ $400,000 = 62.5%
  • Equity percentage: $150,000 ÷ $400,000 = 37.5%
  • At an illustrative 80% target combined LTV, total secured debt could equal $320,000.
  • Mathematical additional borrowing room at that target: $320,000 − $250,000 = $70,000.

The $70,000 figure is not a lender quote. Actual available credit can be lower or unavailable because lenders may consider credit history, income, existing obligations, property type, appraisal, occupancy, lien position and other requirements.

EQUITY VS. BORROWING CAPACITY

Having equity does not mean you can borrow all of it

Home equity and borrowing capacity are different concepts. Equity describes the value remaining after secured debt. A lender may impose a maximum combined LTV or another limit when considering a HELOC or home equity loan. The applicable limit varies by lender and product.

For example, having $150,000 of estimated equity does not automatically mean a lender will allow a $150,000 loan. The lender may require you to keep a portion of the home's value unborrowed and may apply additional underwriting criteria.

Home values can also change. If the property value falls while the debt balance stays unchanged, estimated equity and available borrowing room can decline.

US HOME-EQUITY OPTIONS

How home equity can be accessed

Common home-secured borrowing options include a HELOC, a home equity loan, and a cash-out refinance. A HELOC is generally revolving credit that can be drawn and repaid during the draw period. A home equity loan generally provides a lump sum. A cash-out refinance replaces an existing mortgage with a larger mortgage and provides the difference in cash.

WHAT CHANGES EQUITY?

Factors that can increase or reduce home equity

  • Principal payments: Paying down mortgage principal generally increases the equity represented by the property.
  • Home appreciation: A higher market value can increase estimated equity if debt stays unchanged.
  • Home depreciation: A lower market value can reduce estimated equity and may reduce borrowing capacity.
  • New secured borrowing: A HELOC, home equity loan or other lien increases secured debt and reduces the equity remaining after debt.
  • Property valuation: The value used by a lender may differ from an online estimate or the value entered into this calculator.

FAQ

US home equity calculator FAQs

What is home equity?

Home equity is the difference between a property's estimated value and the amount owed on mortgages and other liens secured by the property.

How do I calculate home equity?

Subtract total property-secured debt from the home's estimated value. If the property is worth $400,000 and secured debt is $250,000, estimated equity is $150,000.

What is LTV?

Loan-to-value compares secured debt with the property's value. The formula is secured debt ÷ home value × 100.

What is combined LTV?

Combined LTV, often called CLTV, considers multiple property-secured balances rather than only the first mortgage. This can be relevant when evaluating a HELOC or home equity loan.

Can I borrow all of my home equity?

Not necessarily. A lender may apply a maximum LTV or CLTV and additional underwriting requirements. The mathematical borrowing room shown here is not an approval amount.

Does home value affect equity?

Yes. If the home's value changes while the debt stays the same, the estimated equity and LTV change.

Does this calculator use an appraisal?

No. You enter the estimated home value yourself. A lender may use an appraisal, valuation model or other property-value method when underwriting a loan.

Is this a home equity loan or HELOC approval calculator?

No. It estimates equity and a user-selected LTV scenario. Actual eligibility, rates and credit limits depend on the lender and borrower.

Does home equity include the value of my personal property?

No. This calculator is based on the estimated real-property value you enter and debt secured by that property.

Educational estimate: This calculator does not determine the appraised value of your home, lender eligibility, available credit, interest rate or approval amount. Review the lender's disclosures and property valuation before relying on a borrowing estimate.
