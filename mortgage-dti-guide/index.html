UNDERSTAND THE BASICS

What is debt-to-income ratio (DTI)?

Debt-to-income ratio compares monthly debt payments with gross monthly income—income before taxes and other deductions. Lenders use DTI as one measure of a borrower's ability to manage monthly payments. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains that different loan products and lenders can apply different DTI limits.

DTI = Total qualifying monthly debt payments ÷ Gross monthly income × 100

For example, if qualifying monthly debt payments total $2,000 and gross monthly income is $6,000, DTI is 33.3% ($2,000 ÷ $6,000 × 100). This is an arithmetic example, not an approval threshold. See the CFPB explanation of DTI.

Housing ratio vs. total DTI

Mortgage discussions sometimes separate the proposed housing payment from all qualifying monthly debt. These ratios are related but not interchangeable:

  • Housing ratio (sometimes called front-end ratio): proposed monthly housing expense divided by gross monthly income. Depending on loan rules, housing expense can include principal, interest, property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance and required association dues.
  • Total DTI (sometimes called back-end ratio): proposed qualifying housing expense plus other qualifying monthly debts, divided by qualifying gross monthly income.

The CFPB's general DTI definition uses all monthly debt payments divided by gross monthly income. Fannie Mae's conventional underwriting guide also includes the qualifying housing payment and other qualifying debts in total DTI. A separate housing-ratio ceiling is a planning or underwriting input in some contexts, not a universal requirement for every mortgage.

Which payments may count as debt?

The lender applies rules for the specific loan program and underwriting method. Common liabilities considered may include:

  • The proposed mortgage housing payment, including the qualifying payment, property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance and required HOA or condominium dues where applicable.
  • Auto loans, student loans, personal loans and other installment debts.
  • Required monthly payments on credit cards and other revolving accounts.
  • Lease payments, such as vehicle leases.
  • Alimony, child support, maintenance and other recurring obligations where required by the applicable rules.
  • Other mortgage obligations and applicable rental-property losses.

Not every payment is treated identically. For example, Fannie Mae's conventional guidance includes installment debt extending beyond ten months and certain shorter-term payments when they significantly affect the borrower's ability to meet credit obligations. It also specifies treatment for revolving debt, leases and support obligations. A lender may apply a more conservative approach consistently across similar applications.

Utilities, groceries, fuel, childcare, subscriptions and routine household spending are generally not counted as monthly debt in DTI. They still matter to your personal budget, so a low DTI does not automatically mean a mortgage payment is comfortable or sustainable.

Source: Fannie Mae Selling Guide B3-6-02: Debt-to-Income Ratios.

What income is used?

For a simple personal estimate, people often start with gross monthly income before taxes and payroll deductions. Mortgage underwriting is more specific: the lender evaluates income that is eligible and documented under the applicable program. The qualifying amount may differ from the money deposited into your bank account or from an informal estimate of annual earnings.

Salary, hourly wages, overtime, bonuses, commissions, self-employment income, rental income and other sources can have different documentation and calculation requirements. Do not assume every income source will count in full. Ask the lender which income it has accepted for qualification and how it converted that income to a monthly figure.

For your own budget, also review take-home pay after taxes, insurance and retirement deductions. DTI is an underwriting measure; take-home cash flow determines how much money is actually available for living expenses and savings.

How to calculate total DTI

  1. Determine gross monthly qualifying income. For a personal estimate with steady income, divide annual gross income by 12.
  2. Estimate the proposed monthly housing payment. Include principal and interest, property tax, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance and required association charges where applicable.
  3. Add other qualifying monthly debts. Include the lender's required payments for auto loans, student loans, revolving accounts, leases and other obligations.
  4. Divide total qualifying monthly debt by gross monthly income, then multiply by 100.

For example, a household with $6,000 gross monthly income, a proposed housing payment of $1,800, an auto payment of $350, a student-loan payment of $250 and credit-card minimum payments of $100 has $2,500 in monthly obligations. Total DTI is 41.7% ($2,500 ÷ $6,000 × 100). The housing ratio is 30% ($1,800 ÷ $6,000 × 100).

This illustrates the arithmetic. A lender may calculate the housing payment, qualifying debts and qualifying income differently under its program rules.

Are 28% and 36% DTI limits universal?

No. The 28% housing and 36% total DTI figures are common conservative planning assumptions, not universal legal limits or approval guarantees. Actual thresholds depend on loan program, underwriting method, credit profile, reserves, property and lender requirements.

For context, Fannie Mae's conventional-loan guidance states:

  • Manually underwritten loans: maximum total DTI is generally 36%. It can go up to 45% when the borrower meets the credit-score and reserve requirements in Fannie Mae's Eligibility Matrix.
  • Desktop Underwriter (DU) casefiles: the maximum allowable DTI is 50% under the standard guidance, subject to applicable exceptions and eligibility requirements.

These are Fannie Mae conventional-loan rules—not universal limits for FHA, VA, USDA, jumbo or portfolio loans. Fannie Mae identifies exceptions for particular transactions and directs government mortgage loans to the respective agency's requirements. Lenders may also apply stricter overlays. A DTI below a published maximum does not guarantee approval.

References: Fannie Mae B3-6-02 and the Fannie Mae Eligibility Matrix (March 4, 2026).

How the Finance Tools Affordability Calculator uses DTI

The Mortgage Affordability Calculator lets you enter two separate planning assumptions: a maximum housing DTI percentage and a maximum total DTI percentage. Its defaults are 28% and 36%. The tool does not apply Fannie Mae, FHA, VA or other lender underwriting rules, and it does not determine whether your income or debts qualify under those rules.

The calculator converts annual gross household income to a monthly amount, then estimates the maximum housing budget as the lower of:

  • Housing limit: gross monthly income multiplied by the entered housing DTI percentage.
  • Total-DTI limit: gross monthly income multiplied by the entered total DTI percentage, minus other monthly debt payments.

It then estimates a home price using the entered down payment, interest rate, term, property-tax assumption, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance and HOA dues. These are simplified user-entered assumptions; an actual lender may use different qualifying income, liabilities, property tax, insurance or payment calculations.

Worked example using the calculator's default assumptions

Assume a household enters annual gross income of $90,000 ($7,500 per month), other monthly debt of $500, a $30,000 down payment, a 6.50% rate, a 30-year term, property tax of 1.20% per year, homeowners insurance of $1,800 per year, and $0 for mortgage insurance and HOA dues. The DTI assumptions are 28% for housing and 36% for total DTI.

The housing limit is $2,100 per month ($7,500 × 28%). The total-DTI limit is $2,700 ($7,500 × 36%). After subtracting $500 of other monthly debt, the total-DTI calculation leaves $2,200 for housing. The lower limit is therefore $2,100 per month.

Under the remaining assumptions, the calculator estimates a maximum home price of approximately $292,271 and a starting loan of approximately $262,271. Estimated monthly principal and interest is about $1,657.73, property tax is about $292.27 and homeowners insurance is $150, for estimated housing costs of $2,100.

This is the calculator's mathematical model—not a lender's qualification decision. Adding mortgage insurance or HOA dues, or increasing tax, insurance or rate assumptions, generally reduces the estimated maximum price. A lender may use different qualifying income or liability amounts.

What can change your DTI during a mortgage application?

A lender may need to review a file again if qualifying income falls or additional debt is discovered after initial underwriting. Fannie Mae's guidance includes re-underwriting requirements for certain material changes before closing. New subordinate financing on the property also triggers re-underwriting under its rules.

During an active mortgage application, tell your lender about material changes and do not take on new debt without first discussing the effect. Financing a vehicle, opening new credit accounts or making large unexplained financial moves can affect the loan review. Do not hide liabilities or change information to make a ratio look lower.

Source: Fannie Mae: DTI tolerance and re-underwriting criteria.

Practical ways to improve your DTI

  • Reduce recurring debt payments. Paying down revolving balances or paying off eligible installment debt may reduce monthly obligations, but check the lender's treatment and avoid draining necessary cash reserves.
  • Avoid new monthly payments. A new auto loan, personal loan or financed purchase can increase obligations before or during underwriting.
  • Document eligible income. Keep accurate records and ask the lender how variable, bonus, commission, rental or self-employment income can be evaluated.
  • Reconsider the loan amount. A smaller mortgage or larger down payment may reduce the proposed payment, although a larger down payment can leave too little money for closing costs and reserves.
  • Test a conservative housing budget. Do not automatically spend up to the maximum DTI assumption. Keep room for repairs, utilities, emergencies and savings.

No single strategy is right for every borrower. Compare the effect on monthly payments, cash reserves and total borrowing costs before changing your finances.

DTI vs. personal affordability

DTI is only one underwriting measure. It does not fully reflect food, utilities, transport, childcare, healthcare, retirement saving, repairs or unpredictable expenses. A borrower can meet a lender's DTI rule and still find the resulting payment uncomfortable.

For personal budgeting, use take-home income and create room for household expenses, emergency savings and ongoing home maintenance. A lender's maximum approval is not a recommendation to borrow that amount.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good DTI for a mortgage?

There is no single DTI percentage that guarantees approval or is right for every household. Lower recurring debt can improve cash flow, but lenders assess the full application under the rules for the specific loan program. For personal comfort, test the payment against take-home income and living expenses too.

Is DTI calculated using gross or net income?

The standard DTI formula uses gross monthly income before taxes and other deductions. Mortgage lenders use qualifying income that meets the applicable documentation and program rules. For your own budget, review take-home pay as well.

Does DTI include property taxes and homeowners insurance?

The qualifying mortgage housing payment commonly includes property taxes and homeowners insurance, along with principal, interest and applicable mortgage insurance. The precise housing payment and treatment of association dues depend on the loan program and underwriting rules.

Are utilities and groceries included in DTI?

They are generally not treated as monthly debt payments in the standard mortgage DTI calculation. They still affect your actual ability to afford a home and belong in your personal budget.

Do credit-card balances count or only minimum payments?

Lenders generally use a required monthly payment for revolving debt rather than adding the full credit-card balance to monthly debt. The exact payment used depends on credit information and applicable underwriting guidance.

Can I get a mortgage with a DTI above 36%?

Possibly. It depends on the mortgage program and underwriting method. Fannie Mae permits some manually underwritten conventional loans above 36% when specified requirements are met, and its DU casefiles have a different maximum. These rules do not apply automatically to other loan programs or guarantee approval.

Does paying off a debt always improve mortgage eligibility?

Not automatically. The lender must determine whether and how that payment changes the qualifying obligation, and using cash to pay debt can reduce funds available for the down payment, closing costs or required reserves. Ask the lender to compare both scenarios before moving money.

Can taking a new loan affect approval after preapproval?

Yes. New debt or reduced qualifying income can change the DTI calculation and may lead to additional review or re-underwriting. Tell the lender about material changes during the process.
