Credit Card Minimum Payment Explained

Understand issuer minimum-payment formulas, the statement repayment warning, interest and payment allocation, and ways to reduce repayment time.

Educational finance guidance

The minimum payment is the amount the card issuer requires you to pay by the due date to keep the account current, subject to the card's agreement.

What is a credit card minimum payment?

Your minimum payment is the smallest amount your card issuer requires you to pay by the due date to keep the account from becoming past due. Your statement shows the amount and the payment deadline. Paying at least that amount on time is important, but it does not mean the balance will be repaid quickly or without substantial interest.

The minimum payment is not the same as the statement balance. Paying the statement balance in full by the due date is generally how you avoid interest on eligible purchases when your account has a grace period and you have met its conditions. Paying only the minimum usually leaves part of the balance unpaid, where interest may continue to accrue.

How credit card companies calculate the minimum

There is no single minimum-payment formula used by every card issuer. The formula is set out in your cardholder agreement and may differ between cards from the same issuer. Common approaches combine a percentage of the statement balance with interest, fees and any past-due amount, subject to a fixed-dollar floor.

In its 2025 Consumer Credit Card Market Report, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) reviewed publicly available issuer agreements. Among the agreements it reviewed, fixed minimum-payment floors ranged from $15 to $50, with $40 the most common. Most issuers in that review used 1% of the statement balance and added finance charges, fees and past-due amounts. These are findings from the reviewed agreements—not a universal rule or a promise about your card.

A simplified formula might look like this:

  • Calculate a stated percentage of the balance, such as 1%.
  • Add interest and applicable fees.
  • Add any amount past due, when required by the agreement.
  • Compare the result with the issuer's minimum-dollar floor and use the amount required by the agreement, subject to the balance due.

Your own issuer may use a different percentage, floor, calculation method or additional conditions. Check the section called “Minimum Payment” or similar wording in your cardholder agreement. The CFPB also provides a credit card agreement database.

Worked example: why the minimum can keep shrinking

Consider a hypothetical card with a $5,000 balance and a 24% APR. For illustration only, assume interest is approximated monthly at APR divided by 12, and the issuer's formula is 1% of the current balance plus that month's interest, subject to a $40 floor. There are no new purchases, fees or promotional balances.

  • First month's estimated interest: $5,000 × 24% ÷ 12 = $100.
  • 1% of the balance: $50.
  • Illustrative first minimum: $50 + $100 = $150, which is above the assumed $40 floor.

In this simplified model, the minimum is recalculated as the balance falls. The required payment can therefore fall too, leaving less money to reduce the principal. Under these assumptions, paying only the calculated minimum takes about 187 months (15 years and 7 months) and produces about $8,219 in interest. Paying a fixed $200 each month instead takes about 36 months and produces about $2,001 in interest.

This is an illustration of one assumed formula, not a prediction of your issuer's bill. Actual credit card interest is commonly calculated daily using the average daily balance, and the amount due depends on the card agreement, transaction types, payment timing, fees and new activity. Use your statement and agreement for the actual minimum.

The minimum-payment warning on your statement

For many credit card accounts, the periodic statement includes a “Minimum Payment Warning” and an estimate of how long it would take to repay the statement balance if you make only minimum payments and add no new charges. The statement also generally shows an estimated monthly payment that would repay that balance in 36 months, along with a credit-counseling contact number.

The 36-month figure is an estimate—not a required payment, a new minimum, or a guarantee that your account will be paid off in three years. The estimate is based on the balance shown on that statement and assumes no additional charges. New purchases, fees, changes in rates or payments below the assumed amount can change the outcome. See the CFPB's explanation of the three-year repayment estimate.

How interest and payment allocation affect repayment

Credit card APR is an annual rate, but many issuers calculate interest using a daily periodic rate and an average daily balance. The daily rate may be based on APR divided by 360 or 365, depending on the issuer's method. That means the exact interest amount in a billing cycle can differ from a simple APR-divided-by-12 estimate.

Your statement may show separate balances and APRs for purchases, balance transfers and cash advances. These categories can have different rates and terms. A purchase grace period may apply only to eligible new purchases and may be lost while you carry a balance, depending on the agreement.

When you pay more than the required minimum, federal rules generally require the amount above the minimum to be applied to the balance with the highest APR first. The issuer generally has more discretion over how it allocates the minimum-payment portion. Special rules can apply to deferred-interest promotional balances. Read the issuer's terms and the CFPB's explanation of credit card interest and payment allocation.

How to read the minimum-payment section of your statement

Do not look only at the amount due. Read the nearby figures together so you understand the balance and the assumptions behind the repayment estimate:

  • Statement balance: the amount owed at the end of the billing period. It may differ from the live balance shown in your app after later transactions or payments.
  • Minimum payment due: the amount the issuer requires by the stated deadline under your agreement.
  • Payment due date: the date by which the issuer must receive or credit the payment under its stated rules. Allow time for bank transfers, weekends, holidays and payment cut-off times.
  • Minimum-payment repayment estimate: an estimate based on the current statement balance and the assumption that you make only minimum payments and add no new amounts.
  • 36-month payment estimate: a fixed monthly amount calculated to repay the statement balance in 36 months under the disclosure's assumptions. It is not a new contractual minimum.

These disclosures are governed by Regulation Z. The exact items shown can depend on the account and regulatory exceptions. Review the CFPB's current periodic-statement rule, § 1026.7, and its Appendix M1 repayment-disclosure calculation rules. The estimate is a comparison tool, not a promise about future bills or a substitute for your issuer's terms.

Compare a shrinking minimum with a fixed monthly payment

Return to the simplified $5,000 balance at 24% APR used above. Assume monthly interest is approximated as balance × 24% ÷ 12, no new purchases or fees occur, and the minimum is 1% of the balance plus interest with a $40 floor. The following results are calculated using those same simplified assumptions:

Payment approachEstimated payoff timeEstimated interest
Recalculated minimum only187 months (about 15 years, 7 months)$8,219
Fixed $150 each month56 months (about 4 years, 8 months)$3,322
Fixed $200 each month36 months$2,001
Fixed $250 each month26 months (about 2 years, 2 months)$1,449

The key difference is that a fixed payment does not automatically fall just because the balance gets smaller. As long as the payment remains affordable and exceeds the required minimum, more money can go toward reducing principal over time. The final payment is smaller than the regular fixed amount when only a smaller balance remains.

These figures are rounded estimates, not a reproduction of a credit card issuer's statement calculation. Real accounts may use daily periodic rates, average daily balances, multiple APR categories, different minimum formulas, fees and payment-posting rules. Your actual result can be materially different. Do not treat the table as a quote or as a guarantee of savings.

What if the minimum payment is more than you can afford?

Do not simply pay an amount you chose and assume the account will remain current. If you cannot make the required minimum, contact the issuer before the due date if possible. Explain what changed, what amount you can realistically pay and when your situation may improve. Ask whether the issuer offers a hardship arrangement, temporary reduced payment, due-date change or other option.

Before accepting an arrangement, ask whether interest and fees continue, whether the card will be frozen or closed, how long the arrangement lasts, what amount is due each month and what happens when it ends. Request the terms in writing. If you have several debts or cannot meet essential expenses, consider a reputable nonprofit credit counselor and ask about fees and services before enrolling.

Making a partial payment may reduce what you owe, but it does not necessarily satisfy the contractual minimum or prevent a late-payment consequence. Only the issuer can confirm whether a specific arrangement changes the amount due or the account's status. The CFPB's guidance for consumers who cannot pay credit card bills recommends contacting the card company promptly and explaining what you can afford.

Common minimum-payment mistakes to avoid

  • Confusing the minimum with the statement balance. The minimum keeps the account from being past due when paid on time; it does not normally clear the statement balance or preserve an interest-free grace period on carried balances.
  • Assuming the minimum is always a fixed percentage. Your agreement may add interest, fees and past-due amounts or apply a fixed-dollar floor.
  • Using the 36-month figure as the required minimum. It is a disclosure estimate for a different repayment pace, not a contractual amount you must pay.
  • Ignoring promotional or special-rate balances. Different balances can have different APRs, payment-allocation rules and expiration dates.
  • Making a payment at the last minute. Check the issuer's accepted payment methods, cut-off times and posting rules. Keep confirmation and verify the payment appears on the account.
  • Continuing to add purchases without updating the plan. New charges can offset progress and make calculator estimates inaccurate.

Practical ways to reduce the cost

  • Pay at least the minimum by the due date. A late or missed minimum can trigger fees and other consequences under the agreement. Set a reminder or automatic payment for at least the minimum, and check that the payment clears.
  • Pay more when your budget allows. A fixed payment above the minimum can reduce the balance faster and limit interest. Choose an amount you can sustain without missing essential expenses or other required bills.
  • Limit new charges while repaying. Adding purchases while making minimum payments can extend repayment and increase the cost.
  • Direct extra payments strategically. If you carry balances at different APRs, understand how the issuer applies the amount above the minimum. The highest-rate balance generally receives that portion first, subject to exceptions.
  • Contact the issuer before you miss a payment. Ask whether a hardship plan, adjusted due date or other assistance is available. Get any changed terms in writing and understand how they affect your account.

For broader consumer guidance, the CFPB explains what to know about credit cards. If you are considering credit counseling, review the statement's contact information and research the organization before sharing financial details or paying fees.

Frequently asked questions

Is paying the minimum enough to keep my account current?

Generally, you must make at least the minimum payment by the due date shown on your statement. Make sure the payment is received and credited on time under the issuer's rules. Paying the minimum does not prevent interest from accruing on the remaining balance.

Does every credit card use a 1% minimum-payment formula?

No. Issuer formulas vary. The CFPB's 2025 review found that most of the agreements it examined used 1% of the statement balance plus finance charges, fees and past-due amounts, but your card may use a different formula. Check your agreement.

Can the minimum payment be less than the interest charged?

It can occur under some account formulas or circumstances. Federal statement-disclosure rules address accounts where making only the minimum would not amortize the balance. Read any warning on your statement and contact the issuer if you do not understand it.

Why does my minimum payment change each month?

The calculation may use a percentage of the current statement balance, interest, fees and past-due amounts. As those figures change, the minimum can change. Your agreement explains the specific calculation.

What is the 36-month payment shown on my statement?

It is an estimate of the monthly amount needed to repay the statement balance in 36 months under the assumptions used for the disclosure. It is not the minimum payment and does not account for future purchases.

Does paying more than the minimum reduce interest?

Usually, yes, because a larger payment can reduce the balance on which interest is calculated. The result depends on the APRs, timing, fees, payment allocation and whether you keep adding new charges.

Will paying only the minimum hurt my credit score?

Paying the required minimum on time is different from missing a payment. A high balance relative to your credit limit can affect credit scores, and missed or late payments can have negative consequences. Scoring models differ, so no exact score effect can be predicted from this page.

What should I do if I cannot afford the minimum?

Contact the card issuer as soon as possible, ideally before the due date, and ask about hardship options. Review the consequences and terms before agreeing to a plan. You can also use the credit-counseling information on your statement to explore outside assistance.
