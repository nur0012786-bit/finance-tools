UNDERSTAND THE BASICS

What is a Closing Disclosure?

A Closing Disclosure is a standardized five-page form that gives the final details of many US closed-end mortgage loans secured by real property. It summarizes the loan terms, projected monthly payments, closing costs, cash to close and other important loan disclosures.

Compare it with your latest Loan Estimate and the terms you agreed to with your lender. It is not just a receipt: review it for incorrect information, unexpected charges, changed loan terms and payment features you do not understand before signing.

For most covered transactions, the lender must ensure you receive the initial Closing Disclosure at least three business days before consummation. Some products, including HELOCs and reverse mortgages, use different disclosure rules. The timing requirement is not a promise that every mortgage process takes a fixed number of days.

Use the CFPB's interactive Closing Disclosure Explainer and keep your most recent Loan Estimate beside you as you review.

Page 1: Confirm loan terms and projected payments

Start with the transaction information and loan terms. Check each field against the latest Loan Estimate, your rate-lock agreement and the purchase contract.

  • Closing and transaction information: check the date issued, closing date, property address, sale price, borrower and seller names, settlement agent and lender.
  • Loan information: confirm the term, purpose, product and loan type, such as conventional, FHA or VA.
  • Loan amount: compare it with the amount you agreed to borrow. Ask why it changed if it is higher or lower.
  • Interest rate: confirm it matches your rate-lock agreement or other agreed pricing. Ask whether the rate is locked and when the lock expires.
  • Prepayment penalty or balloon payment: check whether either feature applies. A prepayment penalty may charge you for paying off the loan early; a balloon payment can leave a large lump sum due.
  • Projected monthly payment: compare principal and interest, mortgage insurance and estimated escrow amounts. Make sure the projected total fits your budget.
  • Taxes, insurance and assessments: identify which items are included in escrow and which you must pay separately. HOA dues are often not included.

Do not compare only principal and interest. Your total monthly housing cost can also include property taxes, homeowners insurance and mortgage insurance. Escrowed taxes or insurance can change later when bills or premiums change.

See the CFPB's page 1 review guidance and our Mortgage Payment Guide.

Page 2: Review itemized closing costs

Page 2 breaks down the charges associated with obtaining the mortgage and completing the transaction. Compare it with page 2 of your latest Loan Estimate and with provider quotes or agreements.

Loan Costs

  • Origination charges: lender fees for making the loan. Check origination charges and any discount points you agreed to pay.
  • Services the borrower did not shop for: third-party services selected or required through the lender's process. Ask about unfamiliar or materially different amounts.
  • Services the borrower did shop for: compare provider names and amounts with the quotes you selected. Ask why a provider or amount differs.

Other Costs

  • Taxes and other government fees: recording, transfer and other charges imposed by government authorities.
  • Prepaids: amounts paid in advance, which can include daily interest from the closing date to the end of the month and an initial homeowners insurance premium.
  • Initial escrow payment: money collected to establish the escrow account for upcoming property-tax or insurance bills.
  • Other costs: other transaction services and charges. Confirm that each item is expected and correctly assigned.

Check the lender credits and seller credits. Confirm each credit matches your agreement and is applied to an eligible cost. Lender credits can be associated with accepting a higher interest rate; seller contributions are subject to your loan program's rules.

Total Closing Costs is not the same as cash to close. Closing costs exclude the down payment, while cash to close also reflects the down payment, deposits, credits and other adjustments.

For more context, read the Mortgage Closing Costs Guide and the CFPB's page 2 review guidance.

Page 3: Verify the cash-to-close calculation

Page 3 compares the estimated cash to close from your Loan Estimate with the final amount due from you. It also summarizes the transaction and reconciles amounts already paid or credited.

  • Due from borrower at closing: amounts you owe, such as the purchase amount and closing costs.
  • Adjustments for items paid by seller in advance: amounts the seller paid earlier that you are required to reimburse under the transaction.
  • Paid already by or on behalf of borrower: credits toward the transaction, which can include the loan amount, earnest money deposit and applicable credits.
  • Adjustments for items unpaid by seller: certain taxes or charges owed by the seller but payable later, for which the seller reimburses you under the settlement calculation.
  • Cash to Close: the final amount due from you after applicable payments, deposits, credits and adjustments are included.

Confirm that your earnest money deposit is shown, seller credits match the contract, lender credits are present and costs already paid are credited correctly. If cash to close differs from your Loan Estimate, ask the lender or settlement agent to explain each change.

Independently verify the method for delivering funds with the settlement agent, using a trusted phone number you already have. Do not rely only on a last-minute email for changed payment instructions. Treat changes to bank or wire details as potential fraud until verified directly.

See the CFPB's cash-to-close explanation and our Closing Costs Calculator.

Page 4: Review other loan disclosures and escrow details

Page 4 contains other disclosures about how the loan works and what may happen if you do not make required payments or if particular loan features apply.

  • Late payment: check when a late fee can be charged and how it is calculated.
  • Partial payments: find out whether the lender accepts less than the full monthly payment and, if so, whether it applies or holds partial payments until the full amount is received.
  • Escrow account: check whether taxes and insurance will be collected monthly by the servicer, which items are included and which costs you must pay separately. HOA dues are often not included.
  • No-escrow arrangement: understand how and when you must pay property taxes and insurance yourself. Ask whether an escrow-waiver fee applies.
  • Assumption: check whether another buyer may be allowed to take over the loan under its terms if you sell the property.
  • Demand feature: understand whether the loan gives the lender a right to demand immediate repayment of the full balance. Ask for an explanation if it appears.
  • Negative amortization: check whether the payment structure could allow the principal balance to increase instead of decline.
  • Security interest: confirm that the property is collateral for the mortgage and understand that failure to meet loan obligations can put the property at risk of foreclosure.

Do not assume these features apply to every mortgage. The form discloses terms for the particular loan. Ask the lender to explain any feature marked as applicable or that you do not understand.

See the CFPB's page 4 definitions, our Mortgage Payment Guide and Mortgage Amortization Guide.

Page 5: Understand total borrowing-cost calculations

Page 5 includes calculations that describe the cost of borrowing. These figures answer different questions and should not be treated as interchangeable.

  • Total of Payments: the total of scheduled payments you would make over the loan term if you make all payments as scheduled.
  • Finance Charge: the cost of credit expressed as a dollar amount, including interest and certain loan charges under the disclosed assumptions.
  • Amount Financed: a disclosure calculation representing the credit provided or made available to you, adjusted for certain prepaid finance charges. It is not necessarily the same as the loan amount on page 1.
  • Annual Percentage Rate (APR): a measure of the cost of credit expressed as a yearly rate. It includes certain costs beyond the note interest rate, so it can differ from the interest rate on page 1.
  • Total Interest Percentage (TIP): the total scheduled interest over the life of the loan expressed as a percentage of the loan amount. It is not the same as APR.
  • Appraisal and other disclosures: review appraisal-related information and other notices shown on the form. Ask the lender for a copy of the appraisal if you have not received it.

These calculations assume scheduled payments and the disclosed terms. They do not predict how long you will actually keep the mortgage or what you will pay if you refinance, sell, make extra principal payments, miss payments or modify the loan.

Read the CFPB's page 5 definitions and compare the loan's rate and upfront costs with your original offer.

Does every correction restart the three-business-day waiting period?

No. A corrected Closing Disclosure is generally required when disclosed terms change. However, most corrections do not automatically create a new three-business-day waiting period. Under CFPB guidance, a new waiting period is required only if one of these three changes occurs:

  1. The disclosed APR becomes inaccurate under the applicable accuracy rules.
  2. The loan product changes so that the product information disclosed becomes inaccurate.
  3. A prepayment penalty is added to the loan.

For other changes, a corrected Closing Disclosure generally must be received at or before consummation, without a new three-business-day wait. Ask the lender to explain the change, whether a corrected disclosure is required and whether the closing date must move. Do not assume a change is covered by an exception without confirmation.

Source: CFPB: corrected Closing Disclosures and the waiting period.

How are the three business days counted?

The three-day waiting period is measured using the business-day definition in the federal mortgage disclosure rules. For this waiting period, a business day is generally every calendar day except Sundays and specified federal legal public holidays. Saturdays generally count. This is different from the definition used for some other mortgage disclosure deadlines.

Do not calculate your own final deadline from a simple calendar count. The lender is responsible for ensuring timely receipt and should confirm the receipt date and scheduled consummation date. If the disclosure is mailed or delivered electronically without confirmed receipt, special receipt-presumption rules may affect the timing.

Source: CFPB TRID FAQs.

What should you do if something looks wrong?

  1. Mark the amount, name, term or line item that looks incorrect.
  2. Compare it with the latest Loan Estimate, purchase contract, rate-lock confirmation, provider quote or deposit receipt.
  3. Contact your loan officer or settlement agent and ask for a written explanation.
  4. Request a corrected disclosure if information is inaccurate.
  5. Do not sign documents or send funds until you understand the issue and know how it will be resolved.

Examples worth questioning include an incorrect borrower name or property address, an unexpected loan amount or interest rate, a new lender fee, a missing deposit or credit, an unfamiliar service provider, an escrow amount that differs from what you discussed, or cash to close that is materially different from the latest estimate.

Closing Disclosure review checklist

  • Confirm borrower names, property address, sale price, lender and settlement agent.
  • Match the loan amount, term, purpose, product, loan type and interest rate to the agreed offer.
  • Review the total monthly payment and identify property costs not included in escrow.
  • Check the loan's prepayment penalty, balloon payment and other disclosed features.
  • Compare lender charges and third-party services with the Loan Estimate and provider quotes.
  • Review taxes, government fees, prepaids and initial escrow deposits.
  • Confirm seller credits, lender credits, deposits and amounts already paid.
  • Reconcile the final cash-to-close amount and independently verify payment instructions.
  • Read escrow, partial-payment and late-fee disclosures.
  • Review APR, Amount Financed, Finance Charge, Total of Payments and TIP; ask about any figure you cannot explain.
  • Obtain and review other closing documents, including the promissory note, mortgage or deed of trust, and deed.

Questions to ask your lender or closing agent

  • Who will deliver my Closing Disclosure, and how will I receive it?
  • What changed between the latest Loan Estimate and this Closing Disclosure?
  • Are all credits and deposits shown correctly?
  • Which taxes and insurance costs are escrowed, and which must I pay separately?
  • Does any disclosed loan feature affect payments, the loan balance or early payoff?
  • Is the APR consistent with the agreed loan terms?
  • What is the exact cash-to-close amount and verified method for delivering funds?
  • Are there remaining items that could delay consummation?

Other documents to review at closing

The Closing Disclosure is not the only document you sign. Ask to receive the other documents in advance so you have time to read them. These commonly include:

  • Promissory Note: the legal promise to repay the loan, including payment obligations.
  • Mortgage or Deed of Trust: the security instrument that gives the lender a security interest in the property.
  • Deed: the document that transfers ownership of the property, as applicable to the transaction.

The note and security instrument contain contractual terms. Read them carefully and ask a qualified professional about legal questions or any provision you do not understand.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Closing Disclosure the same as the Loan Estimate?

No. The Loan Estimate is an earlier estimate used to describe and compare a loan offer. The Closing Disclosure provides final loan terms and transaction costs before consummation for covered loans. Compare the two to identify changes.

When must I receive my Closing Disclosure?

For most covered transactions, the lender must ensure you receive it at least three business days before consummation. Different disclosure rules apply to some products, including reverse mortgages and HELOCs.

Does the three-day period mean three calendar days?

No. For the Closing Disclosure waiting period, Saturdays generally count as business days, while Sundays and specified federal legal public holidays do not. Ask your lender or closing agent to confirm the required receipt date and consummation date.

Does every corrected Closing Disclosure delay closing by three business days?

No. A new waiting period is generally triggered only if the disclosed APR becomes inaccurate, the loan product changes so the disclosure is inaccurate, or a prepayment penalty is added. Other corrections generally must be provided at or before consummation.

Does cash to close equal total closing costs?

No. Total closing costs exclude the down payment. Cash to close reflects the amount due from you after the down payment, costs, credits, deposits and applicable adjustments are reconciled.

Why can the monthly payment differ from principal and interest?

The estimated total monthly payment can include mortgage insurance and escrowed property taxes and homeowners insurance. Some costs, such as HOA dues, may need to be paid separately.

What if I see a charge I did not agree to?

Ask the lender or settlement agent to identify the service, explain why it appears and show how the amount was determined. Compare it with your Loan Estimate and any quote or agreement you have.

Should I sign if I do not understand a disclosure?

Do not sign a document you do not understand. Ask the lender or closing agent for an explanation and seek qualified legal or financial advice when needed.
