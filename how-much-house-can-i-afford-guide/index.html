AFFORDABILITY BASICS

What does “how much house can I afford?” actually mean?

Affordability is an estimate of the home price and housing payment that may fit within a household's financial situation. Lenders evaluate affordability using their own underwriting rules, documentation, loan-program requirements and qualifying criteria.

A calculator can help you explore scenarios, but it is not the same as a lender's preapproval. Your actual borrowing options can depend on factors such as verified income, debts, credit history, assets, employment, loan type, property details and lender requirements.

Start with household income

Begin with the income you expect to use for the home purchase. For a simple planning exercise, use a consistent gross-income figure and avoid counting uncertain future income as if it were guaranteed.

If more than one borrower will be on the mortgage, the lender may evaluate qualifying income from multiple borrowers subject to its underwriting requirements.

Account for recurring debt

Existing monthly obligations can affect the amount of housing payment that fits within a lender's underwriting framework. Examples can include auto loans, student loans, credit-card obligations and other recurring debt.

This is why two households with the same income can receive different affordability estimates.

Understand debt-to-income ratio (DTI)

Debt-to-income ratio, commonly called DTI, compares qualifying monthly debt obligations with gross monthly income. Different loan programs and lenders can use different DTI calculations and limits.

For planning purposes, think of DTI as a way to understand how an additional housing payment interacts with the debts you already have. Do not treat a single DTI threshold as a universal rule for every US mortgage.

Look beyond principal and interest

A mortgage payment is not always the full monthly housing cost. Depending on the property and loan, a buyer may also need to budget for:

  • Property taxes
  • Homeowners insurance
  • Private mortgage insurance or another form of mortgage insurance
  • Homeowners association dues, where applicable
  • Other property-specific expenses

Our Mortgage Calculator focuses on the loan payment, while the Property Tax Calculator and PMI Calculator can help you examine additional components separately.

Down payment changes the scenario

A larger down payment generally means a smaller mortgage balance for the same home price. It can also change the loan-to-value ratio and may affect mortgage-insurance requirements, depending on the loan.

But putting more cash into the down payment can leave less money available for closing costs, moving expenses, repairs, reserves and other ownership costs. Compare the full cash requirement rather than focusing on the down payment alone.

Interest rate and loan term matter

For a given home price and down payment, the interest rate and loan term can materially change principal-and-interest payments. A longer term can reduce the scheduled payment while increasing the number of years over which interest may accrue.

When comparing scenarios, keep the assumptions clear. Changing the home price, rate, term, down payment and taxes at the same time makes it harder to understand which factor changed the result.

Illustrative affordability example

Imagine a household testing a hypothetical $350,000 home with a $70,000 down payment. The mortgage amount would be $280,000 before considering any other financing adjustments. The household would then need to evaluate the mortgage payment, property taxes, homeowners insurance, possible mortgage insurance, recurring debts and other ownership costs.

This example does not establish that $350,000 is affordable for any particular household. The purpose is to show why a home-price number should be tested against the complete monthly and upfront cost.

Affordability vs. mortgage preapproval

An affordability calculator is a planning tool. A preapproval is a lender's assessment based on information and documentation provided during its underwriting process. The two should not be treated as interchangeable.

A lender may also approve a loan amount that is higher or lower than the amount you personally choose to spend on housing. Your own budget can include goals and expenses that a lender's underwriting calculation does not fully capture.

Remember the upfront cash requirement

Buying a home can require more cash than the down payment. Closing costs, prepaid expenses, escrow funding, inspections, moving costs and immediate repairs can affect the amount of cash you want available.

Use the Closing Costs Calculator alongside the down-payment estimate to build a more complete purchase budget.

A practical home-affordability checklist

  • Estimate reliable gross monthly income.
  • List recurring monthly debt obligations.
  • Estimate the mortgage payment for the home price being considered.
  • Add property taxes and homeowners insurance.
  • Check whether mortgage insurance may apply.
  • Estimate the down payment and remaining cash after closing.
  • Allow room for maintenance, repairs, reserves and changing expenses.
  • Compare the scenario with actual lender disclosures before making a purchase decision.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate how much house I can afford?

Start with income and recurring debt, then test a home price against the expected mortgage payment, taxes, insurance, mortgage insurance and upfront cash requirement. A lender's actual qualification can use additional information and rules.

Is there one income-to-home-price rule that works for everyone?

No. Household expenses, debt, loan terms, taxes, insurance, down payment, credit profile and lender or loan-program requirements can all change the result.

Should I use gross or take-home income?

Mortgage underwriting commonly evaluates qualifying income using defined rules, while your personal household budget is easier to manage using the money actually available after taxes and deductions. Use the appropriate income measure for the specific calculation and understand that a lender's method may differ from a personal budgeting method.

Does a higher down payment always mean I can afford a more expensive home?

Not necessarily. A larger down payment can reduce the loan amount, but it also uses cash that could otherwise support reserves, closing costs or other financial needs.

Can a lender approve more than I want to spend?

Yes. A lender's underwriting assessment and your personal spending budget answer different questions. An approval amount should not automatically be treated as a target purchase price.
