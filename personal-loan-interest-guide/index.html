INTEREST BASICS

What is personal loan interest?

Interest is the cost of borrowing money. On a typical personal installment loan, the borrower receives a loan amount and repays it through scheduled installments. Each payment may be divided among interest, principal, and any amounts due under the agreement. As principal is paid down, the balance used to calculate interest generally becomes smaller on an amortizing simple-interest loan.

The exact method matters. A lender may calculate interest using a daily or monthly simple-interest method, or a contract may use a precomputed finance charge. Do not assume every loan calculates interest in the same way. Review the promissory note, payment schedule, and disclosures.

How interest is calculated on an amortizing loan

For a standard fixed-rate loan with equal monthly payments, a common amortization approach uses the monthly periodic rate and the remaining principal balance. The scheduled payment is designed to cover interest due for the period and reduce principal, so the balance is gradually paid off over the stated term.

For a simplified monthly model:

  • Monthly rate: annual nominal interest rate divided by 12.
  • Interest for the month: opening principal balance multiplied by the monthly rate.
  • Principal paid: scheduled payment minus the interest portion, before any other amounts or adjustments.
  • New balance: opening balance minus the principal paid.

Actual contracts may accrue interest daily, use different day-count conventions, or adjust the amount due for payment timing and rounding. This simplified monthly method is useful for understanding an estimate, not reproducing every lender's payoff quote.

Why early payments usually contain more interest

On a level-payment amortizing loan, the outstanding principal is highest near the beginning. Therefore, the interest portion of an early payment is typically larger. As principal declines, a greater share of each equal payment can go toward principal. The scheduled payment may stay the same even though its interest and principal portions change.

This is why looking only at the monthly payment can be misleading. A lower payment may be the result of a longer term, not a lower overall borrowing cost.

Simple interest vs. precomputed interest

Simple interest: interest is calculated over time based on the outstanding balance, commonly using a daily or monthly method. With a simple-interest method, paying down principal sooner can reduce the balance on which later interest is calculated. The exact result depends on when payments are credited and the agreement's calculation method.

Precomputed interest: the finance charge for the scheduled term is calculated at the start and incorporated into the amount owed or payment schedule. Extra payments may not reduce the precomputed charge in the same way they reduce future interest on a simple-interest loan. A contract and applicable law may provide a rebate of unearned interest when the loan is paid early.

The CFPB explains these differences in its consumer guidance on auto loans. The explanation is useful for understanding the methods, but you should confirm the method and early-payoff treatment in the documents for your specific personal loan.

Fixed vs. variable personal loan rates

A fixed rate generally stays the same for the period specified in the agreement. With a fixed-rate loan and an unchanged payment schedule, the principal-and-interest payment is generally predictable.

A variable or adjustable rate can change according to the contract, such as a specified index plus a margin. If the rate changes, the payment, payoff timeline, or both may change depending on the agreement. Check how often the rate can adjust, any rate caps or floors, the index and margin, and how a change affects payments. CFPB consumer guidance notes that personal installment loan rates may be fixed or adjustable.

Interest rate vs. APR

The interest rate is the rate applied to the loan balance under the contract. The annual percentage rate (APR) expresses the cost of credit as an annual rate and includes certain finance charges as required by applicable rules. APR can therefore be higher than the stated interest rate when applicable finance charges are included.

APR is a useful standardized comparison figure, but do not treat it as a list of every possible cost you might incur. Compare the actual cash you receive, fees, payment amount, term, total of payments, and any charges that may arise during the loan. For closed-end consumer credit, Regulation Z disclosures include items such as amount financed, finance charge, APR, total of payments, and the payment schedule, as applicable.

Loan amount, term, and fees all affect cost

Loan amount: at the same rate and term, a larger principal amount generally produces a larger payment and more total interest.

Interest rate: for the same principal and term, a higher rate generally increases the payment and total interest in a standard amortizing model.

Loan term: spreading repayment over more months can reduce the required monthly payment, but it can increase total interest because the debt remains outstanding longer.

Fees: origination, documentation, late-payment, and optional credit or disability insurance charges can increase borrowing costs. Some origination fees may be deducted from the proceeds, which means the amount you receive can be less than the note amount. CFPB consumer guidance recommends reviewing the lender's loan disclosure and documents for applicable fees.

What does “amount financed” mean?

The amount financed is a disclosure term under Regulation Z. It is not necessarily identical to the face amount or note amount. Its calculation can account for amounts financed and certain prepaid finance charges. For example, the CFPB's Regulation Z guidance illustrates a $2,500 loan with a $40 prepaid loan fee withheld from the proceeds, resulting in an amount financed of $2,460 in that example.

Ask the lender to explain the difference between the amount stated in the note, the amount financed, the cash proceeds delivered to you, and the total of payments. This is particularly important when a fee is deducted before you receive the funds.

Does paying extra reduce personal loan interest?

It may, but you need to understand how the lender applies extra money. On a simple-interest loan, an extra amount credited to principal can reduce the balance on which future interest is calculated. However, a payment sent early may instead be treated as an advance payment toward a future installment unless the servicer applies it to principal under the contract and its procedures.

Before making additional payments, ask whether there is a prepayment penalty, whether extra funds are applied to principal immediately, whether you must give payment instructions, and how to request an accurate payoff quote. With a precomputed-interest loan, ask how any unearned-interest rebate is calculated. Do not assume that making an extra payment automatically changes the due date or reduces the next required payment.

How to read a personal loan offer

  1. Amount you will receive: confirm the net proceeds after any fee withheld at origination.
  2. Interest rate and rate type: identify the nominal rate and whether it is fixed or variable.
  3. APR and finance charge: compare disclosed figures and ask which fees are included.
  4. Payment schedule: check the number, amount, and due dates of installments.
  5. Total of payments: review the scheduled total if every payment is made as agreed.
  6. Interest method: ask whether interest accrues daily or monthly, or is precomputed.
  7. Extra-payment rules: verify how additional amounts are credited and whether any penalty or rebate rules apply.
  8. Late and default terms: review grace periods, late fees, collection provisions, and available hardship options.

Frequently asked questions

Is personal loan interest calculated daily or monthly?

It depends on the lender and agreement. Some simple-interest loans calculate interest daily, while others use a monthly method. Ask which method applies and how payment timing affects interest.

Does a lower monthly payment mean a lower-cost loan?

No. A longer term can reduce the scheduled payment while increasing the total interest paid. Compare the APR, fees, term, and total of payments as well as the monthly amount.

Is APR always higher than the interest rate?

Not necessarily in every situation. APR reflects the interest rate and certain finance charges under applicable rules. When there are no additional finance charges included in the APR, the figures may be the same or close. Review the actual disclosure.

Can I pay off a personal loan early?

The agreement and applicable law determine whether early payoff is allowed and whether a prepayment charge or unearned-interest rebate applies. Request a written payoff quote and ask how the interest is calculated through the payoff date.

Will an extra payment automatically reduce principal?

Not always. Ask the servicer how it handles extra payments, whether you need to specify that the amount should be applied to principal, and whether it instead advances a future due date.

Why is the amount I receive lower than the loan amount?

A lender may deduct an origination or other prepaid fee from the proceeds. Check the itemization and amount-financed disclosure so you understand the note amount, net funds delivered, and total repayment obligation.
