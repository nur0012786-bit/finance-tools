What Is a Mortgage Rate Lock?

Understand mortgage rate locks, lock periods, extensions, float-down options and what can happen when a mortgage rate lock expires.

UNDERSTAND THE BASICS

What is a mortgage rate lock?

A mortgage rate lock (also called a lock-in) is a lender agreement to hold a specified interest rate for a defined period while your mortgage is processed. It can protect you from market-rate increases during that period, provided you satisfy the agreement's conditions and close before the lock expires.

A rate lock is not the same as final loan approval. Your lender still has to verify your application, finances, property and loan-program requirements. Read the written lock agreement rather than relying on a verbal statement that your rate is "locked."

Locked rate vs. floating rate

A locked rate is held for the period and under the conditions in the lock agreement. A floating rate is not locked, so it can move as market pricing changes before you lock it. Floating can work in your favor if rates fall, but it also exposes you to increases. Nobody can reliably guarantee which direction rates will move.

The CFPB says rate locks are commonly offered for 30, 45 or 60 days, and sometimes longer. Those are examples, not a universal standard or a promise that every lender offers every term. A longer lock may be priced differently from a shorter one.

Does receiving a Loan Estimate mean your rate is locked?

No. Some lenders lock a rate when they issue a Loan Estimate; others issue the estimate while the rate is still floating. Look near the top of page 1 for the "Rate Lock" information. If the rate is locked, check the stated expiration date and time. The disclosure rules require the expiration date and time, including the applicable time zone, for a locked rate.

Do not confuse the rate-lock expiration with the separate expiration of estimated closing costs shown on the Loan Estimate. Ask the lender to explain both dates and what action you must take to keep the offer valid.

What can still change a locked rate?

A rate lock generally protects against ordinary market movement, not every change to the transaction. The CFPB identifies examples that can affect the rate even after a lock is in place:

  • You change the loan type, loan amount or down payment.
  • The appraisal comes in materially different from the value used when pricing the loan.
  • Your credit score or credit profile changes, such as after taking on new debt or missing a payment.
  • The lender cannot verify income that was used to qualify you, such as overtime or a bonus.

These examples do not mean every change automatically alters the rate. The effect depends on the lender's pricing rules, loan program and written agreement. Before changing your application, ask the lender whether the change will require the loan to be repriced or the lock to be amended.

What happens when a rate lock expires?

If the loan has not closed by the deadline, the original lock may no longer protect the rate. Depending on the lender's policy and the agreement, you may be able to pay for an extension, obtain a new lock at then-current pricing, or face other options. An extension is not automatic, and the lender may have conditions or limits on granting one.

Extension fees and policies vary. Ask for the extension price, how many days it buys, whether the lender can refuse it, and who is responsible if the delay is caused by the lender, the borrower, the property or another party. Get the answer in writing before the expiration date.

What if market rates fall after you lock?

A standard rate lock can also prevent you from automatically receiving a lower market rate if rates fall before closing. Some lenders may offer a float-down or repricing option, which may allow the rate to be reduced under specified conditions. It is not a universal borrower right. Ask whether the option exists, when it can be used, what rate drop triggers it, whether a fee applies and whether it can be exercised more than once.

Compare the complete written terms. A lower advertised rate may require additional points or fees, and a float-down feature may have restrictions that make it less valuable than it first appears.

Illustrative rate-lock example

Suppose a lender issues a 45-day lock and your scheduled closing is close to the end of that period. The purchase is delayed and closing is now expected after the lock expires. Do not assume the lender will extend the original rate automatically. Contact the lender before expiration, request the written extension cost and compare it with the alternative pricing available at that time.

The example is conceptual. It does not estimate a fee or predict future rates; those depend on the lender, loan details and market conditions.

Rate-lock checklist before you agree

  • Confirm the exact interest rate, loan program, loan amount, points and lender credits covered by the lock.
  • Get the expiration date, exact time and time zone in writing.
  • Ask whether there is an upfront lock fee and whether a longer lock changes pricing.
  • Ask what happens if closing is delayed, the cost of an extension, and whether an extension can be denied.
  • Ask which changes to your credit, income, down payment, property or loan request can affect pricing.
  • Ask what happens if market rates fall and whether a float-down or repricing option is available.
  • Keep copies of the Loan Estimate, lock confirmation and any later revised disclosures.

Questions to ask your loan officer

Use these questions and request clear answers in writing:

  • "Is my rate locked now, or is this Loan Estimate based on a floating rate?"
  • "What exact date, time and time zone does my lock expire?"
  • "Are the points and lender credits locked too, or can they change?"
  • "What would an extension cost, how long would it last, and can it be refused?"
  • "What changes to my application or property could cause the rate to be repriced?"
  • "If market rates fall, do you offer a float-down or one-time repricing option?"

Frequently asked questions

How long does a mortgage rate lock usually last?

The CFPB describes 30-, 45- and 60-day locks as typical, with longer periods sometimes available. Your lender's written agreement controls the actual period and expiration.

Is a rate lock free?

Not always. A lender may charge a fee or price different lock periods differently. Ask for the cost of each available period and whether any upfront fee is refundable or credited at closing.

Can a lender change my rate after I lock it?

A lock generally protects against market-rate movement during the agreed period, but changes to the application or transaction can affect pricing under the agreement. Review the lender's conditions and ask before making changes.

Can I lock my rate after receiving a Loan Estimate?

Yes, depending on the lender's process. A Loan Estimate may show a locked or floating rate. Confirm the status with the lender and obtain written lock terms.

What should I do if my closing will happen after the lock expires?

Contact your lender before expiration. Ask whether an extension is available, the fee and added time, and what happens if the extension is not approved. Do not assume the original rate will continue.

Will I automatically get a lower rate if mortgage rates fall?

No. A standard lock can keep you from automatically receiving lower market pricing. Ask whether the lender offers a float-down or repricing option and read its restrictions.

Does a rate lock mean my mortgage is approved?

No. A rate lock is about interest-rate terms for a stated period. It does not replace underwriting, verification, appraisal, title review or final approval.

Where can I check whether the rate is locked?

Review the Rate Lock section near the top of page 1 of your Loan Estimate and compare it with the separate written lock confirmation. Ask the lender to resolve any inconsistency.
