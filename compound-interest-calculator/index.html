What is compound interest?

Compound interest means you can earn returns on both your original money and the interest that has accumulated. Over longer periods, this compounding effect can become a significant part of your account growth.

For example, if you start with $10,000, contribute $300 each month and earn an average 7% annual return for 20 years, the account can grow substantially because contributions and accumulated returns continue working over time.

Why regular contributions matter

Adding money consistently can increase your future balance because each contribution gets more time to potentially earn returns. The earlier money is invested, the more opportunity it has to compound.

Compound interest formula

A basic compound interest calculation can be represented as A = P(1 + r/n)nt, where P is the principal, r is the annual rate, n is the number of compounding periods per year, and t is the number of years. Regular contributions require an additional contribution calculation, so this calculator models monthly deposits separately.

Frequently asked questions

What is compound interest?

Compound interest is growth calculated on your original balance plus previously accumulated interest or returns.

Does this calculator include monthly contributions?

Yes. You can enter a monthly contribution and see how those deposits affect the estimated future value.

What compounding frequency should I choose?

Choose the frequency that matches the rate assumption you are using. Daily, monthly, quarterly and annual options are available.

Are investment returns guaranteed?

No. Investment returns are not guaranteed. This calculator is a mathematical estimate and does not predict actual market performance.

Does this include taxes and fees?

No. Taxes, investment fees, account fees and other costs are not included unless you account for them separately in your assumptions.

Can I use this calculator for retirement planning?

You can use it for a rough growth illustration, but retirement planning should also consider inflation, taxes, fees, changing contributions and investment risk.
