AUTO LOAN GUIDE

How does an auto loan calculator work?

This calculator estimates the amount financed, scheduled monthly payment, total scheduled interest and total of payments from the vehicle price, cash down payment, trade-in value and payoff, financed fees, estimated sales tax, contract interest rate and loan term. It uses a standard fixed-rate monthly amortization model.

How the amount financed is estimated

The basic estimate is:

Vehicle price + estimated sales tax + financed fees − cash down payment − net trade-in equity

Net trade-in equity is the trade-in value minus the payoff on the existing vehicle loan. Positive equity reduces the new loan. Negative equity increases the amount that must be financed if it is rolled into the deal. Always verify the actual trade-in value and payoff amount separately.

The calculator's amount financed is a planning estimate, not a substitute for the amount financed disclosed in the final retail installment contract. Actual contracts can include additional taxes, fees, optional products or other adjustments.

How the monthly payment is calculated

For a standard fixed-rate loan with monthly payments, the calculator uses the contract interest rate and number of monthly payments to estimate principal and interest. It does not use APR as a direct replacement for the contract rate. APR is a broader credit-cost disclosure that may include certain finance charges.

When the interest rate is zero, the amount financed is divided evenly across the scheduled payments. For other rates, the standard amortization formula is used. Actual lenders may accrue interest daily, round installments or apply contract-specific rules, so the lender's payment schedule controls the actual payment.

Sales tax and trade-in credits

Sales-tax rules vary by state and locality. Some jurisdictions calculate tax on the full vehicle price, while others allow an eligible trade-in allowance to reduce the taxable amount. This calculator uses the full vehicle price by default. You can select the trade-in tax-credit assumption only if your state and transaction qualify.

The tax-credit option subtracts the trade-in value—not net trade-in equity—from the taxable vehicle price. It does not determine state eligibility, tax caps, private-party rules or other local exceptions. Confirm the taxable amount with your state revenue authority or dealer's itemized buyer's order.

Why trade-in payoff matters

If the vehicle you trade in still has a loan, the new transaction must account for the lender's payoff. A trade-in worth $12,000 with a $9,000 payoff has $3,000 in positive equity. A trade-in worth $15,000 with an $18,000 payoff has $3,000 in negative equity. If the negative equity is rolled into the new loan, it increases the amount financed and the interest you may pay.

Do not assume a dealer's promise to "pay off" the old loan makes a shortfall disappear. Check how the payoff, trade-in allowance and any negative equity appear in the written contract.

Financed fees and add-ons

Enter only the fees and optional products that will actually be added to the new loan. Do not include amounts you will pay separately in cash. Optional warranties, GAP products, credit insurance, dealer-installed items and other add-ons can increase the amount financed and total repayment cost. Ask whether each item is optional and review its price, coverage, exclusions and cancellation rules.

What this calculator does not include

The calculator does not automatically estimate state-specific title, registration, documentation, dealer or delivery charges; automobile insurance; fuel; maintenance; repairs; parking; or other ownership costs. Include any eligible amount in "Financed fees and add-ons" only when it will be financed. The actual transaction may also use state-specific tax calculations or loan terms not represented here.

Compare more than the monthly payment

A longer term can lower the scheduled monthly payment but generally increases the number of months you pay interest. Compare the amount financed, contract interest rate, APR, loan term, monthly payment, total interest and total of payments. Check the Truth in Lending disclosure and retail installment contract before signing.

Auto loan calculation example

Try a $30,000 vehicle, $5,000 cash down, no trade-in, no financed fees, a 7% annual contract interest rate and a 5-year term. With zero sales tax entered, the estimated amount financed is $25,000 and the monthly principal-and-interest payment is about $495.03.

Then test a trade-in with an outstanding payoff. For example, a $12,000 trade-in value and a $14,000 payoff create $2,000 of negative equity. With all other inputs unchanged, that shortfall adds to the amount financed if it is rolled into the transaction.

Frequently asked questions

Does this calculator use APR or interest rate?

It uses the annual contract interest rate for the standard monthly payment calculation. APR is a broader disclosure measure that may include certain finance charges. Compare the official APR and amount financed on the lender's disclosure.

Does it include the payoff on my current trade-in?

Yes. Enter the trade-in value and the amount needed to pay off its current loan. The calculator subtracts net positive equity or adds negative equity to the estimated amount financed.

Does the sales-tax credit always apply to a trade-in?

No. Tax treatment varies by state and locality. The option is an assumption for users who have confirmed that a trade-in tax credit applies to their transaction.

Does the calculator include dealer fees?

Only if you enter fees or add-ons that will be financed. Enter financed costs only; do not include fees that you will pay separately in cash.

Does it include car insurance?

No. Auto insurance, fuel, maintenance, repairs and other vehicle ownership expenses are separate from the estimated loan payment.

Why might the lender's payment be different?

The lender may use daily interest, different payment dates, rounding, additional financed amounts or contract provisions not included in this simplified monthly model. The written loan contract and payment schedule control.

Can a longer loan term lower the payment?

Generally, yes, but it usually means paying interest over more months and can increase the total cost. Compare total of payments, not only the monthly amount.

Does a larger down payment guarantee a lower APR?

No. A larger down payment reduces the amount financed, but the APR depends on the credit terms and applicable finance charges. Lender pricing and approval also depend on other factors.
