Personal Loan Fees Explained: Common Costs to Check

PERSONAL LOAN COSTS

Why personal loan fees matter

The interest rate is not the only possible cost of a personal loan. A lender may charge fees for originating or processing the loan, and your agreement may specify charges that apply if a payment is late, returned or the loan is secured by collateral. Optional insurance or other add-on products may also be offered.

Fees can affect how much money you receive, the amount you must repay, the APR and the total cost of borrowing. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) advises borrowers to review the loan disclosure and documents before agreeing to a personal installment loan, and to compare offers from multiple lenders. See the CFPB's overview of personal installment loan fees.

Origination fees

An origination fee is a charge some lenders assess for processing or originating a loan. It may be a flat amount or a percentage of the principal. The lender may deduct it from the proceeds before sending the funds, or include it in another part of the transaction. The offer and agreement should explain the amount and how it is collected.

Example: Suppose the contract principal is $10,000 and the lender charges a 5% origination fee. The fee is $500. If the lender deducts it from the proceeds, you receive approximately $9,500, even though the repayment schedule is generally based on the $10,000 principal plus applicable interest. The fee therefore reduces the cash you receive without necessarily reducing the principal you owe.

When comparing offers, look at both the gross loan amount and the net proceeds. If you need a specific amount to pay a bill or consolidate accounts, confirm that the money you will actually receive is sufficient after fees and any required payoff disbursements.

Documentation, application and credit-report fees

Some lenders may list a documentation or processing fee for preparing loan paperwork. Depending on the lender and product, application or credit-report charges may also be disclosed. Ask what service each fee covers, who receives the money, when it is due, whether it is refundable if the application is declined or withdrawn, and whether it is deducted from the loan proceeds or paid separately.

Do not assume a fee is legitimate merely because it has a formal-sounding name. It should be clearly disclosed, consistent with the lender's written terms and permitted by applicable law. Verify an unfamiliar lender and do not send money to an individual or an unrelated account based only on an email, text or phone call.

Late-payment fees

A loan agreement may provide for a late fee when a required payment is not received by the stated due date or within any contractual grace period. The amount, timing and conditions vary by lender, loan type and applicable state law. A late payment can also lead to additional interest or collection activity, and the lender may report payment history to credit bureaus.

Before accepting the loan, confirm the due date, payment cutoff time, accepted payment methods, any grace period and how the late fee is calculated. If you expect to miss a payment, contact the lender as soon as possible and ask whether a hardship arrangement or revised payment plan is available. The CFPB notes that options may include deferment, forbearance or a payment plan, depending on the lender and loan. Review the CFPB's personal installment loan guidance.

Returned-payment and other contract charges

A returned-payment fee may apply if an electronic debit or check is rejected, for example because funds are unavailable or an account detail is incorrect. Whether a charge applies and how much can be assessed depend on the agreement and applicable law. Repeated failed debits can also create problems with your bank account, so check the payment date and funding account before scheduling automatic payments.

Other costs can apply to a particular loan, especially if it is secured or has unusual repayment terms. Ask the lender to identify every fee in writing and the event that triggers it. Do not rely on a verbal statement that a charge is “standard” or “only a formality.”

Credit insurance and disability insurance

A lender may offer credit life, credit disability or other insurance designed to make specified loan payments or repay some debt if a covered event occurs. The CFPB identifies credit insurance and disability insurance as generally optional fees on personal installment loans. The policy may have exclusions, waiting periods, coverage limits and eligibility rules, so the name of the product alone does not tell you what it covers.

Ask whether the product is optional, whether declining it affects the loan approval or interest rate, what the premium costs over the full term, whether the premium is paid upfront or monthly, and how to cancel it. Do not accept an insurance product simply because it is presented with the loan paperwork. Request the policy and cost in writing before agreeing.

Insurance charges can also affect APR treatment. Under federal Truth in Lending rules, certain credit-insurance premiums are generally included in the finance charge unless specified disclosure and affirmative opt-in conditions are met. Ask the lender to explain whether the premium is included in the disclosed APR or financed separately. See the Federal Reserve's Truth in Lending Act provisions on finance charges.

Non-filing insurance and secured loans

Some secured personal loans may involve non-filing insurance or another charge connected with the lender's collateral requirements. This is not a fee every personal-loan borrower should expect. Ask what collateral secures the loan, why the charge is required, who provides the coverage, who benefits from it and whether an alternative is available.

Remember that a secured loan puts the pledged asset at risk if you default, subject to the agreement and applicable law. A lower interest rate does not automatically make a secured offer preferable to an unsecured loan. Read the collateral and default provisions carefully.

Prepayment charges and early payoff

Some personal loans allow you to pay extra or repay the balance early without a penalty, while others may include a charge or a specific method for calculating the payoff amount. Do not assume early repayment is free. Check the contract for any prepayment penalty, rebate of unearned finance charges, or conditions on how extra payments are applied.

Before sending a final payment, request a payoff amount good through a specific date. The payoff may include interest accrued since the last scheduled payment and other amounts due under the agreement. Confirm how to receive written confirmation that the loan has been satisfied.

Which fees may be included in APR?

APR is intended to provide an annualized measure of the cost of credit, but it does not necessarily include every charge you could ever pay. Under the Truth in Lending Act, a finance charge generally includes charges imposed by the creditor as an incident to extending credit, subject to statutory and regulatory exceptions. Loan fees and some credit-report or insurance charges can be finance charges, while other amounts may be treated differently based on their purpose and how they are imposed.

For that reason, do not assume that every item called a “fee” is automatically included in APR—or automatically excluded. Ask the lender to identify the finance charges used to calculate APR and list the separate charges that are not included. Review the APR, amount financed, total of payments and fee schedule together. The Federal Reserve's summary of TILA Section 106, Determination of Finance Charge describes the general rule and examples of charges that may be included.

Compare loan offers using the same assumptions

Ask more than one lender for a written offer based on the same requested net amount and a similar repayment term. Then compare:

  • Origination fee: Amount, percentage, collection method and refund rules.
  • Net proceeds: The amount that will actually reach you after deductions and any creditor payoffs.
  • APR and interest rate: Whether the rates are fixed or variable, and which finance charges are included in APR.
  • Payment and term: Scheduled amount, first due date, number of payments and total of payments.
  • Optional products: Insurance or add-ons, their cost, coverage, cancellation process and APR treatment.
  • Late and returned-payment charges: Trigger, amount, grace period and payment methods.
  • Prepayment terms: Whether a penalty applies and how payoff amounts are calculated.
  • Collateral: Any asset pledged and the consequences of default.

A loan with a lower interest rate can still cost more if it has a substantial origination fee or a longer term. A lower monthly payment can also reflect repayment over more months. Compare the complete disclosed cost, not just the advertised rate or one fee.

Recognize advance-fee loan scams

Be cautious if someone promises guaranteed approval, says your credit history does not matter, pressures you to act immediately or demands payment in exchange for a promise that you will receive a loan. The FTC warns that scammers may describe the requested payment as an insurance fee, processing fee or paperwork charge and then never provide the promised loan.

There is an important distinction: legitimate lenders may disclose application, credit-report or appraisal fees as part of a real application process. But paying a fee should never be treated as a guarantee of approval or funding. Before paying any unfamiliar charge, verify the company through its official contact details, read the written disclosures, confirm what service the charge covers and check whether it is permitted in your state. Do not pay by gift card, cryptocurrency or wire transfer to an unknown person for a supposed loan approval.

Read the FTC's guidance on advance-fee loan scams. If you believe you have encountered a scam, report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

Questions to ask before signing

  • What is the full list of fees, and when is each one charged?
  • Which fees are deducted from proceeds, paid upfront, financed or billed later?
  • What net amount will I receive, and what principal amount must I repay?
  • Which charges are included in APR, and which are separate?
  • Is credit or disability insurance optional, and how do I decline it?
  • What happens if a payment is late or returned?
  • Can I make extra payments or repay early without a penalty?
  • Is any asset being used as collateral?
  • Can I receive the final fee schedule and loan agreement before I accept?

Frequently asked questions

Do all personal loans have an origination fee?

No. Some lenders charge an origination fee and others do not. Compare the APR, net proceeds and total repayment cost rather than assuming a no-fee offer is automatically cheaper.

Is an origination fee deducted from the loan amount?

It may be deducted from the proceeds, but the method depends on the lender and agreement. Confirm the gross principal, fee amount and net deposit before accepting.

Are credit insurance and disability insurance required?

The CFPB describes these products as generally optional on personal installment loans. Ask whether the lender requires any specific coverage, whether declining it changes the offer, and what the product costs and covers.

Are personal loan fees included in APR?

Some fees that qualify as finance charges are included in APR under Truth in Lending rules. Other charges may be excluded. Ask the lender to identify the finance charges included in the APR calculation and review the written disclosures.

Can a lender charge a late fee?

A lender may charge a late fee when the agreement and applicable law permit it. Check the trigger, amount, due date and any grace period in the loan documents.

Can I repay a personal loan early?

It depends on the loan terms and applicable law. Check for a prepayment penalty and ask the lender how extra payments are applied. Request a payoff quote before the final payment.

Is an upfront loan fee always a scam?

No. Legitimate application-related fees can exist and should be clearly disclosed. A demand that you pay in exchange for a guarantee of approval or a promised loan is a major warning sign. Verify the company and the charge before paying.

What if I cannot afford a payment?

Contact the lender as early as possible and ask about hardship options. Confirm any payment reduction, deferment, fees, continued interest and revised schedule in writing.

What documents should I read before accepting?

Read the loan disclosure, fee schedule, promissory note or loan agreement, and any separate insurance or add-on policy. Make sure the net proceeds, APR, payment amount, term and fee treatment match what you were told.
