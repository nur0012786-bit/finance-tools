DOWN PAYMENT BASICS

What is a down payment?

A down payment is the portion of a home's purchase price that the buyer pays upfront rather than financing through the mortgage. The remaining eligible amount is generally financed with a mortgage, subject to the loan program and lender requirements.

For a hypothetical $400,000 home, a 10% down payment would be $40,000, leaving $360,000 to finance before considering other transaction adjustments. This example is illustrative.

Is 20% down required to buy a house?

No single down-payment percentage applies to every US home purchase. Minimum requirements can vary by loan program, lender, property type, occupancy, credit profile and borrower circumstances. A 20% down payment is a common planning benchmark, not a universal requirement.

Some eligible conventional borrowers may qualify with a down payment as low as 3%. FHA financing can permit a 3.5% minimum required investment for eligible borrowers. Eligible VA and USDA borrowers may be able to buy with no down payment, subject to the program rules and lender approval. A low or zero down payment does not mean zero cash is needed: closing costs, prepaid expenses, reserves and other purchase costs can remain.

Low-down-payment and no-down-payment loan examples

Loan programs have different eligibility rules and costs. The following examples are starting points for questions to ask—not a substitute for a lender's review of your credit, income, property and application.

  • Conventional mortgages: Some eligible conventional programs allow a down payment as low as 3%. Borrowers putting less than 20% down commonly pay private mortgage insurance (PMI), although lender-paid or other pricing structures may be offered. Check the rate and total costs rather than assuming a low-down-payment offer is cheaper.
  • FHA loans: FHA's standard minimum required investment can be 3.5% for eligible borrowers with qualifying credit and underwriting. FHA mortgage insurance premiums (MIP) are different from conventional PMI and can continue under different rules. See the HUD FHA loan overview and ask an FHA-approved lender about current requirements.
  • VA-backed purchase loans: Eligible borrowers may be able to buy with no down payment when the purchase price does not exceed the VA reasonable value and program, entitlement and lender requirements are met. VA does not require monthly PMI, but a one-time funding fee may apply unless the borrower is exempt. A lender may still require a down payment in some cases. See the VA purchase-loan rules.
  • USDA guaranteed loans: Eligible households buying an eligible primary residence in a qualifying rural area may receive 100% financing through an approved lender. Income, occupancy, property and other program requirements apply. USDA financing can still involve upfront and annual guarantee fees, closing costs and prepaid expenses. See the USDA Guaranteed Loan Program.

Compare the total monthly payment and total borrowing cost across eligible programs. A lower down payment can preserve cash but may increase mortgage insurance, program fees or interest costs. A larger down payment can lower the loan amount but may use money needed for reserves.

How does a down payment affect your mortgage?

For the same purchase price, a larger down payment generally means a smaller starting loan balance. A smaller balance can reduce the scheduled principal-and-interest payment and the amount of interest charged over the life of the loan, assuming the other loan terms are unchanged.

A down payment can also affect loan-to-value ratio and, for some conventional loans, whether borrower-paid PMI applies.

Down payment and mortgage insurance

With many conventional mortgages, a down payment below 20% can result in borrower-paid private mortgage insurance (PMI). PMI protects the lender, not the borrower, if the borrower stops making payments. It adds to the cost of homeownership and can be structured as a monthly premium, an upfront premium or a combination, depending on the loan offer.

For many conventional mortgages covered by the Homeowners Protection Act, you can generally request PMI cancellation when the principal balance is scheduled to reach 80% of the home's original value, subject to conditions such as a good payment history, being current and having no disqualifying junior liens. PMI is generally scheduled to terminate automatically when the balance is scheduled to reach 78% of original value if you are current. These rules do not apply in the same way to FHA or VA mortgage insurance. Check the applicable disclosures and ask your servicer about your loan.

FHA loans use mortgage insurance premiums (MIP), with different duration and cancellation rules. VA purchase loans do not require monthly PMI, while USDA guaranteed loans have guarantee fees. Do not treat all mortgage insurance as interchangeable.

Down payment vs. closing costs

The down payment is applied toward the purchase price. Closing costs are separate transaction expenses that can include lender, title, settlement, government, prepaid and escrow-related items. The CFPB says closing costs, not including the down payment, typically range from 2% to 5% of the purchase price, but the actual amount depends on the home, location, loan and lender.

For that reason, a buyer who plans to make a $40,000 down payment on a $400,000 home should not assume $40,000 is the total cash required at closing. A rough 2%–5% closing-cost range on that example would be another $8,000–$20,000 before accounting for credits, deposits or other adjustments.

Estimate cash to close before choosing a percentage

A practical starting estimate is:

Cash needed at closing = down payment + closing costs + prepaid expenses and initial escrow deposits − eligible credits and deposits already paid.

The final amount is shown in the Closing Disclosure and can differ from an early estimate. Earnest money paid with the purchase offer is generally shown as a deposit credited toward the transaction if it is retained and applied under the contract. It is not automatically an additional down payment on top of the contract's total cash-to-close amount. Review the purchase contract and Closing Disclosure to see how it is handled.

For example, on a $400,000 home with 10% down, the down payment is $40,000. If estimated closing costs and prepaids total $12,000 and you have already paid a $5,000 earnest-money deposit that is credited at closing, the remaining estimated cash needed would be $47,000 ($40,000 + $12,000 − $5,000), before any other credits or adjustments. This is an illustration, not a quote.

How much cash should you keep after buying?

The amount of cash to keep available after closing depends on personal circumstances and is not a universal percentage. Buyers may need to account for emergency savings, moving costs, repairs, maintenance, insurance deductibles, utility deposits and other obligations.

The CFPB suggests setting aside an emergency cushion—using three to six months of expenses as a general rule of thumb—and subtracting planned moving, renovation and other savings needs before deciding how much cash is available for closing. That is a planning guideline, not a requirement that fits every household.

Using every available dollar for a larger down payment may change the mortgage payment, but it can also leave less liquidity after the purchase. Consider the complete cash-flow picture before choosing a down payment. Review the CFPB's down-payment planning steps.

Gift funds and down-payment assistance

Some buyers use an eligible family gift, grant or down-payment assistance program to help cover the upfront costs. Do not move money into a mortgage transaction without first telling the lender where it came from. The lender must determine whether the source is permitted for the selected program and how it must be documented.

For example, Fannie Mae permits personal gift funds from acceptable donors for eligible principal-residence and second-home transactions, subject to the program's minimum borrower-contribution rules. A gift letter and documentation of the donor's funds and transfer may be required. A gift is not the same as an undisclosed loan that the borrower must repay. Rules differ by loan program, so confirm the donor, amount, permitted use and required records with the lender before the funds are transferred. See the Fannie Mae Selling Guide section on personal gifts.

State and local housing agencies, municipalities and nonprofit organizations may offer down-payment assistance, grants or special second mortgages. These programs can have income limits, purchase-price limits, geographic restrictions, homebuyer education requirements, occupancy rules and repayment or forgiveness conditions. Some assistance is a loan secured by the home, not free money. Start with your state or local housing finance agency and a HUD-approved housing counselor; the CFPB also describes possible down-payment funding sources.

Be careful about borrowing the down payment through a personal loan, credit card cash advance or other undisclosed debt. New borrowing can increase your monthly obligations, affect mortgage qualification and may not be an eligible source of funds. Tell the lender about all financing and follow its sourcing instructions.

How interest rate and loan term fit into the decision

The down payment should be evaluated together with the mortgage rate and term. A smaller loan at one rate and term can have a different payment and total interest cost from a larger loan at another rate or term.

Worked example

Imagine a hypothetical $350,000 purchase. At 10% down, the down payment would be $35,000 and the starting mortgage amount would be approximately $315,000 before other adjustments. At 20% down, the down payment would be $70,000 and the starting mortgage would be approximately $280,000.

The 20% scenario requires another $35,000 upfront but starts with a $35,000 smaller mortgage. Whether that trade-off makes sense depends on the buyer's cash reserves, loan terms, PMI requirements and other financial considerations.

Questions to ask before choosing a down payment

  • What is the minimum down payment for the loan program?
  • Will PMI or another mortgage-insurance cost apply?
  • How much cash is needed for closing costs and prepaid items?
  • How much money will remain available after closing?
  • How does the down payment change the monthly mortgage payment?
  • How does it change the total interest under the quoted loan terms?
  • Are there lender or program-specific requirements that affect the choice?

Frequently asked questions

Can I buy a house with less than 20% down?

Yes, some borrowers qualify for mortgages with less than 20% down. The minimum depends on the loan program, lender, property and borrower circumstances.

Does a larger down payment always mean a lower monthly payment?

For the same purchase price, rate and term, a larger down payment generally produces a smaller loan balance and therefore a lower principal-and-interest payment. Other costs can still vary.

Is the down payment part of closing costs?

No. The down payment is a separate part of the purchase transaction, although both the down payment and closing costs can contribute to the total cash needed at closing.

Does a 20% down payment always eliminate mortgage insurance?

A 20% down payment generally avoids borrower-paid PMI on a standard conventional mortgage, but other mortgage programs have different insurance or guarantee-fee rules. Confirm the loan type and pricing structure. Some conventional offers use lender-paid mortgage insurance or a higher interest rate instead.

Can I use a gift for my down payment?

Some loan programs allow documented gift funds from eligible donors. The lender may require a gift letter and evidence of the source and transfer. Tell the lender before the funds move; the rules vary by program and gifts cannot simply be treated as an undisclosed repayable loan.

Can I get help with my down payment?

Some state, local and nonprofit programs offer grants, forgivable assistance or second mortgages to eligible buyers. Availability, income limits, repayment terms and property requirements vary. Check the program documents and ask whether assistance creates a lien or repayment obligation.

Can I buy a home with no down payment?

Some eligible VA and USDA borrowers may qualify for no-down-payment financing. These programs have specific service, income, property, location, occupancy and lender requirements. Even when a down payment is not required, closing costs, prepaid expenses and other cash needs may remain.

Should I use all my savings for a down payment?

There is no universal answer. Compare the mortgage benefits of a larger down payment with the importance of retaining cash for emergencies, repairs, moving costs and other obligations.
