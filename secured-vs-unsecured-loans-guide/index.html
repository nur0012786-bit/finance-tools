Secured vs. Unsecured Loans: What's the Difference?

Learn how secured and unsecured loans differ, how collateral affects borrowing, and what to compare before taking out a loan.

Educational finance guidance

A secured loan is generally backed by collateral, while an unsecured loan does not rely on a specific asset as security. The exact terms depend on the lender and loan agreement.

What is a secured loan?

A secured loan is backed by collateral: an asset the borrower pledges to support repayment. Depending on the agreement and applicable law, the lender may have a security interest in that asset. Common examples include mortgages secured by a home and auto loans secured by the vehicle. Some personal loans are also secured by savings, a vehicle, or another eligible asset.

Collateral gives the lender a potential recovery source if the borrower defaults. It also puts something the borrower owns at risk. Before signing, identify exactly which asset secures the debt, whether any other property or account is included, and what events count as default.

What is an unsecured loan?

An unsecured loan is not backed by a specific asset pledged as collateral for that loan. Lenders generally assess the borrower's credit history, income, existing debt, and other underwriting factors. Many personal loans and most traditional credit card balances are examples, although product terms vary.

Unsecured does not mean there are no consequences for missing payments. A lender or debt collector may pursue lawful collection activity and may sue to obtain a judgment. Depending on the judgment and state law, collection remedies can include wage garnishment, a bank-account levy, or a property lien. A creditor cannot simply repossess a particular asset under a security agreement that does not exist.

How collateral changes the risk

With a secured loan, you are taking on two linked risks: the repayment obligation and the possibility of losing the asset pledged to support it. For a home-secured loan, that asset may be your home. For an auto loan, it may be the vehicle you rely on for work or daily transportation.

With an unsecured loan, the lender does not have a specific pledged asset to repossess under that loan. However, the debt remains a legal obligation. If a creditor obtains a court judgment, state law may allow certain collection tools, subject to exemptions and other protections. Do not treat an unsecured loan as harmless or consequence-free.

Can a secured loan still leave you owing money after repossession?

Yes. If the lender takes and sells collateral, the sale proceeds may not cover the outstanding balance and permitted costs. The remaining amount is commonly called a deficiency balance. Whether a lender can collect it, and what notices or procedures are required, depends on the type of loan and applicable law.

Illustrative auto-loan example: Suppose the outstanding loan balance is $10,000 and the repossessed vehicle is sold for $7,500. The basic shortfall is $2,500 before any additional permitted repossession costs. The borrower may still owe that deficiency. If the sale produces more than the debt and costs, the borrower may instead be entitled to a surplus under applicable rules. This example is not a prediction of what will happen in every state or contract.

Repossession procedures vary by state. For example, the rules on advance notice, reinstating a loan, the sale process, and recovery of personal belongings can differ. Read notices promptly and contact your state attorney general, a legal-aid organization, or a qualified attorney about a specific dispute.

Does a secured loan always have a lower interest rate?

No. Collateral can reduce a lender's potential loss, and some secured products may offer lower rates than comparable unsecured products. But a secured loan is not automatically cheaper. The rate depends on the lender, the collateral, its value, the borrower's credit and income, the loan amount, the term, and the fees.

Compare the APR, fees, total of payments, monthly payment, repayment term, and collateral risk together. A lower advertised interest rate can still produce a more expensive loan if it comes with substantial fees or a longer repayment period. Use the actual written offers rather than relying on a general rule about secured and unsecured borrowing.

How to compare two loan offers

  1. Confirm the amount you will actually receive. Check whether an origination fee or another charge is deducted from the proceeds or added to the balance.
  2. Compare APR and interest rate. They are not interchangeable. Review the lender's required disclosures and understand which fees are included in the APR calculation.
  3. Compare the full repayment. Record the monthly payment, number of payments, total scheduled payments, and any balloon or final payment.
  4. Read the collateral clause. Identify the asset, lien, any cross-collateralization language, insurance requirements, and the events that could trigger default.
  5. Check hardship and default terms. Ask what happens after a missed payment, how to request a payment change, and what fees or enforcement steps may apply.
  6. Choose a payment you can sustain. Stress-test the budget for reduced income, repairs, medical costs, or other unexpected expenses before putting an essential asset at risk.

Questions to ask before pledging an asset

  • Exactly what property or account secures this loan?
  • Will the lender place a lien, and when will it be released after payoff?
  • Can the agreement secure other debts with the same lender?
  • What counts as default, including missed payments or insurance lapses?
  • What notice, cure, or reinstatement rights apply in my state?
  • Could I owe a deficiency after the collateral is sold?
  • Are there origination, appraisal, title, filing, late, or repossession fees?
  • Can I repay early, and are there any prepayment charges?

When might each type make sense?

A secured loan may be appropriate when the purpose requires financing tied to an asset, the terms are meaningfully better after all costs are compared, and you can accept the risk to the collateral. A mortgage and a typical vehicle purchase loan are common examples of asset-backed borrowing.

An unsecured loan may be worth comparing when you do not want to pledge an asset and the payment and total cost fit your budget. Approval and pricing may be less favorable for some applicants, and the lender can still use lawful collection procedures if the debt is not paid.

Neither category is automatically the better choice. Do not pledge a home, vehicle, savings balance, or other essential asset solely to obtain a lower advertised payment. Consider what losing that asset would mean for your housing, employment, and financial stability.

Frequently asked questions

Is it easier to qualify for a secured loan?

Collateral can change a lender's risk assessment, but it does not guarantee approval. Lenders may still review credit, income, debt obligations, collateral value, and other requirements.

Can an unsecured lender take my house or car?

An unsecured loan does not give the lender a security interest in a specific asset under that loan. But if the creditor obtains a court judgment, state law may permit certain collection remedies, potentially including a lien or levy. The process and exemptions depend on the facts and jurisdiction.

Can a secured lender take more than the collateral?

The lender's rights depend on the contract and applicable law. If the sale proceeds are less than the balance and permitted costs, a deficiency may remain. Do not assume surrendering the asset automatically cancels the debt.

Are mortgages and auto loans secured?

They are commonly secured by the home or vehicle being financed. Review the specific security agreement and loan documents to confirm which asset is pledged.

Does paying off a secured loan remove the lien?

The lender generally must release its security interest when the loan is satisfied, but the steps and timing depend on the asset, documents, and applicable state procedures. Confirm that the lien release or title update is completed.

What should I do if I may miss a secured-loan payment?

Contact the lender as early as possible, ask about hardship or payment options, and get any agreed changes in writing. Do not pay an outside company that guarantees it can stop repossession or erase a debt in exchange for an upfront fee.
