How Long Does a Mortgage Approval Take?

FROM APPLICATION TO CLOSING

How long does mortgage approval take?

There is no single timeline that applies to every mortgage. A straightforward application with complete documents and no property issues may progress faster than a file that needs additional income verification, appraisal work, title corrections or underwriting conditions. The lender, loan program, property, purchase contract and borrower response time all matter.

It is important to separate the overall approval process from the deadlines for specific disclosures. Federal rules set deadlines for documents such as the Loan Estimate and Closing Disclosure, but those deadlines do not promise that underwriting, appraisal, title work or final approval will be completed within a fixed number of days.

The main stages of a mortgage application

2. Purchase contract and formal applicationAfter an offer is accepted, you provide the property and transaction details and submit the loan application.Contract terms, property details, loan structure and complete application information.

Some stages happen in parallel. For example, appraisal, title review and document verification may proceed while underwriting is underway. Your lender or loan officer should be able to tell you which milestones are complete, which are outstanding and who is responsible for the next action.

Two important federal disclosure deadlines

Loan Estimate: generally within three business days

For most mortgage transactions covered by the Loan Estimate rules, the lender must deliver or place the Loan Estimate in the mail within three business days after receiving an application. For this rule, an application is considered received after the lender has the six key pieces of information:

  • Your name.
  • Your income.
  • Your Social Security number, so the lender can obtain a credit report.
  • The address of the property.
  • An estimate of the property's value.
  • The mortgage loan amount sought.

The lender cannot require additional information just to issue the Loan Estimate. You may choose to provide supporting documents to help the lender prepare a more accurate estimate, but receiving a Loan Estimate does not mean the loan has been approved. The disclosure shows the terms the lender expects to offer if you decide to proceed.

These Loan Estimate and Closing Disclosure requirements do not apply to every type of home financing. The CFPB identifies exceptions including reverse mortgages, HELOCs, certain manufactured-home loans not secured by real estate and certain subordinate assistance loans.

Closing Disclosure: generally at least three business days before closing

For covered mortgage transactions, you must receive the Closing Disclosure at least three business days before consummation. It shows the final loan terms, projected payments, closing costs and cash required. The review period gives you time to compare the final figures with your Loan Estimate and ask questions.

Most changes to the Closing Disclosure do not automatically require a new three-business-day waiting period. A new waiting period is generally required when one of three specified changes occurs: the disclosed APR becomes inaccurate, the loan product changes, or a prepayment penalty is added. Other changes generally require a corrected disclosure but not a new three-day wait.

The three-business-day Closing Disclosure rule is a review period at the end of the process. It is not a promise that a mortgage will be approved three days after the disclosure is issued.

Preapproval is not final mortgage approval

A preapproval letter is an early assessment based on the information and verification the lender has completed at that stage. Lenders use the terms "prequalification" and "preapproval" differently, and their processes are not identical. Neither a preapproval letter nor a Loan Estimate guarantees final approval.

After a purchase contract is signed, the lender still needs to review the actual property and transaction and complete required underwriting. Changes in income, employment, debt, credit, assets, loan amount, property value or occupancy may affect eligibility or pricing. Avoid treating a preapproval as permission to take on new debt or make large financial changes.

What happens during underwriting?

Underwriting is the lender's review of whether the borrower, property and requested loan meet the loan program and lender requirements. Depending on the transaction, the lender may verify:

  • Income and employment: pay statements, tax documents, employer information and the stability or continuance of income.
  • Assets and funds to close: bank statements, investment accounts, source of down payment, earnest money and large deposits.
  • Credit and liabilities: credit history, monthly debts, recent credit activity and any required explanations.
  • Property and collateral: appraisal or other permitted valuation, property condition and eligibility for the loan program.
  • Title and insurance: ownership and lien matters, title requirements and acceptable property insurance coverage.
  • Transaction details: purchase contract, occupancy, loan amount, down payment, seller credits and any program-specific requirements.

An underwriter may issue conditional approval. This means the file appears to meet requirements subject to specified items being resolved. It is not the same as a final clear-to-close decision. Read each condition carefully and ask who must provide the document or action.

Appraisal vs. home inspection: different jobs

A lender's appraisal or valuation is primarily used to assess the property's value for the mortgage decision and collateral. A buyer's home inspection is a separate evaluation of the property's condition and potential defects. An appraisal is not a substitute for a home inspection, and a home inspection does not establish the appraised value used for underwriting.

Scheduling, property access, comparable sales, unusual property features, repairs or appraisal disputes can add time. Ask the lender when the appraisal is ordered, whether access has been arranged and whether any follow-up is outstanding.

Common reasons mortgage approval is delayed

  • Incomplete documentation: pages are missing, statements do not cover the requested period, or documents are unreadable.
  • Unexplained deposits or transfers: the lender asks for a paper trail or explanation for funds.
  • Income or employment changes: a job change, reduced hours, commission variation or change in business income needs additional review.
  • New debt or credit inquiries: a new vehicle loan, credit-card balance or other obligation changes the borrower's debt picture.
  • Appraisal problems: low value, property condition, access or comparable-sales questions require further work.
  • Title or ownership issues: liens, judgments, ownership discrepancies or recording matters need resolution.
  • Insurance issues: suitable homeowners or required flood insurance is not available or documentation is incomplete.
  • Contract changes: a change to price, credits, loan amount, property or closing date requires updated review or disclosures.
  • Slow communication: requests are missed, unclear or sent to the wrong person.

What you can do to keep the process organized

  • Provide complete, readable documents in the format the lender requests.
  • Respond promptly and ask whether each upload was received and accepted.
  • Keep copies of bank statements, pay statements, tax documents, insurance information and transaction records.
  • Explain large deposits, transfers or unusual account activity before the lender has to ask.
  • Avoid opening new credit accounts, financing a vehicle or making large purchases before closing without discussing the effect with the lender.
  • Avoid changing jobs or compensation arrangements during underwriting if possible; if a change is unavoidable, notify the lender immediately.
  • Do not move large sums between accounts without keeping a clear record of where funds came from and where they went.
  • Schedule the appraisal, inspection, insurance and title-related tasks as soon as the responsible parties are ready.
  • Ask for a written list of outstanding underwriting conditions and the owner of each item.
  • Keep your real estate agent, loan officer and closing agent informed when the contract or expected closing date changes.

These steps can reduce avoidable back-and-forth, but they cannot guarantee approval or a particular closing date. Some delays depend on third parties, property conditions or underwriting rules beyond the borrower's control.

What does conditional approval or clear to close mean?

Conditional approval means the lender has reviewed the file but still requires listed conditions to be satisfied. Conditions may involve updated pay statements, bank statements, verification of employment, source of funds, appraisal items, title documents or other loan-specific requirements.

Clear to close generally means the lender has completed the required underwriting and closing review and is ready to proceed to the closing stage, subject to final closing procedures. It is not a reason to stop checking instructions, final figures or documents. Ask the lender whether any last-minute verification or funding condition remains.

What to review before closing

Read the Closing Disclosure as soon as it arrives and compare it with the Loan Estimate. Check:

  • Loan amount, interest rate, product, term and whether the rate is locked.
  • Monthly principal and interest, mortgage insurance, escrow and any payment that may change.
  • Points, lender credits, origination charges and third-party services.
  • Taxes, prepaids, initial escrow deposit and total closing costs.
  • Cash to close, earnest money already paid and seller or lender credits.
  • Any prepayment penalty, balloon payment, adjustable-rate feature or other special term.
  • Names, property address and any other transaction details that must be accurate.

Do not sign documents you do not understand or accept unexplained differences under pressure. Ask the lender or settlement agent to explain any change and provide corrected disclosures where required.

Protect your closing funds from wire fraud

Mortgage closing scams can involve fake emails or messages that appear to come from a lender, real estate agent, attorney or title company. A message may claim that wiring instructions have changed at the last minute. Do not rely on emailed instructions alone or use a phone number included in a suspicious message to verify them.

Before wiring money, independently call a trusted, previously verified phone number for the settlement agent or title company and confirm the recipient, bank, account number and amount. Be cautious about urgent requests to change payment details. If you believe you sent funds to a fraudster, contact your bank immediately and report the incident to law enforcement and the relevant authorities.

Questions to ask your loan officer

  • Have you received all six application items, and when should I expect my Loan Estimate?
  • Which underwriting documents or conditions are still outstanding?
  • Has the appraisal been ordered, completed and accepted?
  • Are there any title, insurance or property issues that could affect closing?
  • What does conditional approval mean for my file, and what remains before clear to close?
  • When will I receive the Closing Disclosure, and when is the earliest contractual closing date?
  • Have any changes to my loan terms or costs required a corrected disclosure or new waiting period?
  • Who should I contact if a request or closing instruction looks suspicious?

Frequently asked questions

How many days does mortgage approval take?

There is no single guaranteed number of days for every loan. Application complexity, document readiness, appraisal, title, insurance, underwriting conditions, lender workload and contract timing all affect the process. Ask your lender for a milestone-based estimate and update it when conditions change.

Does a Loan Estimate mean I am approved?

No. A Loan Estimate is an early disclosure of expected loan terms and costs. It does not mean the lender has completed underwriting or approved the loan.

When must a lender send the Loan Estimate?

For covered transactions, generally within three business days after the lender receives the six key pieces of application information: name, income, Social Security number, property address, estimated property value and desired loan amount.

Does the lender need my tax returns before sending a Loan Estimate?

Not just to trigger the Loan Estimate requirement. The six-item application definition does not require income-verification documents. The lender may request tax returns and other documents later to verify the application and underwrite the loan.

What is conditional mortgage approval?

It means the lender has reviewed the file but requires specified conditions to be completed before final approval. It is not the same as clear to close.

What is clear to close?

It generally means the lender has completed the required review and is ready to proceed toward closing, subject to final closing and funding procedures. Confirm any remaining requirements with your lender.

When must I receive the Closing Disclosure?

For covered mortgage transactions, generally at least three business days before consummation. This is a final disclosure review period, not the overall mortgage approval timeline.

Can a change to the Closing Disclosure delay closing?

Some changes require a new three-business-day waiting period: an inaccurate APR, a loan product change, or the addition of a prepayment penalty. Most other changes require a corrected disclosure but do not automatically restart the three-day period.

Does the appraisal happen before underwriting?

Appraisal and underwriting can overlap. A lender may begin reviewing borrower documents while the appraisal and title work are in progress. The lender's process and the status of the file determine the sequence.

Does a home inspection replace an appraisal?

No. An appraisal or valuation is used primarily for the lender's collateral and value assessment. A home inspection evaluates property condition for the buyer. They serve different purposes.

Can I use my preapproval letter as a guarantee?

No. Preapproval is an earlier assessment based on information available at that stage. The lender still has to verify the actual application and property and complete underwriting before final approval.

What should I avoid doing before closing?

Avoid opening new credit, taking on new debt, making unexplained large deposits or transfers, changing financial arrangements or spending funds needed for closing without discussing the impact with your lender. These actions can require new verification or affect qualification.
