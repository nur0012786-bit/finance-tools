US HOME-BUYING GUIDE

How to Estimate Mortgage Affordability

A practical US guide to estimating a comfortable mortgage range using income, recurring debts, down payment, interest rate, property taxes, insurance and mortgage insurance.

What does mortgage affordability mean?

Mortgage affordability is an estimate of the home price and housing payment that may fit within a household's finances. A lender's qualification decision and a household's own budget are not necessarily the same thing.

Use the Mortgage Affordability Calculator to test income, recurring debt and housing-cost assumptions. Then use the Mortgage Calculator to examine the payment for a specific loan amount.

Income is only one part of the calculation

Lenders generally use gross monthly income (before taxes and payroll deductions) in qualifying calculations. Your personal budget should also use take-home pay—the money that reaches your bank account—and include food, utilities, transportation, childcare, healthcare, insurance and savings goals.

A lender's maximum approval and your comfortable spending limit are not necessarily the same. Reserve money for emergencies, retirement, repairs and other priorities instead of using every available dollar to qualify for a larger home. The CFPB recommends focusing on a payment that fits your broader financial priorities, not just the amount a lender is willing to offer.

Housing costs to include

Don't look only at principal and interest. A realistic monthly housing estimate can include property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, supplementary insurance such as flood coverage, and HOA or condominium dues. Taxes and insurance may be collected through escrow or paid separately; either way, they belong in your budget.

Utilities, routine maintenance, repairs and major replacements are generally outside the mortgage payment, but they still affect what you can comfortably afford. Estimate costs for the specific property and location rather than relying only on a national average.

How debt-to-income ratios fit in

Debt-to-income ratio (DTI) compares monthly debt obligations with gross monthly income. The general formula is:

DTI = Monthly debt obligations ÷ Gross monthly income × 100

Mortgage analysis may distinguish a housing ratio (proposed monthly housing obligation divided by gross monthly income) from total DTI (housing obligation plus other qualifying monthly debts divided by gross monthly income). Other debts can include auto loans, student loans, minimum credit-card payments and personal loans. The lender determines which obligations and income sources count under the applicable underwriting rules.

For example, a household earning $8,000 gross per month with $1,200 in other monthly debt and a 36% total DTI assumption has $2,880 available for all qualifying monthly debts. Subtracting the $1,200 leaves $1,680 for housing. Even if a 28% housing assumption permits $2,240, the total-DTI calculation is the tighter constraint in this example.

DTI is an underwriting measure, not a complete household budget. It does not capture every living expense, savings goal or future repair. A payment can fit the DTI calculation and still be too high for your actual cash flow.

Are 28% and 36% DTI limits required by law?

No single 28% housing or 36% total DTI rule applies to every US mortgage borrower. These figures are common planning assumptions, not universal legal limits or a guarantee of approval. Actual underwriting requirements depend on the loan program, lender, credit profile, reserves and other factors.

For context, Fannie Mae's published guidance for manually underwritten loans describes a 36% maximum total DTI, with possible increases up to 45% when specified credit-score and reserve requirements are met. For loan casefiles underwritten through Desktop Underwriter (DU), its published maximum is 50%. These are Fannie Mae conventional-loan underwriting rules, not universal limits for all lenders or mortgage programs. A ratio below a stated ceiling does not guarantee approval.

The calculator lets you change housing and total DTI assumptions to model different scenarios. Its default 28% and 36% values are planning assumptions, not a lender decision. Government-backed loan programs and lender-specific rules may differ. See the Debt-to-Income Ratio Guide.

Down payment matters

A down payment reduces the starting loan balance. It can also affect loan-to-value ratio and, depending on the loan, whether mortgage insurance applies.

For example, on a $400,000 home, a $40,000 down payment represents 10% of the purchase price and leaves a starting principal of $360,000 before other financing adjustments. Use the Down Payment Calculator to compare cash requirements.

Interest rate and loan term

The interest rate directly affects the scheduled principal-and-interest payment. The loan term determines how many monthly payments are scheduled. A longer term can produce a lower scheduled payment while increasing the total interest paid when the loan is carried to maturity.

Because mortgage rates change over time, affordability estimates should be tested across more than one rate assumption rather than treated as a permanent number.

Taxes, insurance and PMI can change the result

Two homes with the same purchase price can have different monthly housing costs because property-tax rates, insurance premiums, HOA dues and mortgage-insurance requirements differ.

This is why a home-price estimate should be converted into a complete monthly housing-cost estimate before a buyer decides whether the payment fits the household budget.

Affordability example

Assume a household has $8,000 gross monthly income, $1,200 in other monthly debt, a $60,000 down payment, a 6.50% fixed rate and a 30-year term. Use a 28% housing DTI assumption and a 36% total DTI assumption.

The housing assumption allows up to $2,240 per month ($8,000 × 28%). The total DTI assumption allows $2,880 for all qualifying monthly debt ($8,000 × 36%). After subtracting the $1,200 in other debt, the total-DTI limit leaves $1,680 for housing. That is the tighter limit in this example.

Assume annual property taxes are 1.10% of the home price, homeowners insurance is $1,800 per year, and mortgage insurance and HOA dues are $0. Under those assumptions, the calculator estimates a maximum home price of about $263,804, with a starting loan of about $203,804. Estimated monthly principal and interest is about $1,288.18, property tax is about $241.82 and homeowners insurance is $150, for total housing costs of $1,680.

Adding PMI, HOA dues, higher taxes, higher insurance or a higher interest rate would reduce the estimated price that fits the same DTI assumptions. This is a mathematical example, not a lender approval or a recommendation to spend the full amount. The Mortgage Affordability Calculator lets you test different assumptions.

Affordability vs. preapproval

A calculator can help with planning, but it cannot verify income, assets, credit history, employment, property details or lender-specific underwriting requirements. A mortgage preapproval is based on information reviewed by a lender and may be subject to conditions, updated documentation, appraisal and final underwriting.

Before choosing a home price, estimate the down payment, lender and third-party closing costs, prepaid taxes and insurance, initial escrow deposits, inspections, moving expenses and immediate repairs. Then check how much cash will remain for emergencies. Review the lender's Loan Estimate for the projected monthly payment, rate, loan type, closing costs and cash to close.

Questions to ask before choosing a home price

  • How much cash will remain after the down payment and closing costs?
  • Can the household continue saving after the projected housing payment?
  • What happens to the budget if taxes or insurance increase?
  • Is there room for repairs and maintenance?
  • Does the payment still work under a higher interest-rate assumption?

Mortgage affordability FAQ

How much house can I afford?

There is no single percentage that works for every household. Income, recurring debts, loan terms, taxes, insurance, down payment, reserves and other expenses all affect affordability.

What is DTI?

DTI is debt-to-income ratio. It compares qualifying monthly debt obligations with gross monthly income and is one factor that can be used in mortgage underwriting.

Should I use gross income or take-home pay?

Mortgage DTI calculations generally use gross income before taxes and deductions. For your personal budget, use take-home pay and account for expenses that DTI does not measure. These answer different questions, and both matter.

Is 28/36 a mortgage approval rule?

No. It is a common budgeting convention and a useful scenario to test, not a universal legal requirement. Different mortgage programs and underwriting systems may use different limits and other qualifying factors.

Does a bigger down payment increase affordability?

It can reduce the amount borrowed and may reduce the monthly payment. It can also change loan-to-value and mortgage-insurance requirements. However, using too much cash for a down payment can reduce emergency reserves.

Does a mortgage calculator tell me what a lender will approve?

No. A calculator is a planning tool. A lender evaluates borrower and property information under its applicable underwriting requirements.
