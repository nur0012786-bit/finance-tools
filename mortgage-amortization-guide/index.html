AMORTIZATION BASICS

What is mortgage amortization?

Mortgage amortization is the process of paying a loan balance down through scheduled payments over a stated term. On a typical fixed-rate amortizing mortgage, each scheduled payment includes an interest amount and an amount applied to principal.

An amortization schedule lays out those calculations payment by payment. It can show the scheduled payment, interest, principal and remaining balance for each period.

What does an amortization schedule show?

ColumnWhat it represents
Payment numberThe position of the payment within the scheduled amortization.
PaymentThe scheduled principal-and-interest amount, subject to the loan terms.
InterestThe portion of the payment allocated to interest for that period.
PrincipalThe portion applied to reduce the outstanding loan balance.
Remaining balanceThe mortgage balance after the scheduled principal reduction.

A real lender or servicer statement can contain additional fields, especially when taxes, insurance, escrow, fees or other transactions are involved.

Why is more interest paid early in the loan?

For a standard amortizing mortgage, periodic interest is based on the outstanding balance. At the beginning of the loan, that balance is at its highest, so the interest amount can represent a larger portion of the scheduled principal-and-interest payment.

As principal is repaid, the balance becomes smaller. Future interest charges can therefore become smaller, allowing a larger share of the scheduled payment to reduce principal.

Simple example of one amortization payment

Imagine a hypothetical loan where the scheduled principal-and-interest payment is $2,000. If $1,500 is allocated to interest for one payment period, the remaining $500 would be applied to principal, assuming no other adjustments affect the payment.

The following payment would be calculated using the updated balance. Over time, the interest and principal amounts change even when the scheduled principal-and-interest payment stays level on a fixed-rate mortgage.

This is an illustration only. Actual mortgage calculations depend on the loan amount, rate, payment frequency, timing and contract terms.

How loan amount affects amortization

A larger starting balance generally produces a larger principal-and-interest payment when rate and term are held constant. It also means more interest can accrue because the initial balance is larger.

A down payment changes the starting mortgage balance by reducing the amount financed from the purchase price.

How interest rate affects amortization

For the same loan amount and term, a higher interest rate generally results in a higher scheduled principal-and-interest payment and more total interest over the amortization period, all else equal.

When comparing offers, look at the rate together with the loan amount, term, points, lender credits and other costs rather than comparing a single number in isolation.

How loan term affects amortization

The amortization term determines how many scheduled payments are used to repay the loan. A longer term generally spreads repayment over more periods, while a shorter term generally requires a larger scheduled payment for the same balance and rate.

The total interest paid can differ substantially between terms. A longer term can have a lower scheduled payment but more time for interest to accrue.

What happens with extra principal payments?

An extra principal payment reduces the outstanding balance beyond the scheduled principal reduction. A lower balance can reduce future interest because future interest is calculated from a smaller balance, subject to the loan's terms.

Depending on the mortgage and how the servicer applies extra payments, additional principal can also shorten the payoff period. Check your loan documents and servicer instructions before assuming how an extra payment will be applied.

How to read your own amortization schedule

  1. Find the starting loan balance.
  2. Confirm the interest rate and scheduled term.
  3. Look at the first payment's interest and principal amounts.
  4. Check that the remaining balance falls after principal is applied.
  5. Compare later payments to see how the principal/interest mix changes.
  6. Review the final scheduled balance and payoff date against your loan documents.

What can make a real schedule look different?

Not every mortgage follows the simplest fixed-rate example. Adjustable-rate loans, different payment frequencies, irregular payments, refinancing, deferments, fees, escrow transactions and other loan-specific events can affect statements and schedules.

For a real loan, the lender or servicer's disclosures and account records are the authoritative source for the actual payment and balance information.

Frequently asked questions

Does an amortization schedule show my entire mortgage payment?

Usually, the core schedule focuses on principal and interest. Taxes, homeowners insurance, PMI and other costs may be handled separately or through escrow.

Why does the principal portion increase over time?

On a typical fixed-rate amortizing mortgage, the outstanding balance declines. Because interest is calculated from that balance, the interest amount generally declines and more of the scheduled payment can go toward principal.

Can extra payments change my amortization schedule?

Yes. An extra principal payment can reduce the outstanding balance and may shorten the payoff period or reduce future interest. The exact result depends on the loan terms and how the servicer applies the payment.

Is a 30-year mortgage always paid off in exactly 30 years?

A scheduled 30-year amortization is designed to repay the loan over the stated schedule when required payments are made as agreed. Refinancing, extra payments, missed payments, modifications or other changes can alter the actual payoff timing.

Where can I get the official amortization information for my mortgage?

Review your mortgage disclosures and contact your lender or servicer for the authoritative schedule and current account balance.
