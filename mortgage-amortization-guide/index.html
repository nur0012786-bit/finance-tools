AMORTIZATION BASICS

What is mortgage amortization?

Mortgage amortization is the process of paying a loan balance down through scheduled payments over a stated term. On a typical fixed-rate amortizing mortgage, each scheduled payment includes an interest amount and an amount applied to principal.

An amortization schedule lays out those calculations payment by payment. It can show the scheduled payment, interest, principal and remaining balance for each period.

What does an amortization schedule show?

ColumnWhat it represents
Payment numberThe position of the payment within the scheduled amortization.
PaymentThe scheduled principal-and-interest amount, subject to the loan terms.
InterestThe portion of the payment allocated to interest for that period.
PrincipalThe portion applied to reduce the outstanding loan balance.
Remaining balanceThe mortgage balance after the scheduled principal reduction.

A real lender or servicer statement can contain additional fields, especially when taxes, insurance, escrow, fees or other transactions are involved.

For a fully amortizing fixed-rate mortgage, the scheduled monthly principal-and-interest payment can be calculated with:

M = P × [r(1 + r)n] ÷ [(1 + r)n − 1]

  • P is the starting principal balance.
  • r is the monthly interest rate expressed as a decimal (annual note rate divided by 12).
  • n is the number of scheduled monthly payments.
  • M is the scheduled monthly principal-and-interest payment.

For each period in a simple monthly model, interest is the beginning balance multiplied by the monthly rate. Principal is the scheduled payment minus that period's interest, and the new balance is the prior balance minus principal. Real servicers may use contractual accrual and rounding conventions that differ from a simplified monthly model.

Why is more interest paid early in the loan?

For a standard amortizing mortgage, periodic interest is based on the outstanding balance. At the beginning of the loan, that balance is at its highest, so the interest amount can represent a larger portion of the scheduled principal-and-interest payment.

As principal is repaid, the balance becomes smaller. Future interest charges can therefore become smaller, allowing a larger share of the scheduled payment to reduce principal.

Worked amortization example: $300,000 at 6.50%

Assume a $300,000 fully amortizing fixed-rate mortgage with a 6.50% annual interest rate and a 30-year term. The monthly principal-and-interest payment is approximately $1,896.20, or 360 scheduled payments. The monthly rate used in this illustration is 6.50% divided by 12.

For the first payment, estimated interest is $1,625.00 ($300,000 × 6.50% ÷ 12). The remaining $271.20 of the $1,896.20 payment reduces principal, leaving an estimated balance of $299,728.80.

How loan amount affects amortization

A larger starting balance generally produces a larger principal-and-interest payment when rate and term are held constant. It also means more interest can accrue because the initial balance is larger.

A down payment changes the starting mortgage balance by reducing the amount financed from the purchase price.

How interest rate affects amortization

For the same loan amount and term, a higher interest rate generally results in a higher scheduled principal-and-interest payment and more total interest over the amortization period, all else equal.

When comparing offers, look at the rate together with the loan amount, term, points, lender credits and other costs rather than comparing a single number in isolation.

How loan term affects amortization

The amortization term determines how many scheduled payments are used to repay the loan. A longer term generally spreads repayment over more periods, while a shorter term generally requires a larger scheduled payment for the same balance and rate.

The total interest paid can differ substantially between terms. A longer term can have a lower scheduled payment but more time for interest to accrue.

What happens with extra principal payments?

An extra principal payment reduces the outstanding balance beyond the scheduled principal reduction. A lower balance can reduce the interest charged in later periods and may shorten the time needed to repay the loan. The effect depends on the rate, timing, amount, payment history and how the servicer applies the extra amount.

For the $300,000 example above, assume the borrower pays an additional $100 toward principal every month, on top of the scheduled $1,896.20 principal-and-interest payment. Under a monthly-payment model, the loan would be paid off in about 312 months (26 years) instead of 360 months (30 years). Estimated total interest would fall from about $382,633.47 to $321,638.68, a modeled reduction of approximately $60,994.79.

This comparison assumes the extra $100 is applied directly to principal each month, the interest rate remains fixed, no additional draws or fees occur, and the borrower continues the extra payments until payoff. It is not a guarantee of savings for an individual loan. Tell your servicer that an additional amount is intended for principal and check your loan terms for any prepayment provisions. The CFPB also recommends confirming how extra payments will be applied.

Amortization vs. total monthly housing payment

A standard amortization schedule generally focuses on the mortgage principal and interest. Your total monthly housing expense can be higher because property taxes, homeowners insurance, PMI, HOA dues and other costs may be paid separately or through an escrow account. The CFPB explains that escrow and mortgage insurance can be included in the amount collected by the servicer, while HOA charges are commonly separate.

How to read your own amortization schedule

  1. Find the starting loan balance.
  2. Confirm the interest rate and scheduled term.
  3. Look at the first payment's interest and principal amounts.
  4. Check that the remaining balance falls after principal is applied.
  5. Compare later payments to see how the principal/interest mix changes.
  6. Review the final scheduled balance and payoff date against your loan documents.

When a loan does not amortize like a standard fixed-rate mortgage

A standard fully amortizing mortgage is designed to repay the full principal by the end of the stated term when scheduled payments are made as agreed. Other loan structures can work differently:

  • Interest-only period: scheduled payments cover interest but do not reduce principal during the interest-only period.
  • Negative amortization: a payment is not large enough to cover all accrued interest, so unpaid interest is added to the amount owed and the balance grows.
  • Balloon payment: regular payments do not fully repay the loan, leaving a larger lump-sum balance due at a specified date.
  • Adjustable-rate mortgage: the rate and required payment can change when the rate adjusts, so a new payment schedule may be needed.

Do not assume every mortgage schedule will reduce the balance every month. Ask the lender whether the loan fully amortizes, whether there is an interest-only or negative-amortization feature, and whether a balloon payment is due. The CFPB explains the risks of negative amortization and non-fully-amortizing loans.

For a real loan, the note, mortgage disclosures and servicer account records are the authoritative sources for payment and balance information.

Frequently asked questions

Does an amortization schedule show my entire mortgage payment?

Usually, the core schedule focuses on principal and interest. Taxes, homeowners insurance, PMI and other costs may be handled separately or through escrow.

Why does the principal portion increase over time?

On a typical fixed-rate amortizing mortgage, the outstanding balance declines. Because interest is calculated from that balance, the interest amount generally declines and more of the scheduled payment can go toward principal.

Can extra payments change my amortization schedule?

Yes. An extra principal payment can reduce the outstanding balance and may shorten the payoff period or reduce future interest. The exact result depends on the loan terms and how the servicer applies the payment.

Is a 30-year mortgage always paid off in exactly 30 years?

A scheduled 30-year amortization is designed to repay the loan over the stated schedule when required payments are made as agreed. Refinancing, extra payments, missed payments, modifications or other changes can alter the actual payoff timing.

Where can I get the official amortization information for my mortgage?

Review your mortgage disclosures and contact your lender or servicer for the authoritative schedule and current account balance.

Can an amortization schedule show negative amortization?

It can, if the loan terms allow payments that are less than the interest due. Unpaid interest may be added to the principal balance, causing the amount owed to grow instead of shrink. Ask your lender whether the loan can negatively amortize.

Does an extra payment automatically reduce principal?

Do not assume so. Tell your servicer how the extra amount should be applied and review your next statement to confirm it reduced principal. Check your loan terms and whether any prepayment provisions apply.

Why does my servicer's balance differ from an online schedule?

A simple calculator may assume monthly interest, on-time payments and no fees or account adjustments. Your actual schedule can differ because of payment dates, interest accrual, rounding, fees, escrow transactions, rate changes or payment history. Your servicer's account records control the actual balance.
