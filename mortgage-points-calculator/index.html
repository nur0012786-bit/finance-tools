HOW IT WORKS

How this mortgage points calculator works

Mortgage discount points are upfront charges paid to a lender in exchange for a lower interest rate. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau states that one discount point generally equals 1% of the loan amount. For example, one point on a $300,000 mortgage is $3,000.

The amount a point reduces the interest rate is not universal. It depends on the lender, loan type and market conditions. That is why this calculator asks you to enter both the rate without points and the actual rate offered with points rather than assuming a fixed rate reduction.

The calculator then compares principal-and-interest payments at the two rates. The simple break-even period is the points cost divided by the monthly payment savings. This is a basic cash-flow calculation, not a complete cost-of-borrowing analysis.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Mortgage points example

Suppose you borrow $300,000 for 30 years. Paying 1 point would cost $3,000. If the rate without points is 6.50% and the rate with points is 6.25%, the calculator compares the resulting monthly principal-and-interest payments and estimates how long the monthly savings would take to equal the $3,000 upfront cost.

The exact rate reduction must come from the lender's actual loan options. A 1-point charge does not automatically mean a 0.25 percentage-point rate reduction.

BREAK-EVEN & LOAN TERM

Why break-even is only one part of the comparison

The simple break-even figure divides points cost by the monthly payment reduction. It does not account for the fact that a borrower who sells or refinances must also repay the remaining mortgage balance. The economic break-even and holding-period result on this page include that remaining balance in the comparison.

The holding-period result is a nominal-dollar estimate: total principal-and-interest payments made plus the remaining loan balance, with the points cost added to the points option. A positive net saving means the points option has lower modeled cost at that point; a negative amount means it has cost more.

This is not a discounted cash-flow or tax calculation. It does not account for the time value of money, tax deductibility, refinancing or selling expenses, or changes in mortgage rates. Compare actual Loan Estimates for several timeframes, as the CFPB recommends.

POINTS VS. LENDER CREDITS

Discount points and lender credits work in opposite directions

Discount points generally mean paying more upfront for a lower interest rate. Lender credits generally reduce upfront closing costs in exchange for a higher interest rate. These are pricing tradeoffs, so compare the rate, points, credits and total loan costs together.

Points shown on a Loan Estimate and Closing Disclosure should be connected to a discounted interest rate. Ask the lender to explain exactly how the quoted points affect the rate before comparing offers.

TAX & COST LIMITATIONS

What this calculator does not estimate

The calculator focuses on principal and interest and assumes both options have the same original loan amount and term. It does not estimate property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, HOA dues, lender credits, other closing costs, refinancing or selling expenses, the time value of money or individual tax treatment.

It also does not determine whether points are deductible for your particular tax situation. For tax questions, use current IRS guidance or consult a qualified tax professional.

FAQ

US mortgage points calculator FAQs

What are mortgage discount points?

Discount points are upfront charges paid to a lender in exchange for a lower interest rate than the zero-point option offered for the same type of loan.

How much does one mortgage point cost?

One discount point generally equals 1% of the mortgage loan amount. On a $300,000 loan, one point is $3,000. You can enter fractional points too.

Does one point always reduce the rate by 0.25%?

No. There is no universal rate reduction per point. The rate change depends on the lender, loan type and mortgage market. Enter the actual rates from comparable lender offers.

What is the simple break-even?

It is the upfront points cost divided by monthly principal-and-interest savings. It is easy to understand, but does not account for the difference in remaining loan balances.

What is economic break-even in this calculator?

It is the first modeled month when cumulative principal-and-interest payments plus the remaining loan balance, including the upfront points charge, are lower with points than without points. It is still a simplified nominal-dollar comparison.

Why does the calculator ask how long I expect to keep the loan?

Points are paid upfront to reduce the rate over time. If you sell or refinance sooner than expected, you may not recover the initial cost. Compare your likely timeframe with shorter and longer scenarios.

Should I compare total interest or holding-period cost?

Both answer different questions. Full-term interest assumes you keep the loan to maturity. Holding-period cost considers the payments made and the balance that would still be owed when you sell or refinance at your selected timeframe.

Are taxes and insurance included?

No. This tool compares principal and interest only. Property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance and other housing expenses are excluded.

Can I use this calculator for a refinance?

Yes, as a basic comparison if both offers have the same starting loan amount and term. Add any refinance-specific fees separately when comparing actual offers; this calculator does not model them automatically.
