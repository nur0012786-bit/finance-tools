HOW IT WORKS

How this mortgage points calculator works

Mortgage discount points are upfront charges paid to a lender in exchange for a lower interest rate. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau states that one discount point generally equals 1% of the loan amount. For example, one point on a $300,000 mortgage is $3,000.

The amount a point reduces the interest rate is not universal. It depends on the lender, loan type and market conditions. That is why this calculator asks you to enter both the rate without points and the actual rate offered with points rather than assuming a fixed rate reduction.

The calculator then compares principal-and-interest payments at the two rates. The simple break-even period is the points cost divided by the monthly payment savings. This is a basic cash-flow calculation, not a complete cost-of-borrowing analysis.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Mortgage points example

Suppose you borrow $300,000 for 30 years. Paying 1 point would cost $3,000. If the rate without points is 6.50% and the rate with points is 6.25%, the calculator compares the resulting monthly principal-and-interest payments and estimates how long the monthly savings would take to equal the $3,000 upfront cost.

The exact rate reduction must come from the lender's actual loan options. A 1-point charge does not automatically mean a 0.25 percentage-point rate reduction.

BREAK-EVEN & LOAN TERM

Why break-even is only one part of the comparison

A simple break-even calculation can help show when cumulative monthly savings equal the upfront points cost. But the result depends on how long the mortgage remains outstanding. If you sell or refinance before the break-even point, you may not recover the upfront cost through monthly savings.

Comparing total interest over the full loan term can also be misleading if you do not expect to keep the mortgage for the full term. A more useful comparison can be made over several possible holding periods using the actual Loan Estimates from your lender.

CFPB guidance recommends comparing the upfront cost of points with the lower rate over different time periods rather than relying on a single break-even assumption.

POINTS VS. LENDER CREDITS

Discount points and lender credits work in opposite directions

Discount points generally mean paying more upfront for a lower interest rate. Lender credits generally reduce upfront closing costs in exchange for a higher interest rate. These are pricing tradeoffs, so compare the rate, points, credits and total loan costs together.

Points shown on a Loan Estimate and Closing Disclosure should be connected to a discounted interest rate. Ask the lender to explain exactly how the quoted points affect the rate before comparing offers.

TAX & COST LIMITATIONS

What this calculator does not estimate

The calculator focuses on the mortgage principal-and-interest difference created by the two entered rates. It does not estimate property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, HOA dues, lender credits, other closing costs, refinancing costs, selling costs, the time value of money or individual tax treatment.

It also does not determine whether points are deductible for your particular tax situation. For tax questions, use current IRS guidance or consult a qualified tax professional.

FAQ

US mortgage points calculator FAQs

What are mortgage discount points?

Discount points are upfront charges paid to a lender to obtain a lower interest rate than the rate available without the points.

How much does one mortgage point cost?

One discount point generally equals 1% of the mortgage loan amount. On a $300,000 loan, one point is $3,000.

Does one point always reduce the rate by 0.25%?

No. There is no universal rate reduction per point. The reduction varies with the lender, loan type and market conditions.

How is the simple break-even period calculated?

The calculator divides the upfront points cost by the monthly principal-and-interest payment savings. It reports how long those savings would take to equal the upfront cost.

Why does the calculator ask for two interest rates?

Because the rate reduction associated with points is not fixed. Using the actual rates from a lender's offers produces a more useful comparison than assuming a standard reduction.

Should I compare total interest or break-even time?

Both can provide useful information, but they answer different questions. Break-even focuses on recovering the upfront cost through monthly savings, while full-term interest assumes the loan remains outstanding through maturity.

Are taxes and insurance included?

No. The calculator compares principal and interest only. Property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance and other housing costs are excluded.

Can points be used when refinancing?

Discount points can be part of refinance pricing as well as purchase-loan pricing. Use the actual refinance Loan Estimate and include the points in your comparison of upfront costs and interest rates.
