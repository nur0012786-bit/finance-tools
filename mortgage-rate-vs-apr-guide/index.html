Mortgage Interest Rate vs. APR: What's the Difference?

The mortgage interest rate and annual percentage rate, or APR, are related but different numbers. Understanding what each represents can make mortgage offers easier to compare.

What is a mortgage interest rate?

Your mortgage interest rate is the rate the lender charges for borrowing the principal. For a standard fixed-rate mortgage, that contractual rate is used to calculate interest on the outstanding balance under the loan's payment schedule. The rate does not, by itself, show the fees and other charges you may pay to obtain the loan.

The interest rate is a key input in the scheduled principal-and-interest payment. For the same loan amount and term, a higher contractual rate generally means a higher scheduled payment and more interest over the full repayment schedule, assuming the loan is held and paid as scheduled.

What is APR?

APR means annual percentage rate. It is a broader, standardized measure of the cost of borrowing than the stated interest rate. For a closed-end mortgage, APR reflects the interest rate and certain finance charges associated with obtaining the loan, expressed as an annualized rate under the applicable disclosure rules.

Finance charges can include discount points, mortgage broker fees and certain loan fees. However, APR does not simply include every item on the Closing Disclosure. Federal rules exclude certain bona fide and reasonable real-estate-related charges, such as specified title, survey, document-preparation, notary, credit-report and appraisal fees, as well as certain escrow deposits. The precise treatment depends on the fee and the applicable rules.

That is why APR is a useful comparison measure, but it is not a complete inventory of every dollar you will spend to buy and own a home.

Why is APR often higher than the interest rate?

APR is usually higher because it incorporates certain costs of obtaining the loan in addition to interest. Points and mortgage broker fees are examples of charges that can be included in the finance charge. The amount of the difference depends on the charges included, the loan amount, the payment schedule and the term used in the APR calculation.

A larger difference between rate and APR can be a signal to review the finance charges carefully. It is not proof that an offer is bad: paying upfront points, for example, may be deliberate if the lower rate suits your expected holding period. Compare the actual dollar costs and loan terms rather than aiming for the smallest gap.

What APR leaves out

APR is not the same as total cash to close or the full cost of homeownership. Some costs are not part of the APR finance charge, and future expenses may change. Depending on the transaction and the rules, items such as certain title and appraisal charges, taxes, prepaid insurance, escrow deposits, homeowners association dues, maintenance and repairs may not be represented in the APR.

Review the itemized Loan Estimate for the costs you will actually pay. In particular, distinguish lender-controlled charges from taxes, insurance and other costs that may be determined by third parties or local authorities.

Where to find the rate and APR on a Loan Estimate

For a typical covered mortgage transaction using the CFPB Loan Estimate form:

  • Page 1 — Loan Terms: find the Interest Rate and check whether the rate is locked or floating. Also review the loan amount, term and whether the principal-and-interest payment can increase.
  • Page 2 — Loan Costs and Other Costs: inspect origination charges, services, lender credits, taxes, prepaids, initial escrow and total closing costs. Check which fees vary between lenders.
  • Page 3 — Comparisons: find the APR, the "In 5 years" comparison and the Total Interest Percentage (TIP).

The CFPB explains that the "In 5 years" line shows the total amount you will have paid in principal, interest, mortgage insurance and loan costs over five years, alongside the principal paid off. Subtract the principal-paid figure from the total-paid figure to estimate the five-year interest and fee cost using that disclosure measure.

TIP is different from APR: it expresses the total interest paid over the scheduled loan term as a percentage of the loan amount. Neither APR nor TIP replaces a review of the actual payment, fees and loan features.

How to compare two mortgage offers fairly

Comparisons are most useful when the offers describe the same transaction and are issued close together. Mortgage rates can change daily. Before comparing, check that the loan amount, loan term, product type, down payment, occupancy, rate-lock status and points or lender credits are aligned.

  • Compare the interest rate and whether each rate is locked, including the lock expiration.
  • Compare points, lender credits and total lender origination charges. A lower rate may require more upfront points.
  • Compare the monthly principal-and-interest payment and the total projected monthly payment, including mortgage insurance and escrow.
  • Review total closing costs and cash to close, not just the APR.
  • Use the Loan Estimate's five-year comparison to evaluate a common holding period, while also considering how long you actually expect to keep the loan.
  • Ask each lender to provide a revised Loan Estimate using the same points or lender-credit structure if the offers are not directly comparable.

The CFPB recommends focusing on charges within the lender's control, such as origination charges, certain services and lender credits. Differences in taxes, insurance, prepaid items and initial escrow may reflect estimates or local costs rather than a better lender offer.

Example: paying points for a lower rate

Imagine two hypothetical offers for the same fixed-rate loan amount and term. Offer A has a lower interest rate but requires discount points at closing. Offer B has a slightly higher interest rate but requires fewer upfront points.

Offer A may have a lower monthly principal-and-interest payment but higher upfront costs and a higher APR relative to its stated rate. Offer B may require less cash upfront but have a higher payment over time. The better choice depends on the actual dollar difference, how long you expect to keep the mortgage, and whether you can comfortably pay the upfront points.

Do not assume that the lower APR automatically wins or that paying points always pays off. Ask the lender for options with different points and lender credits and compare the dollar costs over a realistic holding period.

Important caution for adjustable-rate mortgages

Be especially careful when comparing APRs for adjustable-rate mortgages (ARMs). The disclosed APR is calculated using prescribed assumptions and does not show the maximum interest rate the loan could reach. A fixed-rate mortgage APR and an ARM APR may not be directly comparable. For an ARM, review the index, margin, initial rate period, adjustment frequency, rate caps, floor and maximum possible payment.

Also do not treat a closed-end mortgage APR as directly comparable to a home equity line of credit (HELOC) APR. The CFPB notes that the APR for a closed-end loan includes fees that are not included in the same way for a HELOC. Compare each product's full pricing and repayment structure.

Questions to ask each lender

  • Is the quoted rate locked? What is the expiration date and time?
  • What points, origination charges and lender credits are included?
  • Which charges are included in the APR, and which closing costs are not?
  • Can you provide the same loan with zero points and with lender credits so I can compare?
  • What are the principal-and-interest payment and the full projected monthly payment?
  • What does the Loan Estimate show for total closing costs, cash to close and the five-year cost comparison?
  • For an ARM, what are the rate caps and maximum possible payment?

Frequently asked questions

Is APR the same as the mortgage interest rate?

No. The interest rate is the contractual rate used to calculate loan interest. APR is a broader annualized measure that includes the interest rate and certain finance charges under applicable disclosure rules.

Is APR always higher than the interest rate?

It is usually higher when the loan has finance charges included in the APR calculation. The exact relationship depends on the transaction and its charges. Review the Loan Estimate rather than assuming a fixed difference.

Does my monthly mortgage payment use APR?

No. For a standard amortizing mortgage, the scheduled principal-and-interest payment is calculated using the contractual interest rate, loan amount and term. APR is a disclosure measure, not a replacement input for the payment formula.

Does APR include all closing costs?

No. APR includes certain finance charges, but federal rules exclude some bona fide and reasonable real-estate-related fees and other items. Review the itemized Loan Estimate and Closing Disclosure for the full costs of the transaction.

Is a lower APR always the better mortgage?

No. Compare similar loans and review upfront points, lender credits, cash to close, payment, loan features and the period you expect to keep the loan. APR is useful, but it is not a complete decision rule.

Where is the interest rate on the Loan Estimate?

On page 1 in the Loan Terms section. Check the Rate Lock line too, because a floating rate can change before it is locked.

Where is APR on the Loan Estimate?

On page 3 in the Comparisons section. The same section includes the "In 5 years" measure and Total Interest Percentage (TIP).

Why should I be careful comparing ARM APRs?

An ARM's APR does not show the maximum interest rate the loan could reach. Review the adjustment rules, index, margin, caps, floor and payment changes rather than relying on APR alone.

Can two lenders show different APRs for the same interest rate?

Yes. If the finance charges and other relevant loan terms differ, the APR can differ even when the stated interest rate is the same. Compare itemized fees and lender credits as well as the headline numbers.
