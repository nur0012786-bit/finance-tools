Credit Card APR Explained

Understand purchase, balance-transfer and cash-advance APRs, variable rates, grace periods and how daily interest affects the cost of a carried balance.

Educational finance guidance

APR is an annualized percentage rate disclosed for credit-card financing. A card can have different APRs for different transaction types or circumstances.

What does APR mean on a credit card?

APR stands for annual percentage rate. On a credit card, it is the annualized rate used to calculate interest on balances that are subject to interest under your card agreement. It helps you compare borrowing costs, but it is not a fee charged once a year and it does not mean you pay that percentage every month.

Your card may show more than one APR because purchases, balance transfers and cash advances can be treated as separate balance categories. The interest you actually owe depends on the APR for each category, the amount and timing of transactions, payments, the billing-cycle length, any grace period and the issuer's calculation method.

APR is only one part of a card's cost. Annual fees, balance-transfer fees, cash-advance fees, late fees and other charges may apply separately. Review the pricing table, account agreement and statement rather than comparing cards by APR alone. See the CFPB's credit card key terms.

Common types of credit card APR

Purchase APR

The purchase APR applies to eligible purchases when those balances become subject to interest. If your card offers a purchase grace period and you meet its conditions, you may avoid interest on eligible new purchases by paying the required balance in full by the due date. Carrying a balance can affect whether the grace period applies to new purchases.

Balance-transfer APR

A balance transfer moves debt from one account to another. The transferred balance may receive a promotional or standard balance-transfer APR, which can be different from the purchase APR. The issuer may also charge a transfer fee, commonly calculated as a percentage of the amount transferred or a stated minimum—so a 0% promotional APR does not necessarily make the transfer free.

Before transferring a balance, check the promotional expiration date, the APR that applies afterward, the transfer fee, whether new purchases receive a grace period, and whether the transfer can be completed before the offer expires. Do not assume a promotional rate applies to purchases or cash advances.

Cash-advance APR

Cash advances can carry a separate APR and a transaction fee. They often begin accruing interest immediately, without the purchase grace period. A cash advance may therefore cost more than an ordinary purchase even when the same card is used. Check the cash-advance rate, fee and interest-start date before using this feature.

Introductory or promotional APR

An introductory APR is a lower rate offered for a disclosed period or for specified transaction categories. The offer should state which transactions qualify, how long the rate lasts and the rate that applies after it ends. A promotional balance does not automatically make later purchases interest-free. Track the end date and repayment amount rather than assuming the promotional rate will continue.

Do not confuse a 0% introductory APR with deferred interest. With a true 0% APR offer, interest generally is not added retroactively for the promotional period; any remaining eligible balance begins accruing interest at the post-promotion rate after the offer ends. With a deferred-interest offer, often worded “no interest if paid in full,” failing to pay the qualifying promotional balance in full by the deadline can trigger interest calculated back to the original transaction date. Read the exact offer language and payment conditions before relying on either type of promotion. The CFPB explains the difference between zero-interest and deferred-interest offers.

Penalty APR

Some agreements provide a higher penalty APR after certain events, such as a payment being more than 60 days late. Whether it applies, which balances it affects and how it can be removed depend on the agreement and federal protections. Contact the issuer if a penalty rate appears on your statement and ask which event triggered it and what conditions apply to restoring a lower rate.

Fixed APR vs. variable APR

A fixed or non-variable APR is not tied to a public index in the same way as a variable APR, but “fixed” does not mean the rate can never change. An issuer may change rates in circumstances permitted by the agreement and applicable law, subject to notice and other protections.

A variable APR commonly consists of a public index—often the U.S. Prime Rate—plus a margin set out in the agreement. For example, if an agreement states an index plus a 15 percentage-point margin, a change in the index can change the APR even when the margin stays the same. Your agreement should identify the index, margin and how often the rate is updated.

Illustration: if the stated index is 8.50% and the margin is 15 percentage points, the resulting APR is 23.50%. If that index later rises by 0.25 percentage points and the agreement's adjustment schedule applies the change, the APR would become 23.75%. These figures are hypothetical; use the actual index, margin, measurement date and adjustment frequency in your agreement. Under Regulation Z, an increase under the variable-rate exception must be due to an increase in a publicly available index outside the issuer's control. See Regulation Z § 1026.55.

Federal rules generally restrict increases on existing balances, with exceptions that include an increase in a qualifying variable-rate index, expiration of a properly disclosed promotional rate, and certain serious payment delinquencies. For many increases on new transactions, advance notice is generally required. The details matter: see the CFPB's explanation of when a card issuer can increase an interest rate and the rule in Regulation Z, § 1026.55.

How APR becomes an interest charge

Many card issuers calculate interest daily using a daily periodic rate and an average daily balance. A common simplified approach is:

  • Daily periodic rate: APR divided by 365, or another divisor specified by the issuer's method.
  • Average daily balance: Add the daily balances for the billing cycle and divide by the number of days in that cycle.
  • Estimated interest: Average daily balance × daily periodic rate × number of days in the billing cycle.

This is an illustration, not a substitute for the agreement. Issuers can use different permitted calculation methods, and daily balances change when purchases, payments, credits, fees or interest post. Your statement shows the interest actually charged for the billing period. The CFPB explains the daily interest and average daily balance method.

Worked example: same balance, different APR

Assume a constant average daily balance of $5,000 for a 30-day billing cycle, with no grace period, new transactions, payments or fees during the cycle. Using a simplified APR ÷ 365 daily rate:

  • At 24% APR: $5,000 × (24% ÷ 365) × 30 ≈ $98.63 in interest.
  • At 18% APR: $5,000 × (18% ÷ 365) × 30 ≈ $73.97 in interest.
  • Illustrative difference: About $24.66 for that billing cycle.

This example isolates the rate effect. A real statement can differ because the balance changes during the cycle, the billing period may have a different number of days, or the issuer uses a different calculation method. The APR is not applied to a balance that is fully covered by a valid grace period.

What is a credit card grace period?

A grace period is the time between the end of a billing cycle and the payment due date during which eligible purchases may avoid interest. Federal law does not require every card to offer a grace period. Many cards do offer one for purchases, but the agreement controls its length and conditions.

Typically, you need to pay the statement balance in full by the due date to preserve the purchase grace period. Paying only the minimum usually leaves part of the statement balance unpaid and may cause interest to accrue. If you have been carrying a balance, new purchases may begin accruing interest from their transaction dates, depending on the agreement. Cash advances commonly do not receive a purchase grace period.

Read your statement and agreement to understand how to regain a grace period after carrying a balance. The CFPB explains the relationship between grace periods, interest rates and daily periodic rates.

Example: evaluating a balance-transfer offer

Suppose you transfer a $4,000 balance to a card offering 0% introductory APR for 15 months, with a 3% balance-transfer fee. If the fee is charged on the transferred amount:

  • Transfer fee: $4,000 × 3% = $120.
  • Starting transferred balance, before any other charges: $4,120 if the fee is added to the account balance.
  • Amount needed to clear $4,120 over 15 months: about $274.67 per month, assuming no other charges and payments are made on schedule.

This is a planning example, not a statement that every issuer adds the fee to the transferred balance or that every offer uses these terms. Confirm the fee, transfer limit, promotional start date, qualifying balance, minimum-payment requirements and post-promotion APR. If you cannot clear the balance before the offer ends, estimate the interest that could accrue afterward. A 0% APR offer can still include a transfer fee. The CFPB confirms that a fee may apply even with a zero-percent offer.

Also check whether purchases made on the new card receive a grace period while you carry the transferred balance. Do not assume they do.

How to read APR information on your statement

Review the interest-charge calculation table on each statement. It generally separates balance categories, shows the APR for each category, identifies the balance subject to that rate and reports the interest charged. Compare the statement with the rates in your account agreement and any promotional offer you accepted.

  • Check whether the rate is introductory, promotional, variable or a penalty rate.
  • Identify which balances are purchases, transfers, cash advances or other categories.
  • Look for the index and margin if the rate is variable, and review notices about changes.
  • Check transfer and cash-advance fees separately from APR.
  • Confirm the promotional end date and the post-promotion APR.
  • Check whether a grace period applies and what balance must be paid by the due date.

If you see a rate you do not recognize, contact the issuer using the number on your card or statement. Ask for the applicable agreement provision, effective date and balance category. Keep the response with your records.

Ways to reduce credit card interest

  • Pay the statement balance in full when possible. This can avoid interest on eligible purchases when your account has a grace period and you meet its conditions.
  • Pay more than the minimum. A larger payment can reduce the balance on which interest is charged and shorten repayment time.
  • Prioritize higher-rate debt. When you pay more than the minimum, federal rules generally direct the excess first to the balance with the highest APR, with special rules for certain deferred-interest balances.
  • Limit cash advances. Their rates, fees and lack of a grace period can make them expensive.
  • Compare balance-transfer offers carefully. Include the transfer fee and post-promotion APR in the comparison, and plan to repay the balance before the promotional period ends where possible.
  • Ask the issuer about a lower rate. You can request a rate review or ask about hardship options. A reduction is not guaranteed, so confirm any new rate and effective date in writing.

Frequently asked questions

Is a 0% APR credit card completely free?

Not necessarily. A 0% APR offer may apply only to eligible transactions for a limited period. Annual fees, transfer fees, late fees or other charges may still apply. Check the promotional terms and the rate that applies after the offer expires.

Is APR the same as the interest rate?

APR is the annualized rate used to disclose and compare credit costs. On a credit card, it is the annual rate used to calculate interest on balances subject to interest, while separate account fees may also add to the total cost. APR is not the amount of interest charged in one billing cycle.

Why does my card have several APRs?

Issuers can assign different rates to purchases, balance transfers, cash advances and promotional or penalty balances. Your statement should identify the rate and balance for each category.

Does a variable APR change every month?

It can change when the index specified in the agreement changes, but the agreement determines the rate-adjustment schedule. Check your card's index, margin and rate-change disclosures.

Can a credit card issuer raise the APR on my existing balance?

Federal protections generally restrict increases on existing balances, but exceptions apply, including certain variable-rate changes, disclosed promotional-rate expirations and serious payment delinquency. The rules depend on the circumstances and balance category. Review the issuer's notice and Regulation Z.

Will paying the minimum avoid interest?

Usually not. The minimum payment keeps the account from becoming past due when it is paid on time, but it normally leaves part of the balance unpaid. Interest can continue to accrue on that remaining balance. A valid purchase grace period generally requires paying the statement balance in full.

Does a balance transfer always save money?

No. A transfer can reduce interest if the promotional APR and repayment plan outweigh the transfer fee and any later interest. Compare the fee, promotional period, post-promotion APR and your ability to repay before applying.

Where can I check my card's actual APR?

Review the card's pricing information, account agreement and latest statement. The statement generally lists each balance category, its APR and the related interest charge. Contact the issuer if a rate or fee is unclear.
