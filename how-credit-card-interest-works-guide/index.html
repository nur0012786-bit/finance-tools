How Credit Card Interest Works

Understand daily credit card interest, average daily balances, APR, grace periods, payment timing and how different balance categories are treated.

Educational finance guidance

Credit card interest depends on the card's agreement and billing practices. Unlike a typical fixed installment loan, a revolving balance can change as purchases, payments and credits occur.

What credit card interest is

Credit card interest is the cost of carrying a balance when your account terms require interest on that balance. Your card's annual percentage rate (APR) is an annualized rate, but many issuers calculate the interest charge day by day during each billing cycle. The amount on your statement therefore depends on more than the APR alone: daily balances, transaction dates, payments, credits, the number of days in the billing cycle and the issuer's calculation method can all matter.

A credit card is revolving credit. Unlike a typical installment loan with a scheduled payoff amount, the balance can rise when you make purchases or fees post and fall when payments or credits are applied. Separate balance categories may also carry separate APRs. The CFPB explains that many issuers calculate interest daily using an average daily balance and that the statement identifies balances subject to different APRs. Read the CFPB explanation of credit card interest.

APR and the daily periodic rate

The daily periodic rate (DPR) is the daily rate an issuer uses when applying a daily-interest calculation. The CFPB notes that an issuer may calculate it by dividing the APR by 360 or 365, depending on the card's terms. Do not assume a divisor without checking your agreement.

For illustration, if a card has a 24% APR and uses 365 as the divisor:

  • APR as a decimal: 0.24
  • Daily periodic rate: 0.24 ÷ 365 = 0.0006575, or about 0.06575% per day
  • That daily rate is applied to the relevant daily balance or average daily balance, depending on the issuer's method.

The daily rate is not the same as charging 24% every month. APR is an annualized rate; the daily periodic rate converts it to the rate used for a day. The card agreement should explain the applicable rate and method. See the CFPB's explanation of a daily periodic rate.

How the daily balance method works

Under a daily balance method, the issuer determines a balance for each day of the billing cycle. That day's balance can reflect the prior balance, new transactions, payments, credits and fees, based on the rules in the agreement. The issuer applies the daily periodic rate to the daily balance for each day and totals the interest charges for the cycle.

Some daily-balance methods include the previous day's interest in the next day's balance, which produces daily compounding. Other methods do not add unpaid interest to the daily balance in the same way. The issuer's agreement or contract definitions should state which method applies. The CFPB's credit card contract definitions describe daily-balance methods with and without compounding.

How the average daily balance method works

Some issuers use an average daily balance (ADB) method. In a simplified version, the issuer:

  1. Determines the balance for each day in the billing cycle.
  2. Adds the daily balances together.
  3. Divides that total by the number of days in the billing cycle to calculate the average daily balance.
  4. Applies the applicable daily periodic rate to the average daily balance and the number of days in the cycle, according to the agreement's method.

Payments and credits can reduce the daily balance from the date they are credited, while purchases and fees can increase it from the dates specified in the agreement. This is why the timing of activity matters. A payment credited earlier in the cycle may reduce more daily balances than the same payment credited later, assuming the same amount and other conditions.

Not every issuer describes its method in exactly the same way. The contract may calculate interest separately for each balance category, and the account's compounding rules can affect the result. Use the method stated in your agreement rather than treating the simplified sequence above as a universal formula.

Worked example: a constant balance for 30 days

Suppose a card has a $3,000 balance that stays unchanged for a 30-day billing cycle, a 24% APR, and a daily periodic rate calculated as APR ÷ 365. For a simplified average-daily-balance calculation without daily compounding:

  • Average daily balance: $3,000
  • Daily periodic rate: 0.24 ÷ 365
  • Estimated interest: $3,000 × (0.24 ÷ 365) × 30 = $59.18

This example assumes the same $3,000 balance every day, one APR, no grace period, no new transactions, no payments, no fees and no daily compounding. It illustrates the arithmetic only. If the balance changes during the cycle, the issuer uses a different divisor, or the contract includes daily compounding or another method, the actual charge will differ. Your statement—not this illustration—is the source for the amount actually billed.

Why the timing of purchases and payments matters

When interest accrues daily, the balance can change on different days within the same billing cycle. A purchase can increase the balance from the day it is included under the issuer's terms. A payment or credit can reduce the balance once it is credited. As a result, two people with the same APR and the same end-of-cycle balance may still have different interest charges if their transaction and payment timing differs.

Paying earlier can reduce interest when a balance is accruing interest and the payment is credited earlier, because it can lower the balance used in subsequent daily calculations. This does not mean a payment is always applied immediately when you submit it; check the issuer's cut-off times, accepted payment methods and crediting policies. Keep confirmation of payments and check the next statement.

What a credit card grace period does

A grace period is the time between the end of a billing cycle and the payment due date. Credit card companies are not required to offer a grace period, but many offer one for eligible purchases. When your card has a purchase grace period and you meet its conditions—often by paying the statement balance in full by the due date—you can generally avoid interest on those eligible purchases.

If you carry a balance instead of paying the required amount in full, you may lose the purchase grace period. Depending on the agreement, interest can then apply to new purchases from their transaction dates. Cash advances and certain other transactions commonly do not receive the purchase grace period and may begin accruing interest on the transaction date. Check your agreement for the transaction categories and conditions that qualify.

The CFPB explains that a grace period is not mandatory, usually applies to purchases when offered, and that cash advances generally start accruing interest from the transaction date. It also explains that card issuers must provide billing statements at least 21 days before the payment due date. Read the CFPB grace-period explanation.

Different balances can have different interest rates

A card account may contain a purchase balance, a balance-transfer balance, a cash-advance balance or a promotional balance, each with a different APR or rate period. Your statement should identify the balance categories, the APR that applies to each one and the amount subject to that rate. A promotional APR may expire, and a penalty APR may apply in circumstances described in the agreement and permitted by law.

Do not multiply your entire card balance by one APR if the account has multiple categories with different rates. Review the interest-charge calculation table on the statement and the pricing terms in your agreement. The Credit Card APR Guide explains common APR categories and promotional rates.

How payments are applied to different APR balances

When you pay more than the required minimum, federal Regulation Z generally requires the amount above the minimum to be applied first to the balance with the highest APR, then to other balances in descending APR order. The issuer generally has discretion over how the required minimum-payment portion is allocated, subject to applicable rules.

There is a special rule for certain deferred-interest balances. During the two billing cycles immediately before the deferred-interest period expires, the excess payment generally must be applied first to that deferred-interest balance, with the remaining excess allocated under the usual ordering, unless the issuer uses a permitted consumer-request method. A deferred-interest offer is not necessarily the same as a straightforward 0% APR promotion.

See the current CFPB text of Regulation Z, § 1026.53 (Allocation of payments). The specific treatment of your account should also be checked in your agreement and statements.

How to check an interest charge on your statement

  • Identify the balance category and APR used for each interest calculation.
  • Check the billing-cycle start and end dates and the number of days in the cycle.
  • Find the daily periodic rate or other method described in the agreement.
  • Review payments, credits, purchases, fees and their posting dates.
  • Check whether the card offered a grace period and whether you met its full-payment conditions.
  • Compare the calculation with the issuer's agreement and contact the issuer if the statement is unclear.

Your statement may show an interest-charge calculation table that separates balance categories and their rates. Keep statements and payment confirmations so you can compare changes from one cycle to the next. If you believe a charge is incorrect, follow the issuer's billing-error instructions and applicable deadlines rather than relying only on a phone conversation.

Ways to reduce interest costs

  • Pay the statement balance in full when possible. This can preserve a purchase grace period when your card offers one and you meet its terms.
  • Pay earlier when a balance is accruing interest. Once the payment is credited, it can reduce the daily balance used for later calculations.
  • Pay more than the minimum. A larger payment can reduce the balance more quickly and limit future interest.
  • Prioritize high-rate balances. Understand the issuer's payment-allocation rules, especially when one account has multiple APR categories.
  • Limit new charges while repaying. New purchases can increase the balance and extend repayment.
  • Be cautious with cash advances. They may carry a separate APR and fee and often start accruing interest immediately.
  • Review promotional deadlines. Know the post-promotional APR and the exact terms of any deferred-interest offer.

Frequently asked questions

Is credit card interest calculated monthly or daily?

Many issuers calculate interest daily, often using a daily periodic rate and daily or average daily balances. The method and divisor depend on the card agreement. A simplified APR-divided-by-12 calculation may not match a statement.

Is the daily periodic rate always APR divided by 365?

No. The CFPB says issuers may use 360 or 365, depending on the issuer and agreement. Check the stated calculation method for your card.

Does paying a credit card early reduce interest?

If a balance is accruing interest, a payment credited earlier can reduce the balance used in later daily calculations. The actual effect depends on the payment posting date and the issuer's calculation method.

Will paying the minimum avoid interest?

Usually not. The minimum payment generally keeps the account current when paid on time, but it does not normally pay the full statement balance. If a purchase grace period applies, you generally need to pay the required statement balance in full by the due date to avoid purchase interest.

Do cash advances have a grace period?

Cash advances generally do not receive the purchase grace period and commonly begin accruing interest on the transaction date. Confirm the APR and terms in your card agreement.

Why was I charged interest after making a payment?

A payment may not have covered the full amount required to preserve a grace period, may have been credited after the relevant deadline, or may have applied to a different balance category. Interest may also apply to transactions that do not qualify for a grace period. Review the statement and ask the issuer to explain the calculation.

Can my card have more than one APR?

Yes. Purchases, balance transfers, cash advances and promotional balances can have different APRs. The statement should show the APR and balance for each category.

Does the calculator show my exact credit card interest?

No. The linked calculator uses a simplified monthly-interest model for planning. Your issuer may calculate interest daily and apply different rates, grace-period terms, posting rules and fees.
