HOW IT WORKS

How the down payment calculator works

The calculator multiplies the home purchase price by your planned down payment percentage. The result is your down payment. Subtracting that amount from the purchase price gives the estimated base mortgage amount before any financed program fees or other loan adjustments.

  • Down payment: home price × planned down payment percentage.
  • Base mortgage amount: home price − down payment.
  • Cash needed before deposit: down payment + estimated closing costs.
  • Cash due at closing: cash needed before deposit − earnest money applied, floored at zero.
  • Cash surplus or shortfall: available cash today, excluding the deposit already paid, − cash due at closing.
  • Maximum down payment supported by your cash budget: available cash today + earnest money available for this purchase − closing costs, limited to the home price.

Earnest money is normally credited toward the buyer's funds due at closing; it is not an additional cost on top of the down payment and closing costs. The amount credited in the final settlement depends on the contract and closing statement.

For the cash-surplus calculation, enter cash you can still use at closing after setting aside your emergency cushion, moving costs, renovation funds and other savings goals. Do not include earnest money already transferred in that number; enter it separately so it is not counted twice.

This calculator does not estimate property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, HOA dues, prepaid costs or loan-program fees separately. Include relevant estimates in the closing-cost input or use your lender's Loan Estimate. Actual cash to close can also reflect seller credits, lender credits and transaction adjustments.

US HOME-BUYING GUIDANCE

Down payment is only part of your upfront budget

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau recommends subtracting funds for other savings goals, moving costs, renovations, furnishings and an emergency cushion before deciding how much is actually available for the down payment and closing. It also explains that closing costs are separate from the down payment and commonly fall within a broad 2%–5% planning range, depending on the transaction.

Down payment requirements vary by loan program and lender. The CFPB describes options that can start around 3% for some conventional loans and 3.5% for FHA loans, while special programs may offer other terms. A low down payment can make homeownership accessible, but may increase financing costs or trigger mortgage insurance. Mortgage-insurance rules differ by loan program: CFPB: How mortgage insurance works. Check actual eligibility and quoted terms.

EXAMPLE

Example down payment comparison

On a $400,000 home, a 10% down payment is $40,000, leaving an estimated $360,000 mortgage. At 20%, the down payment is $80,000 and the estimated mortgage is $320,000. If closing costs were $12,000, the corresponding cash needed before deposits would be $52,000 and $92,000.

Assume the buyer has $100,000 still available for closing, excluding $5,000 of earnest money already paid. With $80,000 down and $12,000 in closing costs, cash due at closing is $87,000 ($80,000 + $12,000 − $5,000). The buyer would have an estimated $13,000 surplus from the $100,000 still available. This is a planning example; actual cash to close can reflect credits, prepaids and settlement adjustments.

FAQ

Down payment calculator FAQs

Do I need 20% down?

No. Down payment requirements vary by loan program and lender. Some options allow substantially less than 20% down.

Does a bigger down payment lower the loan amount?

Yes. For a given purchase price, every additional dollar paid upfront generally reduces the amount that must be financed.

Does a down payment include closing costs?

No. Closing costs are separate. Your total cash to close can include the down payment and closing costs, less deposits and credits that apply.

Could a smaller down payment increase monthly costs?

It can. A smaller down payment means a larger mortgage, and mortgage insurance may apply depending on the loan type and lender. Conventional loans with less than 20% down commonly require private mortgage insurance (PMI); FHA and USDA loans have separate mortgage-insurance or guarantee-fee rules. Check the actual quote for your loan program.

Should I use all my savings for the down payment?

No. The CFPB suggests setting aside money for other savings goals, moving costs, furnishings, renovations and an emergency cushion. A larger down payment reduces the amount borrowed, but it also leaves less cash available for unexpected expenses.

Does earnest money count toward the down payment?

Earnest money is generally credited toward the funds due at closing under the purchase contract. It can be applied toward the down payment and/or closing costs in the final settlement. Enter the deposit separately so the calculator does not count it as a new cost.

Is this calculator a loan-qualification tool?

No. It estimates down payment and cash requirements from the numbers you enter. Lenders use underwriting criteria to determine eligibility, required funds, rates and loan terms.

Does 20% down guarantee the best mortgage deal?

No. A larger down payment can reduce borrowing and may avoid conventional PMI, but the best choice depends on rates, fees, mortgage-insurance pricing, loan program and the value of keeping cash available. Compare complete Loan Estimates from lenders.

Educational estimate: This calculator does not determine loan eligibility or recommend a down payment. Loan programs, lender requirements, mortgage insurance, closing costs and cash-to-close figures can vary.
