How to Pay Off a Mortgage Early

Learn common ways borrowers may accelerate mortgage payoff, how extra principal can affect interest, and what to check with a mortgage servicer.

PLAN AN EARLIER PAYOFF

What does it mean to pay off a mortgage early?

Paying off a mortgage early means reducing the unpaid principal faster than the original repayment schedule requires. You can do this through regular additional principal payments, occasional lump-sum payments or a combination of both. When extra money is applied to principal, the balance on which future interest is calculated is lower.

Paying off a mortgage early can reduce future interest and free up monthly cash flow once the loan is fully repaid. But it also moves money into home equity, which is less liquid than cash in a bank account. The right approach depends on your savings, other debts, expected expenses, loan terms and financial priorities—not just the interest saved.

How extra principal payments reduce interest

For a typical amortizing mortgage, interest for each payment period is calculated using the outstanding principal balance and the applicable interest rate. An extra payment that is correctly applied to principal lowers that balance sooner. Future interest is then calculated on a smaller amount, allowing more of later scheduled payments to reduce principal.

The earlier an extra principal payment is made, the longer the reduced balance can affect later interest calculations. However, the exact result depends on the amount, timing, rate, payment schedule, loan terms and how the servicer applies the funds.

Common ways to pay a mortgage off faster

  • Add a fixed amount each month: Pay the regular scheduled amount plus an additional principal amount, such as $50, $100 or another affordable figure.
  • Make one extra payment each year: You can make an additional principal payment when your budget allows. Confirm how the servicer will apply it.
  • Use occasional lump sums: A work bonus, tax refund or other available cash can reduce principal, provided you do not need that money for near-term expenses or reserves.
  • Choose a shorter term when taking a new mortgage: A 15-year term may pay down principal faster than a 30-year term, but the required monthly payment is usually higher. Check that the payment remains affordable.
  • Increase payments gradually: You can start with a small amount and review your budget before increasing it. A sustainable amount is better than a payment plan that causes missed bills or new high-interest debt.

Worked example: adding $100 per month

Consider a hypothetical $300,000 fixed-rate mortgage at 6.5% interest with a 30-year term and standard monthly amortization. The scheduled principal-and-interest payment is approximately $1,896.20 per month.

Regular scheduled payments only$1,896.2030 years$382,633
$100 extra principal each month$1,996.20About 26 years$321,639

Under this simplified monthly model, paying an additional $100 toward principal each month shortens the modeled repayment period by about 4 years and reduces scheduled interest by approximately $60,995.

This is an illustration, not a payoff quote or guarantee. It assumes the extra $100 is made every month from the start and applied directly to principal, the rate stays at 6.5%, there are no missed or late payments, no fees or prepayment penalties apply, and payments follow a standard monthly amortization method. Actual servicer calculations can differ due to payment timing, rounding, loan terms and servicing procedures. The interest comparison does not include taxes, insurance, escrow or the opportunity cost of using the extra cash.

Should you pay extra monthly or make a lump-sum payment?

Both approaches can reduce principal, but they fit different cash-flow situations. A regular extra payment creates a predictable habit and can reduce the balance every month. A lump sum can make a larger immediate reduction, but it may use savings you need for an emergency, a home repair or another planned expense.

Do not empty your cash reserves just to reduce the mortgage balance. Home equity is not the same as money available to pay an urgent bill. Consider keeping a suitable emergency reserve and funding near-term obligations before committing extra money to a mortgage.

Compare mortgage payoff with other financial priorities

Paying extra toward a mortgage is not automatically the best use of every spare dollar. Before accelerating payoff, compare it with:

  • High-interest debt: Credit-card or other expensive debt may cost more than your mortgage. Compare rates, fees and repayment plans.
  • Emergency savings: Cash reserves can help prevent borrowing when an unexpected expense occurs.
  • Retirement contributions: Consider your employer match and long-term retirement needs before diverting all spare cash to a mortgage.
  • Upcoming home costs: Repairs, maintenance, property taxes, insurance and other ownership expenses may require cash.
  • Other goals: Education, business plans, medical expenses and other time-sensitive obligations can affect how much money you can reasonably commit.

Mortgage interest may receive tax treatment that depends on your circumstances and current tax rules. Do not assume that every borrower receives a tax deduction or that a deduction makes interest-free borrowing beneficial. Ask a qualified tax professional about your situation.

Check for a prepayment penalty

A prepayment penalty is a fee that may apply if you pay some or all of the mortgage principal before it is due. Not all mortgages have one. The CFPB says these penalties typically apply when the full balance is paid off—such as through a sale or refinance—during a specified period, often three or five years, although some contracts may also impose a penalty for a large lump-sum payment.

Small extra principal payments do not normally trigger a prepayment penalty, but do not assume your loan has no restrictions. Review the promissory note and any addendum, check the prepayment-penalty disclosure and ask your servicer how the provision applies to the amount you plan to pay. If you are comparing new loans, ask for a similar option without a prepayment penalty and compare the total costs.

How to make sure extra money is applied to principal

Follow the servicer's written payment instructions. Depending on the online portal or payment method, you may need to select "additional principal," enter the extra amount in a separate field or include a clear instruction with a mailed payment. Do not assume that sending more money automatically applies it to principal in the way you intend.

  • Make the full scheduled payment due first, including any required escrow amount.
  • Use the servicer's designated extra-principal field or written instructions.
  • Do not label an extra payment as an advance installment unless that is what you intend.
  • Check the next mortgage statement and transaction history to confirm the amount was credited to principal.
  • Compare the new unpaid principal balance with the balance you expected after the extra payment.

If you send less than a full periodic payment, the servicer may hold the partial amount in a suspense or unapplied-funds account until enough money accumulates to cover a full payment. A partial payment is not the same as an extra principal payment. The CFPB says mortgage statements generally provide information about how payments were applied and whether partial payments are being held.

Should you use a biweekly mortgage payment program?

Some programs collect half of the monthly payment every two weeks. Because there are 26 two-week periods in a year, that schedule can result in 13 full monthly-payment equivalents annually. If the extra amount is applied to principal, it can accelerate repayment.

However, you do not need to pay a third party to create the equivalent of an extra annual payment. Ask your servicer whether it offers a no-fee biweekly option and when funds are credited. Some third-party plans charge enrollment or transaction fees, may hold money before forwarding it, or may not apply it as expected. You can compare the cost and timing with making a separate principal-only payment yourself.

What happens to your required monthly payment?

Making an extra principal payment usually reduces the outstanding balance and can shorten the payoff period. It does not normally reduce the required scheduled monthly payment automatically. You generally continue making the original required payment unless the loan is formally recast, modified or otherwise changed under the lender's rules.

A mortgage recast is a servicer-approved recalculation of the required payment after a qualifying lump-sum principal reduction. The interest rate and remaining maturity usually stay the same, but the payment is recalculated using the lower balance. Not every loan or servicer permits recasting, and a fee or minimum principal payment may apply. Ask the servicer for written eligibility requirements before sending a large lump sum.

A recast is different from refinancing. A refinance replaces the existing mortgage with a new loan, generally involving new underwriting, pricing and closing costs. A recast changes the payment schedule of the existing loan without replacing it.

How to pay the mortgage off in full

When you are ready to pay off the entire balance, do not simply send the principal amount shown on your latest statement. Request a written payoff statement from the servicer for a specific good-through date. The payoff amount can include principal, interest through the date funds are received and any applicable fees or adjustments.

The CFPB says that, for covered consumer mortgage transactions, a servicer generally must provide an accurate payoff statement within seven business days after receiving a written request, subject to specified exceptions. Confirm the payment method, deadline, wire instructions and any per-diem interest with the servicer. Independently verify wire instructions using a trusted phone number because payment-instruction fraud can lead to irreversible losses.

After the payoff funds clear, review the final statement and confirm the loan is shown as paid in full. Ask about any remaining escrow balance and the process for releasing the mortgage lien. Keep your payoff confirmation and closing records.

Practical checklist before making extra payments

  • Confirm your current principal balance, interest rate and remaining loan term.
  • Check the note and addenda for a prepayment penalty or other restrictions.
  • Keep enough cash for emergencies, planned expenses and home maintenance.
  • Compare the mortgage rate with other debts and financial priorities.
  • Ask your servicer how to designate additional principal and when it will be credited.
  • Make sure the regular payment is fully satisfied before sending a separate extra amount.
  • Review your next statement to verify that principal was reduced correctly.
  • Ask about recast eligibility and fees if your goal is to reduce the required payment after a lump sum.
  • Use an official payoff statement—not the statement balance—when paying the loan in full.

Frequently asked questions

Does paying extra principal reduce mortgage interest?

It can. When the extra payment is applied to principal, the unpaid balance falls sooner and future interest is generally calculated on a smaller balance. The amount saved depends on the payment amount, timing, interest rate and loan terms.

How much time can an extra $100 per month save?

It depends on the balance, interest rate, remaining term and when you begin. In the illustrative $300,000, 6.5%, 30-year model above, $100 extra each month shortens the estimated payoff by about four years. Your loan may produce a different result.

Can I make one extra mortgage payment every year?

Often, borrowers can make an additional payment, but check the loan and servicer instructions. Make clear that the extra amount is intended for principal, and verify how it is credited.

Will an extra payment lower my next required monthly payment?

Usually not. Extra principal generally reduces the balance and shortens the payoff period. A recast, if your loan and servicer permit it, can recalculate the required payment after a qualifying principal reduction.

Can I be charged for paying off my mortgage early?

Some mortgages have prepayment penalties, but many do not. The terms and applicable rules determine when a fee can apply. Review your loan documents and ask the servicer before a large lump-sum payment or full payoff.

Are small additional principal payments usually subject to a penalty?

The CFPB says prepayment penalties do not normally apply to small extra principal payments made in chunks, but borrowers should still confirm their own contract and ask the servicer.

Is a biweekly mortgage plan worth it?

It can accelerate payoff if the extra annual payment is actually credited to principal, but compare any program fee and payment timing. You may be able to achieve a similar result by making an extra principal payment directly through your servicer.

What is the difference between a recast and a refinance?

A recast recalculates the payment on the existing mortgage after a qualifying principal reduction, if allowed. A refinance replaces the existing mortgage with a new loan and usually involves new underwriting and closing costs.

How do I know the servicer applied my extra payment correctly?

Review the transaction history and the unpaid principal balance on the next statement. If the payment was not applied as instructed, contact the servicer using its designated address for payment or account errors and keep copies of your records.

How do I find the exact payoff amount?

Request a written payoff statement for a specific date. It may include accrued interest and other amounts not shown in the unpaid principal balance on your latest statement.
